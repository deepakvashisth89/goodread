Here are some of the things I learned from this book.Lojong teachings share the unusual message that we can use our difficulties and problems to awaken our hearts. Unconditional compassion for ourselves is what leads naturally to unconditional compassion for others. If we are willing to stand fully in our own shoes and never give up on ourselves, then we will be able to put ourselves in the shoes of others and never give up on them. True compassion does not come from wanting to help out those less fortunate than ourselves but from realizing our kinship with all beings.The Lojong teachings propose that we could relate compassionately with that which we prefer to push away, and we could learn to give away and share that which we hold most dear.As we dwell in sitting meditation and practice labeling thoughts, it is very healing to stop hiding from yourself. By knowing yourself, you’re coming to know humanness altogether. It is important to not be harsh with ourselves here, let our labeling of thoughts be compassionate and gentle and humorous. Compassion for others begins with kindness to ourselves.Lojong teachings encourage use to think of other people and wish for them to feel what we’re feeling when we are enjoying what we are experiencing. Be generous with your joy. Instead of fearing that our insights and delights are going to slip away and trying to hold onto them, share them.These practices help us to develop trust in our awakened heart, our Bodhichitta.Sitting here being gentle with ourselves, we’re rediscovering something. Like a mother reuniting with her child; having been lost to eachother for a long, long time, they reunite. The way to reunite with bodhichatta is to lighten up in your practice and in your whole life.She talks about the Three Objects, Three Poisons, and Three Seeds of Virtue. The three main poisons are passion, aggression, and ignorance. It is important to not repress any of these, but to recognize when they’re present and do embrace them. Tonglen practice helps us to breathe in the sources of pain, and to refrain from resisting them, and rather surrender. You surrender to yourself, you acknowledge who you are, you honor yourself. Similarly, for the things that bring us delight, connect with what about it is inspiring or relieving, and breathe it out to others.Tonglen practice has four stages. First is a flashing openness to give us a calm space. Second is working with the texture, to breathe in the dark heavy and hot while breathing out tue white light and cool. Third is working with relieving a specific, heartfelt instance of suffering. The fourth is extending that wish to help everyone. It is important to not get caught up in the storyline of each persons suffering, so when you project that relief to everyone, it is not specific to any one story, and resonates with any core human suffering.She also talks about how when the world is filled with evil, transform all mishaps into the path of enlightenment. She encourages you to use the unwanted and unfavorable circumstances in life as the actual material of awakening. Whatever occurs isn’t considered to be an interruption or an obstacle, but a way to wake up. This is a very powerful and succinct way to help us overcome the trap of dejection and stubbornness that usually comes with feeling defeat or overwhelming.She also identifies ego as a room of our own, with a view and temperature and smell and music just as we like. Trying to reside here all of the time will cause us to try to get life to always come out to suit you, and the more your fear of other people and what’s outside your room grows. Instead of becoming more relaxed, you pull down the blinds and lock the door. When you do get out, you’ll find the experience more unsettling and disagreeable. The more you fight to get it your way, the less you feel at home. To develop compassion for others, you have to unlock the door. As you befriend the feelings of the fear that somebody you might not like comes through the door, you can begin to open it. This is a great analysis of the feeling of trying to control, and offers a path around that, while still finding peace with our life’s conditions.She goes onto teach the slogan of “Drive all blames into one”. With this, we begin to imagine what it feels like to blame someone else. What’s actually under all that talking and conversation about how wrong somebody or something is? What does blame feel like in your stomach? When these really unresolved issues of our lives come up, we are no longer trying to escape but are beginning to be curious and open towards these parts of ourselves. Drive All Blames into One doesn’t mean to blame yourself instead of other people, it means to touch in with what blame feels like altogether. Instead of guarding yourself and pushing things away, begin to get in touch with the fact that there’s a very soft spot under all that armor, and blame id probably one of the most well-perfected armors that we have.As we dive into the slogan “Be grateful to everyone”, we make peace with the aspects of ourselves that we have rejected. The people who repel us unwittingly show us the aspects of ourselves that we find unacceptable, which otherwise we can’t see.On overcoming resistance, we have four practices that help us. First, accumulating merit. The way to accumulate merit is to be willing to give willing to open, willing to not hold back. It is described as letting go of holding onto yourself, letting go of your stronghold of ego go. Instead of collecting things for yourself you open and give them away. As a result of opening yourself, you begin to experience your world as more friendly. That’s merit. You find it easier to practice the dharma, you have fewer kleshas, and circumstances seem to be hospitable.Second, purifying our negative actions. This segment is about acknowledgement and gentle forgiveness. This is not a confession, rather it is a self reflection for us to honestly see what we do and develop a yearning to take off our dark colored glasses. This has a few stages. Regret, Refraining, Remedial Action, and Resolution. Regret comes from us being mindful and it becoming harder than ever to hide from ourselves. It is the realization that you’re tired of eating poison or the constant complaint to ourselves. Refraining comes from the observation it’s painful to see how in spite of everything we’ve come to regret, we continue our behavior. In our pursuit, this goes with “One day at a time”. Remedial action is where we do something about what we noticed from these first two items. It’s important here to take refuge in the Buddha, the Dharma, and the Sangha because they all are examples for us to look to of others facing their challenges as well and that we can do it too. Resolution is where we resolve not to repeat the behavior once we have corrected it.Third, feeding the ghosts. This is where we relate to our unreasonableness. Specifically the kind of unreasonableness that comes up out of nowhere like when we all the sudden are very sad, or very angry with a situation. You can make a relationship with the ghosts which are the negative aspects of ourselves, by physically them something physical like a cake. There is an incantation that says “Not only do I not want you to go away, you can come back any time you like. And here, have some cake”. We invite them back because they show us when we have lost our mindfulness.Fourth, offering to the protectors. We look to the protectors that help us preserve our mindfulness. They serve as a big “No”. This is like a bell that returns us to mindfulness when we stray off. We offer to the protectors and they help remind us to return, like when we storm off angry and stub our toe, they are a subtle reminder to return to ourselves.These practices help us to overcome resistance, the best path for transforming bad circumstance into the way of enlightenment.Peña talks about the big squeeze. This represents a state where we find that our ideals and the reality of what’s really happening don’t match. This serves as an inflection point in our life’s, where we can use all of the teachings and practices that we’ve learned and begin to slowly but surely begin acting them out in our lives, no matter how far from our path the situations throw us.Overall, I enjoyed these teachings on compassion from Pema Chodron. Many of the lessons taught in this book urge me to get deeper into meditation practice. This book allowed me to notice that I am the colorblind doctor who is a technical expert on colors. I have been accumulating so much knowledge of what it means to meditate and how to best shape its experience, but I have been so far removed from the experience itself up to this point. Very soon I will attempt to get into practice. I have been so clingy to holding onto my thoughts that it has been almost a threat to go into a space where I can be so tender to them and let them go. I had a harder time following the book because so many of the lessons were related to really sitting down with ourself and lowering our defenses. This calls for a very vulnerable state which is a lot to overcome. I’d say that I’ll get there eventually, but as the book title suggests, I really can start where I am. I think if I come back to this book a little while later on my path, I can extract a lot more out of it than I was able to this time around.
