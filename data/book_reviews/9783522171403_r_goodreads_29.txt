I WISH I HAD READ THIS AS A CHILD. 🐀🖤🗡️ A beloved children’s classic that tells the story of a peaceful abbey threatened by a ruthless rat warlord. At the heart of the tale is Matthias, a young mouse who embarks on a courageous quest to protect his home and uncover his own strength. With its charming characters, vivid descriptions, and a timeless sense of adventure, Redwall captures the joy of a classic hero’s journey while celebrating friendship, bravery, and hope. It’s filled with charming characters, rich descriptions of food that feel nearly tangible, and an epic story of heroism and war.

This book came highly recommended by my husband and a dear friend, both of whom adored it as children. I approached it knowing this book would likely have been something I treasured if I had read it at a younger age. Even with the awareness that I wasn’t the intended audience, I still found myself thoroughly enjoying it. It’s fun and immersive, with plenty of adventure, lovable characters, and a surprisingly honest depiction of the realities of war. If I had read this as a child, I truly believe it would have become a defining part of my identity. 😂

I LOVED so many of the characters.The main character’s growth from beginning to end is compelling, and I found myself thoroughly entertained throughout. Knowing there are twenty-two books in this series that continue to explore the stories of different characters in this tale is astounding. I don’t know if I’ll read all of them, but I’m certainly interested in revisiting this world.

While the book doesn’t introduce particularly new themes for medieval-style tales, the choice to use animals as the central characters and to exclude humans entirely felt refreshing and imaginative for it's time. I’ve seen critiques about the world-building, such as questions about whether humans exist or who built the structures the animals inhabit. To me, these critiques miss the mark. This story isn’t trying to be a complete, immersive world in the way adult fantasies often are. It’s a children’s book, and I think readers who found the world lacking may not be the audience this story was meant for.

I read through quite a few reviews of this book and often found myself wanting to defend it. Many critiques seemed to take issue with how adolescent the story feels, but then I came across a comment on a one-star review that shed light on why Brian Jacques wrotein the first place. It led me to research more about the book’s origins, and I found something truly special worth sharing:

Brian Jacques wrote this series with a very specific audience in mind: the children at the Royal Wavertree School for the Blind in Liverpool. As a milkman, Jacques delivered to the school and wanted to create a story filled with vivid descriptions that would allow visually impaired children to experience the world he imagined as clearly as possible. This intention is felt throughout the book, from the mouthwatering descriptions of feasts to the lush details of Mossflower Woods. Jacques drew on the timeless storytelling of his youth, crafting a tale full of courage, decency, and heart. Knowing this adds an extra layer of warmth to the tale, as it reminds readers that its whimsical charm and immersive detail were created to spark joy and imagination for children who did not have stories like this available to them. ♥️

I don’t feel like I’m adding anything new to the conversation about this tale, as it’s already considered a treasured classic. But if you’re someone who loves fantasy, stories of heroes and villains, and tales where different animals shine through their unique strengths (whether mice, owls, badgers, sparrows, shrews, a hilarious hare or a sly fox) this book is simply a joy to read. I’m so glad I finally picked it up, and I hope the younger people in my life will read it someday so we can share in the excitement together. I can’t help but feel that anyone who missed out on as a kid (such as myself) was robbed of a truly magical adventure. For anyone who loves fantasy and has children in their lives, this book is absolutely worth revisiting, not just for the chance to share its fun, engaging, and child-friendly world, but also to explore its deeper themes, like the realities of war, loss, death, and the complexities of courage in the face of violence and uncertainty.
