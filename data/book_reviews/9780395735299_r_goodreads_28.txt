This book was 100% "on brand" for Patton. Maybe it's because much of what I have heard/read/seen about Patton in the past is based on this book, but it felt as though Patton himself had carefully crafted his brand in an age when that was far less common than today. The silver helmet, the pearl handled pistols; his relentless committent to the offensive and the attack; it all feels so relentless that it is either a core part of his nature or it's a splendid act that has been rehearsed to promote the image he wanted to portray. The additional factor this book contained was his deep knowledge of history. Largely around warfare, but other things as well, even in African culture. Maybe not deep but certainly broad. I don't know his family background, but some of the stories of the life of a general being taken from one luxury location to another was a bit nauseating when thinking about the situation the troops were in, but Patton did seem to embrace his role as a representative of the US government and humbled himself when in the thick of battle to not sit too high above the troops. Even then though, it was a very carefully orchestrated image he had. "Allow the troops to see you moving forward toward the front, but fly in a plane back the headquarters so they don't see you moving away from the front". Likely sage advice, but it just seems contrived.Overall, a good book for anyone interested in warfare, battle, and history. You can't study WWII without studying Patton. At times a bit dry and probably more relatable to someone in the military, but I enjoyed the book for what it was.
