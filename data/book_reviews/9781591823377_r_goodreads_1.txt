Volume 5 findsGravifirmly set into its defining mixture of absurdist humor and truly deranged and dark storylines, occasionally intwined in moments of peak bizarreness. So we managed to have what may be attempted murder and two characters visiting the zoo dressed as capybaras, to mention but an example. And these two events are at least not connected, we also getShuuichi bursting through a wall, in chibified form, just as Yuki and Aizawa are having a confrontation.We get to meet a couple of new characters, watch Shuuichi and Yuki's relationship actually take steps towards becoming, well, a relationship, and get to find out a bit about what the length of a manga means in terms of actually creating it.These new members of the cast include K, an American who up and decides to become Bad Luck's manager (used to manage the weirdly named Nittle Grasper), all along spewing random English lines and touting his gun (because, American, get it?), Suguru, a boy genius and new keyboardist in charge of the arrangements and Ryuuichi, the often mentioned singer of the above mentioned very weirdly named Nittle Grasper.Ryuuichi had already appeared very shortly in volume 4 and is such a presence that had arguably already been a member of the cast but the other two are new to us. Which is not say that they are new to Murakami-sensei.As she explains in one of those increasingly harder to decipher running bits of commentary (will the new translation keep these, and if so, will they be printed normally or opt for a style closer to the crabbed handwriting, I wonder), she had already designed K and I think Suguru as well, at an earlier date. If I remember correctly, the NG members predate Shuuichi and Yuki.This co-opting previously designed characters for new purposes and changing them considerably along the way is part and parcel of the manga having run much longer than it was meant to. Murakami-sensei even states that she feels like she could go on writing it forever. Which is a wonderful thought but goes to show that these specific strategies become pressing and may very well have something to do with the publishing schedule. Adding to the recurring cast requires a kind of foresight that is not always allowed when chapters need be produced and deadlines kept so recycling, so to speak, already existing characters becomes not just valid but probably borderline unavoidable.With all this said, everyone fits perfectly into the manga. K is zany and a walking American cliché. This means he is tall, very blonde, bulldozers his way through everything, touts the above mentioned gun and has no qualms in literally just grabbing Shuuichi and running off with him. Granted, he is under the impression that Shuuichi is Ryuuichi- more on this later- but this is his introduction scene for a reason. Interestingly enough, if his being a foreigner leads to a lot of very uncomfortable talk about "the damn foreigner" even on the part of very mild and shy Sakano, it also means that Shuuichi expects K to be more understanding of queerness. This is a very interesting glimpse into perception across borders.One does not associate the United States in the mid-90s with a haven of queer tolerance and acceptance. Yet there was a kind of visibility there that did not quite exist in Japan, despite Shinjuku's 2-Chome. A good way of putting things into perspective is to consider the movie "Philadelphia" released in 1993 and presenting a gay character with great dignity and straight comedian "Hard Gay" who debuted in 2003 whose entire shtick is his acting "gay" while attired in leather. The connection may be tenuous but if we keep in mind that "Hard Gay"'s entire act revolved around his dressing in leather and thus appearing more "American" and thus acceptable to be shown on Japanese television- as per the actual code!- as a native queer performer, or someone even impersonate more native forms of queerness would have been deemed unacceptable, it becomes clear that regardless of how vibrant queer circles may be, American queerness- as mediated by film, too- is oddly more visible as far as a general audience goes.Gravithrives so much on parody that K's being a walking caricature with a good heart and genuinely good ideas to help the band does not hit as it might do in other manga. Very often, American characters get poked fun at but in a good humored kind of way.Suguru is a teenager who is in school- I think, as does Shuuichi- and is mistaken by him as a would-be Idol when they crash meet at the lobby of the agency. Here, the manga shows that for all its oddities, it has a finger firmly set on the country's music industry as Shuuichi mentions a real talent agency that specialized in Idols. Since then, horrible stories of abuse connected to it have surfaced but at time these were not known.Shuuichi's beef with him comes from Suguru taking the liberty of doing his own arrangements, being very young (although he is not actually that much younger than Shuuichi himself, I don't think) and being too mysterious a person. If the overall note of this volume is that Shuuichi is unfair to the new elements in his band, being immediately dismissive of K and dead set against Suguru, his concerns are not entirely invalid. Yes, he is very spastic about it, venting at Yuki who for once is actually very helpful, and showing a rotten attitude but expecting a composer and singer to just get a new manager on the fly, who is not even Japanese (which matters since the industry in Japan is a really unique beast and requires specific knowledge) and a keyboardist whom he has never met and about whom he knows virtually nothing, is a stretch.Turns out Suguru was being so cagey because he is Seguchi's cousin.If all this were not enough, there is also Ryuuichi.Ryuuichi is a strange case as he was vital in shaping Shuuichi as a musician and such a major influence in his formative years that a lot of the tension in this volume results precisely from Shuuichi's struggling to know precisely how to relate to his idol- and how to even consider himself as competition- with emotions that are very personal involved. It is not just being uncertain of how to interact with someone as massively famous as Ryuuichi but also how to handle what Shuuichi, wrongly, views as a snub.Ryuuichi does not make it easier, either, although it is not intentional. In a very real sense, he incarnates the duality of this manga. Usually, he is a complete goof who makes Shuuichi come across as a well grounded individual. Childish to the point he carries a bunny plush around, his mode of speech is not just informal but highly immature and he spends a lot of time in chibi form. As mentioned above, he dresses up as a bunny to go the zoo, which is meant to hide his identity as per Tatsuha's idea (who works parttime at the zoo despite being a Buddhist monk (!) and apparently knows Ryuuichi well enough for this to be a thing) but is absolutely in character. He is friendly to the point of annoyance, immediately befriending anyone and takes such a shine to Shuuichi that he asks for Shuuichi's autograph after an impromptu duet.I suspect that at some point, the two characters were either one or were drawn from a third one. The names are alike and they are supposed to look enough alike for K to mistake them.But Ryuuichi has another side that defines him as much as this silliness. When he performs, he turns into an entirely different person. Visually this is conveyed by the character design actually shifting entirely. He goes from puppy eyed super deformed in a panel to narrowed eyes, normal sized and sharply drawn in the very next. All it takes is his being in the zone as a performer.K comments on this and I think it had been mentioned before but it will become a much more important theme as the story progresses. To the point several find him "scary" when he is in this mode and the sudden switch is viewed as prodigious even in a manga where tonal shifts are common. There are even fan theories that Ryuuichi has killed someone or would do so without so much as batting an eye and is the real scary one of the bunch, not Yuki or Seguchi. I suspect this is a bit more of flanderization going on but it is quite an interesting way of looking at him. The manga itself likes to sort of wink in this direction, even as early as this: in one of the chapter opening pages, the bunny has "food source" written across the head and looks quite distressed.Shuuichi is at first dismayed when he lands himself in duet with Ryuuichi at a TV show and thinks he was slighted, then angry at being an opening act for the newly reassembled NG at another show then overwhelmed at Ryuuichi's superiority as a singer. This a whole rollercoaster of emotions to pack in a few chapters. Overall, it works, if only because Yuki steps up to the plate and offers genuinely helpful perspective. This is Yuki at his best and it is probably not amiss that he is at his most human in this volume. I've mentioned that the anime takes Yuki way too seriously. The manga has him blush and act really embarrassed at Shuuichi's entirely deliberate displays of affection. This is another way in whichGravibroke with the meta-genre conventions. More, Shuuichi is entirely able and willing of using his sex appeal, and this volume progresses into a kind of true consent that even modern BL does not always achieve.To this day, I fearGraviwas not given a fair shot if only in this very salient regard. The way in which the mutual attraction is depicted is not just great, it's borderline innovative.Not everything works, though. Shuuichi's anger at not being a lead act is odd as it is to be expected. NG is only the biggest act in the country and his band just got started. His frustrating at not measuring up to Ryuuichi also feels a bit misplaced. Ryuuichi has years and years of experience, after all. K is playing it as allowing Shuuichi is grow into challenging NG in due time but here we have genre constraints.Japanese media loves to treat music as a kind of zero sum game. Fierce competition is either actually hard baked into the premise, to the point it's a subgenre, or it becomes important once rivals are introduced. Music media tends to function as sports media: there is a winner and a loser. It makes sense from a dramatic point of view and injects energy into the narrative but it is also very difficult to convey. These pivotal moments, when the underdog encounters the utterly overwhelming superiority of the powers that me and realize fully the gap between them, serve to motivate them. The problem with music related media is that it is not as easy to depict this apparently fundamental disparity. Sports can do this by simply giving us the record of wins from the excellent team/athlete and then have them clubber the heroes.But when it comes to music, be it a 2D depiction or an audio support, we are dealing with professionals whose work is absolutely on par. If anything, the actual music, that inGraviexists through the anime (and drama CDs, I think) blurs these lines even more. Shuuichi's voice actor is every bit as good as Ryuuichi's. He is not as sultry but that's more a matter of style than anything else. I do not think anyone who listens to songs from both bands, in this period, would reach the conclusion that NG is simply so ahead of BL.We are clued in about this in-universe disparity through character reactions. Everyone on set likes BL's set but NG's just blows it out of the water. This is unanimous, too, and leads to another oddity in treating too much like sports: there is little in terms of subjective appreciation. One band is better and that is it. This superiority is validated by profit, number of CDs, merch sold, number of concert goers and other very clear quantifiers.The risk is to slide into a kind of capitalism with rhythm, with quality being one with quantity. What is meant to be at play is the amount of hearts touched, not the money made, but in practical terms it is the money that serves as the signifier of this emotional pull.It is not often addressed but there is an understanding that this zero sum game exists within each genre. Presumably, there would be "a best rap artist" who would not be better or worse than NG. But even considering that, it leaves precious little space for individual likes and dislikes. This is particularly strange as Japan has plenty of Indies bands with very loyal followers.The subjective element that is such a crucial element to all music (take me, name a famous band that odds are I will be indifferent to it but I'll talk symphonic gothic metal all day long) is stripped in favor of the competition angle, the leads have to win and for that happen there must an actual clear winner. None of this "I like the kind of music I like" stuff.In-universe, this allows an unlikely comparison between Aizawa, of all people, and Shuuichi. Aizawa's situation remains utterly bonkers as even in the story, he has no reason to overwhelmed by Shuuichi but his deeply felt sense of inferiority as a singer and uncertainty as whether he can ever even begin to bridge the gap, is not that different from Shuuichi's deeply sense of inferiority toward Ryuuichi. The manga does not bring this home but a reader cannot help but considering it.Overall this is a great volume. Murakami-sensei is, so to say, winging it at this point but she is doing it with grace. It must be really stressful, this kind of writing, when you cannot be sure how much room you'll have to expand your story and still have to plan ahead so as to give it a sense of cohesion. It would be so in any medium but manga's insane deadlines surely add the pressure.Here's to hoping that the rerelease in English will help putGravion the map again, at least as far as localization goes, and bring more informed critics to it so that its legacy can be fully appreciate for all it was.
