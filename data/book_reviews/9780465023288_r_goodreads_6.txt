Facing the Past

I have to say I mostly enjoy Sir Niall Ferguson’s work. I find him intelligent, thought provoking and live the wide range of topics he turns his attention to. However, I am aware a lot of people don’t like him. His book Empire: How Britain Made the Modern World is probably one of the main reasons. I would say that this book offers a compelling and controversial examination of the British Empire's global impact. Ferguson’s central thesis is that, despite its many flaws, the British Empire was a force for good in shaping the modern world. He argues that British imperialism laid the foundations for globalisation, promoting free trade, legal institutions, parliamentary democracy, and even improving health and education across its colonies.

Ferguson’s writing is engaging and accessible, blending historical narrative with economic analysis. He excels at making complex geopolitical and economic trends comprehensible, showing how Britain’s naval and financial power enabled its imperial dominance. is well-researched, drawing on a broad range of sources, and Ferguson’s insights into economic history are particularly strong. He explores how Britain exported capitalist institutions, often laying the groundwork for modern economies in places like India, Australia, and Hong Kong.

However, is also deeply controversial for its sympathetic portrayal of British imperialism. Ferguson acknowledges the brutal aspects of empire, such as the exploitation, violence, and racism, but he consistently frames these as outweighed by the benefits. This interpretation has been criticised for downplaying the suffering of colonised peoples and failing to fully account for the long-term negative impacts of imperial rule, such as the economic and social inequalities that persist in former colonies. Another criticism is Ferguson’s tendency to prioritise economic and institutional achievements while minimising cultural or ethical considerations. His focus on Britain’s contributions to modernity often glosses over the ways in which the British Empire disrupted local cultures, imposed artificial borders, and fostered ethnic tensions that persist to this day.

Overall, is a thought-provoking and well-written book that will appeal to readers interested in global history, particularly those intrigued by the British Empire’s role in shaping modern institutions. While it offers valuable insights into the economic and political legacies of imperialism, it also reflects Ferguson’s provocative, and at times one-sided, perspective. Readers should approach the book critically, balancing its narrative with other accounts that more fully explore the human costs of empire. What both sides of the fence should consider is that practically all societies have had empires at such point in their past and the British Empire should be seen as neither a force for good or bad. It primarily started for commerce and as often it grew at the hands of locals rather than through British policy which often tried to restrict expansion. Both sides should face the facts there is some good things the Empire did and also some bad things.
