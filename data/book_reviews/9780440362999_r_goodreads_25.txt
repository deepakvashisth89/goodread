I had heard this was a classic piece of environmentalist non fiction…but I’ve read a lot of those, and they tend to be rather depressing. I might have picked this up sooner had I known how FUNNY it is! The writing style, including the ironic statements and multiple self-deprecating moments, reminds me a great deal of Bill Bryson.

Mowat states that his “original plan was to write a satire about quite a different beast – that peculiar mutation of the human species known as the Bureaucrat.” And while, as he notes, the family of wolves he dubs George, Angeline, and Uncle Albert (plus pups) rather take over the story, there is certainly some good skewering of government bureaucracy, as well as the practice of sending people out to try and find specific results – in this case, hoping to back up the claim that wolves, rather than human hunters, are to blame for a decline in caribou.

He also says in the preface that and that this has led many to claim the work is just fiction, but that his observations have been largely backed up by other scientists since. From this, I concluded that what he was that he exaggerated certain incidents for comedic effect – and, certainly, in relating stuff about humans almost anyone could make a good guess at where this happened. Some reviewers here do seem to think this extends to wolf facts as well, contending, for instance, that saying wolves eat mice is ridiculous. But Mowat never said wolves eat mice! Somewhat later in the book he recounts details of how they hunt caribou. But caribou are migratory, so it was not at all surprising to me as an ecologist to read an account of them hunting much smaller game at certain times of the year. Even a tame dog may bite if you invade its space too much, after all.

So, yeah, in addition to being funny, overall the scientific part of the book holds up reasonably well, especially this key point: That going in with your conclusion already set is not proper science! It is important to set up the study so that the data can falsify your hypothesis if, indeed, it is wrong. And, also, back-of-the-envelope calculations can be very helpful in figuring out if people have been lying! For instance, a trapper friend, Mike, says that he kills 200-300 caribou a year, in large part to feed his dog team, while trappers polled in Ottawa (who may have been told the purpose was to explain the decline of the caribou) only claimed to kill 1-2.

At the end of the book Mowat reflects sadly on the fact that, even after getting to know his wolf family well, when getting spooked by finding himself in a confined space with some of them, his next response after fear (which proved unfounded) was and at being made to feel afraid and then silly. As he notes, someone having that reaction with a gun in their hand could easily have committed violence. And while this sequence of events is by no means every human’s response to fear-followed-by-embarrassment, it unfortunately common in those who are or feel they should be powerful, and that can have tragic effects for their fellow humans, as well as animals.
