Quite an end! And quite a story! Must ponder more.

***

This is quite an odd place to start with Hugo. Something about it appealed to me though. Perhaps because I love the ocean. (It turns out I may not love the ocean as much as I thought... 😏). I did love the first half of this book and the final eighth. The descriptions of Guernsey are lovely. I love how Hugo describes the superstition of the islanders and their suspicion of anyone new (like hero Gilliatt) or anything new (like Moss Lethierry's steam engine). Hugo sets up the conflict between the steam engine and the creeping advance of technological progress brilliantly. Into that, he weaves stories of individuals: a sea captain with a secret plan, Lethierry's young, beautiful niece, Moss Lethierry himself, Gilliatt. The crisis of the novel comes and then comes the middle 3/8s of the novel.

The hero, Gilliatt, decides to undertake what seems an impossible task to rescue the steam engine from a shipwrecked boat that is stuck on the treacherous rocks off the coast of Guernsey. When I take a step back from the minutiae of this section, the 3/8s that describe Gilliatt's feats of nearly superhuman ingenuity and strength is riveting. He's literally a hero of old, a Hercules with his labors. If you're an engineer or scientist, I could easily imagine this section being the best part of the book. The details of Gilliatt's primitive tools, his battle with wind, waves, and rock, and his ingenuity in facing many obstacles are all written with incredible attention to detail and a scientific intensity. (There's even a battle with a sea creature! What kind of sea creature?? You must read to find out!)

Unfortunately, I felt that I lost the thread of the novel in this section that started so powerfully in the first half. The minutiae of the engineering and science really bogged down the story for me. (I did lots of judicious skimming, and I don't regret it.) It’s a strange combo of an incredibly intense survival story and incredibly intricate details about the natural world. Hugo says in a short preface that this is his Man v. Nature story. If you like that kind of story, this would be a remarkable read. Turns out I prefer the dynamics of tea and cake and emotional dilemmas. 😜

However, the intensity (of various kinds) in the 3/8s section does set up the end to be exquisitely painful. Actually it’s rather brilliant. Does Gilliatt end as a hero?? Ah, I could debate this question gladly.

I write all this to share my own experience of reading this novel rather than a comment on Hugo’s craft. I am very glad I read this. I have a feeling the imaginative landscape of this story and the epic nature of it will stay in my mind for a long time.
