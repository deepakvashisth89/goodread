Notw: A better version of this essay appears on the Bartleby the Sailor SubstackOn Friday August 18, 1843, a young itinerant sailor signed on the Navy frigate, the USS United States, in Oahu, then part of the Sandwich Islands, as an ordinary seaman. Although little correspondence from his several month sojourn in the Sandwich Islands remains, one can assume that the young man, who had, a couple of weeks prior, marked his 24th birthday, was eager to return “home” to the Eastern Seaboard of the United States. The young man had, about two years prior, signed on a whaling vessel, only to find himself miserable. He had deserted in the Marquesas Islands of the South Pacific, allegedly among a cannibal tribe, and eventually found his way, like a wayward pre-20th century hitchhiker on two whaling vessels, to Oahu.1 This unknown young man was the soon-to-be best-selling author, Herman Melville.2Melville remained on theUSS United States, a ship he was to rechristen theUSS Neversinkin his inimitable tongue-in-cheek fashion, in his fictionalized account of the voyage,White-Jacket or The World in a Man-of-War(1850), for over 14 months. Since he was young and—presumably—in excellent physical condition, he was assigned to sail tending tasks under the supervision of Englishman Jack Chase, the Captain of the Top Main, to whom the book is dedicated, and among the most competent and most revered seaman on the ship. Melville worked aloft—high above the deck—on the For Top Main sail, among other duties. One can infer that Melville was an asset to the crew who performed many of the more dangerous daily duties on board. Thus, his experiences and acceptance by other crewmembers was markedly different from that on his first ship, the St. Lawrence, which plied trade between Liverpool and New York City. On theSt. Lawrence, Melville was a common sailor or ship’s boy. His experiences were that of a mere novice and resulted in his underrated novel,Redburn: His First Voyage: Being the Sailor-boy Confessions and Reminiscences of the Son-of-a-Gentleman, in the Merchant Service(1849).I have referred, many times, to literary reminisces of the sort exhibited inRedburnas Young Man Goes to Sea Novels, many of which I have discussed on this Substack. These works have many similar traits, e.g. a naive narrator from a more gentry-like background than the rest of the crew who is primarily sailing to gain life “experience”; initial ostracism by the more experienced crew; and fear of venereal diseases etc. While Melville was too brilliant and too talented a novelist to fall afoul of most of these obvious cliché tropes and pitfalls, it is worth noting that he completed writingRedburnandWhite-Jacketover the course of several months in the summer, fall and winter of 1849-50 in a frenzied display of productivity that was an attempt to secure financial advances. If one discounts his earlier, hugely successful novels,TypeeandOmoo, which dealt with his desertion on the Marquesas Islands and his salacious adventures there rather than life on ship, one quickly realizes that Melville’s entire maritime career is related—with ample artistic license—in the three novels he wrote between 1848 and fall of 1851, whenMoby-Dick; or, the Whaleappeared. What to make ofWhite-Jacket, which appeared betweenRedburn, which Melville—unfairly in my opinion—considered a lucre earning potboiler, and the erstwhilevastly under-appreciatedMoby-Dick, the belated Great American Novel and a pre-Modernist Modernist masterpiece which eventually anchors the US literary canon?It is my contention thatWhite-Jackethas been unfairly relegated by literary critics to the second tier of Melville’s works, below his essential one: the novels,Moby-Dick; Pierre, or the Ambiguities;and the three novellas,Bartleby, the Scrivener; Benito Cereno;and the posthumousBilly Budd, Sailor. Back in the early 90’s, as a graduate student in Dr. Ken Silverman’s NYU seminar on Hawthorne, Melville and Whitman, I was assigned all the above “essential” Melville works and had to read them cover to cover in three mere weeks along with ample soporific criticism; my resentment to this chore led to my underestimation and knee-jerk resentment of Melville until I started reading him with a mariner’s keen eye years later. Literary critics rarely come equipped with maritime experience, which is a shame, especially when considering that Melville was a mariner before he was a writer.While I recognize all these Melville novels as essential to the American canon,White-Jackettakes prominence in any maritime canon and has a particular spot reserved close to my heart since I have served as a chartered subcontractor on Naval supply vessels as a civilian. Anyone who has ever enlisted in the Navy or worked as a subcontractor on a Naval vessel should be familiar withWhite-Jacket. The characters are timeless and easily recognizable today. Little has changed. Same old same old.Indeed, from the frigate’s crew might be culled out men of all callings and vocations, from a backslidden parson to a broken-down comedian. The Navy is the asylum for the perverse, the home of the unfortunate. Here the sons of adversity meet the children of calamity, and here the children of calamity meet the offspring of sin.(Chapter 18).While not quite at home among this motley assortment of characters, Melville, whose once great gentry family was impoverished due to the foolish mercantile dealings of his father, was more comfortable among the crew than the officers, though he maintained the airs of a man from a noble family who was horrified with the lack of rights that seamen possessed; they had less rights than slaves.3 Melville was of the people, unlike the other great English language maritime author, Joseph Conrad who had sailed as mate and, once, as Captain. Furthermore, unlike his literary contemporary, Richard Henry Dana, who sailed only once, Melville sailed on one merchant ship, three whale ships (albeit two only briefly in order to get to Hawaii) and, finally, theUSS United States,making his view from “Before the Mast” vastly more comprehensive than any other English language author. He amassed all these experiences before writing any of his novels. The impressions he came away with, especially of societal underdogs, the Ishmaels, influenced him until his dying day. See the posthumous Billy Budd, Sailor for an eloquent argument against impressment and the arbitrary power that officers and the Masters-of-Arms wields; Melville’s antipathy towards the Masters-of-Arms, the vessels cops if you will, is apparent throughout White-Jacket.4 Melville was a monster. A monster of the people who eloquently advocated for them in an era before anyone ashore gave them even the whiff of a thought.. . .This Landless was a favorite with the officers, among whom he went by the name of “Happy Jack.” And it is just such Happy Jacks as Landless that most sea-officers profess to admire.; a fellow without shame, without a soul, so dead to the least dignity of manhood that he could hardly be called a man. Whereas, a seaman who exhibit traits of moral sensitiveness, whose demeanor shows some dignity within; this is the man they, in many cases, instinctively dislike. The reason is they feel such a man to be a continual reproach to them, as being mentally superior to their power. He has no business in a man-of-war; they do not want such men. To them there is an insolence in his manly freedom, contempt in his very carriage. He is unendurable, as an erect, lofty-minded African would be to some slave-driving planter.(Chapter 90).5As I mentioned above, Melville wroteWhite-Jacketin a frenzy of literary activity to maximize his dwindling advances.Mardi and a Voyage Thither6 which precededRedburn, did not sell well, and publishers were increasingly leery of the financial risk in associating with a truculent racy author whose eloquent logorrhea went off on tangents that befuddled the literary establishment. Add to this Melville’s dismissal of and suspicion of Christian missionaries, and we have an author poised to fail in sententious mid-1800’s literary society and, at the very least, throughout his beleaguered lifetime. However, although many critics who never went to sea may denigrate or underestimateWhite-Jacket, all while doing the diligent and requisite critical research to separate fact from fiction and show that Melville pirated certain incidents from other sources, most notablyLife In a Man-of-War or Scenes in “Old Ironsides” During Her Cruise in the Pacific by A Fore-Top-Man(1841),White-Jacketremains a recognizable chronicle or log depicting the day to day reality on board a naval vessel from the humdrum daily routine—recognizable to all mariners— to the ever-present threat of flogging and the depiction of bizarre incidents, e.g. an old sailor being capriciously flogged for refusing to shave his beard.While I have never served as a member of the US Navy, I have worked countless times as a civilian subcontractor on US Navy chartered supply vessels in positions ranging from Wiper to Able-Bodied Seaman to Third Mate. Never have I read a book seeping with more veracity about the industry from depictions of a drunken cavalcade of degenerates returning from shore leave (Liberty) to surreptitious gambling and a Master pseudonymously but aptly named Captain Claret.7 The only book in my ken that comes close to conveying the plight of an enlisted man in the US Navy as accurately as Melville did is Darryl Ponsican’sThe Last Detail(1970), which does not take place on any vessel. Most of you will recall the Hal Ashby film version, which was one of the best films of the 70’s with an iconic role played by Jack Nicholson.
