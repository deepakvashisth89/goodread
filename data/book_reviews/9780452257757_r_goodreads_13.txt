This was my favorite book yet; the protagonist acquires a real lover after a couple of marriages of convenience. While this is going on, World War II continues to wave in the background, while side characters report back about conditions in their various European home countries, or try out the newly formed Israel. Some people die horrifically offscreen. This book finally explains the title of this quintet, “Children of Violence,” about the veneration raised after the horrific mass death of World War I who were taught to never repeat it, only to have WWII unleash even more violence and horror and atrocities. The massive scope of the violence defines their view of the world, but at the same time, the messy complications of daily life continue.More than the three books that came before it, this one deals with the reality of living in a segregated colony that relies on Black slavery but has white people being superior and defensive about it. The tension when the Black labor force goes on strike for a few days is intense. The paternalism and lack of satisfaction from white socialists who want to “help” these Black people to reject their oppressors and colonizers without ever actually getting to know any of them, or discuss openly what they might want - Lessing is vicious in her excoriation of white complacency, imo.And among all of this, Martha Quest is visiting a dying parent while reading headlines about unfathomable war crimes, dealing with her mother’s disapproval and judgment and also efforts to express love. The incongruity of familial tragedy interwoven with almost senseless geopolitical war games felt uh, pretty familiar.It’s difficult to capture what I like so much about Lessing’s writing: the tedium and insufferable personalities present at political meetings; the cringing realization of how ridiculous you were in your early twenties even when everything felt very grave at the time. The pain and humor people express when in conversation with one another - the unspoken aspects of communication.It reads like so many books about younger protagonists with detailed analyses of feelings and the structures around you, but does not shy away at all from the subject matter of adulthood.I’m fascinated by how book 5 is going to go - so far it’s been thinly-disguised autobiographical stuff, but book 5 takes a departure into an SF future, apparently. Kind of dying to see how that goes.
