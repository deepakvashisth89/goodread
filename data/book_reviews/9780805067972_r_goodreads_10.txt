This is another book I read in high school and spent at least half an hour trying to find the title to read years later. Like Drowning Anna, which I read last year, I liked it better ten years ago, most likely because I'm no longer the target audience and the book is clearly a product of its time. It was a fun reread for nostalgia's sake, at the very least.

The most unique thing about this book is the way Sara's past is explained through her writing. Instead of using flashbacks or explaining through her diary the trauma she experienced due to her biological father sexually abusing her, everything is hinted at within the assignments Sara turns in for her honors lit class. Because she's been in and out of foster homes and doesn't open up easily, her internalized confusion and feelings come out in her writing, and it's heartbreaking with each assignment to learn a little more about what she's been through.

That doesn't mean the book isn't littered with problems. Dulcie, Sara's best friend, falls flat and doesn't get much development beyond the lovesick teenage girl stereotype. Sara herself often comes across as judgmental and catty when it comes to comparing her interests to her peers, and reading about that in 2019 is getting old, even for a book published in 2001. There are subplots that never get resolved and the ending is wrapped up much too quickly; the climax is devastating and emotional, but things move too fast after the initial impact and everything works itself out, despite the heartbreak. I would have liked to see a bit more struggle for Sara, because while she earns her happy ending, the overall book could have benefited from a longer resolution.

Slight nitpick: the email format was too saturated and had too much going on with the dates and times, and it was a relief when the AOL messaging was introduced. I realize that this takes place in the late 90s and technology was nowhere near as convenient as to what we have now, but for pacing and a cleaner story, I wish the messaging system would have been utilized more than the email.

I don't think this is a bad book, but I am disappointed that it fell short of its potential. As a character-driven piece, it has a strong main character and addresses heavier topics in a respectful way, but it severely lacks fleshed-out side characters and has major pacing issues. I'd love to see Mary Beth Lundgren revisit this, since it was her first YA novel, but I know that's wishful thinking on my end. I liked getting to revisit this book and discovering things I hadn't paid attention to the first time around, and for that it was definitely worth it.
