This book is, I suppose, something of a family favorite. It was a favorite of my father's and became one of mine as well. R. H. Dana was a student at Harvard in the 1830s who, following an illness which compromised his eyesight and forced an extended leave from study, signed on as a rank-and-file seaman aboard a merchant vessel bound to California via the arduous passage around Cape Horn. (It is worth noting that, in the old days, “deck hands” and other sailors without rank or of low rank were berthed in the forecastle of a ship, while officers were berthed in the stern. The fact that Dana was numbered among the former during his voyages is reflected in the title: .) The trade involved procuring a shipload of dried cattle hides from storehouses up and down the coast of California, which were transported back to Boston to be turned into leather goods by the factories there, many of which were in turn transported back to California on subsequent voyages to trade for more raw hides.

The book is delightful both as a portrait of life at sea in the days of sail and as a sketch of California as it was before acquisition by the United States following the Mexican War and the Gold Rush of 1849 transformed it forever. I traveled to California for the first time shortly after first reading this book, and Dana’s account greatly enriched the experience. One of the high points of that trip was a visit to the mission of Santa Barbara and its beautiful old fountain, from which Dana had watered his own horse during an excursion ashore some 160 years prior.
