Hot on the heels of Han Solo at Stars' End, Daley's second novel in the Star Wars universe hit shelves in late 1979, still exploring the background of Han Solo prior to the events of George Lucas's film. Despite ostensibly being slung together at much the same time as the previous book, it's remarkable how much more mature Han Solo's Revenge feels over its predecessor.

Here, Han is tricked into unsavory work by a couple of shady individuals, and when the job sours and Han blasts a few of them before he gets paid, he sets out on a mission to take out his would-be employers and get what's his. He's joined this time by a much less stupid cast of secondary characters, although Daley still writes with more of a sensibility of than .

The most egregiously stupid character is ultimately Spray, an otter-like "skip-tracer" who chases Han and Chewbacca around for a debt. The character being a weird anthropomorph doesn't have much bearing at all on the story of the novel, but it's yet another strange creature-creation decision that seems endemic to the writers of these early novelists; while it's clear Lucas and his creature studio took inspiration from many real-world critters for their alien designs, straight up cartoon characters are an ongoing annoyance for me in these stories.

But aside from the cartoonish nature of some of the secondary characters, the early sections of Daley's novel is just about perfect, and as always, Daley puts his foot to the floor when it comes to pacing. This book is jam-packed with action in the style of the old pulps from which he continues to draw inspiration, with dogfights and daring escapes and shootouts bristling across every page. Daley's book zigs when you feel like it should zag, yet never fails to deliver some high-quality low-brow entertainment.

Still, the fast-paced nature of the book can't hide the fact that the book's plot and final denouement feels incongruous with the premise of the book, and I found the final chapter, especially, to be laughably incomprehensible. For all that Daley crams into his story--and it is a --it feels like the plot all falls apart abruptly in the end, leaving me scratching my head as to what the dude must actually have been thinking when he wrote it.

Regardless of how stupidly the book ends, it's 100% adrenaline, and I found myself laughing out loud at just how zany Daley's adventure really is. There's so much to love about this book, from its daring action pieces to Daley's genuinely invested take on what makes Han Solo such a great character, and I keep thinking about just how much more fun these novels are than they deserve to be.
