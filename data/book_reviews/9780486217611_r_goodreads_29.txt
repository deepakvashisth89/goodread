Actually managed to get this done before the end of October, nice.Sitting down to write a review for this book is a pretty daunting task, given the immense scope of the work. But I guess this holds true for most philosophy texts. Really, these should be texts that are studied over the course of many months to years, so take with a grain of salt that, having read this in the span of a few weeks, the flaws that I identify here may have already been addressed.Also, I should probably note that yes, indeed this is only labelled as 'Vol. 1', but the second volume was released decades after the original text and merely contains supplementary essays that Schopenhauer refers the reader to in various aspects of the text. This second volume is also longer than the first, so I don't think I'll be tackling it any time soon.Actually, going down that route, Schopenhauer is a particularly demanding philosophical author. To tackle this text, you really should be quite familiar with Kant's main works, as well as Plato's, and should also have some familiarity with Eastern religions. Schopenhauer will also tell you in the preface that you ought to read his other book, 'On the Principle of Sufficient Reason,' which he treats as an introductory essay. He also expects you to read 'The World as Will and Representation' front to back twice, as the earlier parts expect some familiarity with later parts. Really, it's all quite confusing.But anyways, onto the text I guess. Gonna be a long review.Schopenhauer splits the text into four books:1. The world as representation, first consideration2. The world as will, first consideration3. The world as representation, second consideration4. The world as will, second considerationHe also includes a lengthy appendix critiquing Kant's philosophy.In book 1, Schopenhauer lays out a view of the world quite similar to Kant's. Namely, he lays out how the world we experience around us necessarily conforms to oura prioriconcepts of space and time, which condition all existence. In fact, he takes this a step further, and claims that there is 'no object without subject,' which is really a quite succinct way of summing up transcendental idealism in a single phrase. He goes on to state more about the principle of sufficient reason (i.e., causation) as well as laying out what he deems to be a neater separation of the mental faculties. Some parts of this felt antiquated, other parts got very bogged down in technicalities. I largely struggled through these parts, but somehow managed.In book 2, things really start flying off the rails and getting interesting. To begin with, Schopenhauer claims that the world presented as representation is unsatisfying, as it doesn't tell us anything about what we ourselves are (as thing in itself, not representation) as well as what anything around us actually is in itself. Schopenhauer's solution to this comes from looking inwards, and experiencing our own essence intuitively. He claims that there is a certain essence present in everything; in ourselves, in animals, in plants, even in inanimate objects. He dubs this essence "will". This is where the influence of Eastern religions comes in. To be sure, Schopenhauer doesn't attribute any sort of divine nature to this will of his; rather, he himself identified as a staunch atheist, something apparent in his various attacks on religion scattered throughout this work. Instead, he describes the nature of this will that pervades all things as one of "striving". He uses examples of how objects strive towards Earth in the case of gravity, or how life forms strive towards more life through survival and reproduction, etc. etc.Now, of course, there are major problems with this view on the world. First off, if we ourselves are merely representation, how on earth are we supposed to be able to get a notion of the thing-in-itself of objects that aren't us? I mean, sure, we can look inward and try to get a grasp on our own consciousness and faculties etc., but how exactly can we attribute whatever we discover here to things totally separate from ourselves? Really, this notion of the will and striving seems to be merely a tidy way to wrap up all of our representations into one abstract concept, done through apparent personification of everything around us, i.e. by attributing the will Schopenhauer recognizes in the human being to being present in everything. I think this is something Kant himself, even with his own flaws, would recognize here as a gross overstep of reason.But anyways, continuing onwards to book 3. This is primarily concerned with how we can escape our usual existence as a striving, "will"-ful being and instead enter a state of contemplation of the Platonic Ideas. The thought here is that, when we enter into a contemplation of these Ideas that are present across space and time throughout nature, we ourselves begin to see the universal that is threaded throughout everything. Schopenhauer examines how this contemplation can occur through media such as physical beauty, various forms of art, nature. Here there are also some quite antiquated concepts, such as physiognomy, the beautiful vs. the sublime, etc. etc. He also advocates for attacking bad artists: "Critics and satirists should pitilessly and mercilessly castigate the mediocre poets." Maybe a bit extreme.Finally, in book 4, Schopenhauer considers the world as will again. Here, he lays out his systems of ethics and virtue under the framework of the world as will. Simply, this can be summarized as empathy and the golden rule, with some slight tweaks and worded using more philosophical language. He also lays out his thought that we, as human beings with reason, can actually overcome the will in ourselves. Again, this is an idea seemingly taken right out of Eastern religion (Schopenhauer acknowledges Eastern religions throughout). Namely, by identifying all desiring with all suffering, we can learn to let go of all desires and attain a state of perfect empathy, essentially, through seeing the one will that is present in everyone. Notably, Schopenhauer is not advocating for suicide here, as that would be an assertion of the will. He does, however, state that one who dies by simply forgetting to eat, i.e. suicide by starvation, is something that he would essentially admire. Now, of course, there is the slight problem that one must will to become will-less in order to become will-less, and hence we have a conundrum. And then there are other small problems with the notion of becoming an ascetic in general, but which I think I laid out in my review of the Pali Canon previously, so will not harp on here.Finally, there is the Appendix, critiquing Kant's philosophy. This runs ~100 pages and is quite lengthy and technical. Essentially, Schopenhauer praises Kant for identifying space and time as a priori conditions for all experience, but lambasts Kant's architectonic tendencies and obsession with symmetry in his presentation of ideas, things which he believes hindered Kant's philosophy and all who followed after him, as they introduced various errors into an otherwise groundbreaking and error-free system (If you're familiar with Kant's 'Critique of Pure Reason', this refers to most everything in that volume besides the Transcendental Aesthetic).So yeah. Schopenhauer had some pretty nuts ideas. He also was a very aggressive critic: of religions, universities, Hegel, Kant, contemporaries, philistines. Really, pretty much everyone to a certain extent. Perhaps his greatest accomplishment here is his cleaning up of some Kantian ideas. As for the whole idea of world as will, I personally think it's pretty unconvincing and effectively spirituality without religion. But I also would probably need a few more reads to be certain. To quote Schopenhauer himself: "It is much easier to indicate errors and mistakes in the work of a great mind than to give a clear and complete account of its value."If you've made it this far, you're a real one. I appreciate you.
