In 1935, Nobel-prize-winning author Sinclair Lewis published this novel, which describes a Fascist takeover of the US following the upcoming 1936 election. The takeover is similar to the one Hitler effected after coming to power in Germany two years earlier. The fictional American dictator, Buzz Windrip, is supposed to be modelled on the real authoritarian politician Huey Long.

The blurb says the novel was written very quickly, and it shows. It doesn't read like it's been written by someone who'd recently won the Nobel Prize; it reads like it's been put together in two weeks by someone who's drunk far more coffee than their doctor would recommend. As literature, it's not very successful, though it is a page-turner. But the question is irrelevant; the author is evidently not trying to write literature. He's trying to stop the US from going Fascist.

In fact, the US didn't go Fascist, and the thing I kept wondering was how much this was due to Lewis's book. Lewis keeps describing European journalists who visit the US after the takeover and post admiring pieces in their newspapers. "None of it is true!" they exclaim. "The people love President Windrip! His methods are unorthodox but successful. Talk of violence is grossly exaggerated." I'm guessing that this is modelled on what American journalists were writing about Hitler. I still find it extraordinary that representatives of what were soon to be the Allies turned up for the 1936 Olympics in Berlin and were even instructed to give Hitler the Nazi salute, "so as not offend him needlessly". We were slow to understand how bad it was. We almost always are.

Maybe Lewis helped open enough people's eyes that he really did make a difference. It was very difficult to get the US to act against Hitler; it's well-known that Roosevelt had to resort to all kinds of tricks to overcome "America first" sentiment. I don't think any one thing was decisive, I have seen at least three different accounts, but I wonder if this wasn't part of it. Lewis was a popular writer at the time.

It's nice to think that writing without fear about what you think is vitally important may, just possibly, change history.
