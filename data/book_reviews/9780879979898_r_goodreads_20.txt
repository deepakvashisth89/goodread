I wish I read the reviews that explicitly stated this ends without resolution, because aside from that Chanur’s Venture is a fun and rewarding challenge of a ride.

Cherryh writes a bit like Hemingway: straightforward scant exposition or reflection; attentive readers will be rewarded. Cherryh just presents the action, and it’s up to the reader to note any significance and consequences. I am happy I read this with somebody, as we were in continual discussion figuring things out, oftentimes one of us missing a detail. For example, in one scene Captain Pyanfar and a crewmember are commenting on a list of ships docked at station.



…and that’s it! No explanation or backstory. I flipped through the book several times and found nothing; perhaps the Kekkikkt is mentioned in the first book.

Cherryh is not going to stop and explain things to you, be it an alien culture or the story itself. This makes interactions with Tully (the only human) and other aliens fun and authentic. When a golden ring is given to Tully, he sure acts weird, but readers recognize this as a wedding band. Now, we may be able to understand humans, but , and ? Yeah right. There were several parts where I was lost trying to understand the dialogue of a mahendo’sat, yet Captain Pyanfar seemed to understand things fine; I felt like I was just nodding through a conversation.

For me, this provided the charm for the series so far. Each alien species is a puzzle to figure out, and the same goes for the plot. Action picks up abruptly, and before I could figure out what is happening the characters are already reacting. I love the , a seemingly goofy primate race that can barely communicate, half their dialogue consisting of “got” or “number one”. However, these aliens seem to be pulling the strings like the illuminati, and their military might is unmatched.

While reading, we went back and forth discussing whether or not the book is feminist. I’m still not sure, but it is ironic to read aliens discussing a cultural “glass ceiling” for males in their culture. The relationships between gender and culture is certainly a major theme. I must mention the seem like an unintentional hyperbole of LGBTQ+ culture.

I strongly recommend readers flip to the back and read the appendix fully before getting a quarter through the book. I am loving this series and eager to get to the next book.
