I don't even know how to classify this book. What a weird, and surprisingly racist, story.T.H. White appears to have held pretty strong anti-Irish views, which I've heard about in a vaguely historical sense (not a thing we really learn in the American educational system) but don't think I've ever actually experienced in books before. Lest you think I exaggerate, here's one of the not uncommon passages where his English writer main character ("Mr. White") talks about what it's like to live in Ireland:Good God, I wonder if any living Englishman or American realizes what life in Ireland is really like? They can have no idea of it from their own lives, still less from the printed works put out by the Irish. If you have to live as the next-door neighbor of an empire, naturally you get an inferiority complex and console yourself with delusions of grandeur. Naturally you invent a lot of wonderful reasons why you are the only decent person living, and why the empire is really a scoundrel who does everything wrong.The only bridges that hold up during the Second Flood are, of course, the ones that were built by those terrible invading Englishmen, and one of them succeeds in smashing Mr. White's new Ark to pieces. The Irish folks, of course, only manage to fall into religious fervors and paranoia and wind up fighting with each other while Mr. White is swept out to sea (to be ultimately rescued by "a mail boat from England"). White-the-writer pokes a certain amount of fun at Mr.-White-the-character, since the Ark isn't exactly a triumph, but his follies are nothing in comparison to the utter imbecility of the people around him.I certainly can't and won't quote everything on this subject, but other lines include: "All his neighbors had always been either thieves or swindlers or assassins" and "the best thing would be to exterminate the Irish altogether."It's never even clearwhyMr. White is living in Ireland to begin with, when he despises it so entirely, but most of the "humor" of this book comes from the bumblingly idiotic (or lazy, or dishonest, or violent) actions of the stupid, superstitious, backwards folks in the little country town where he's settled to write semi-successful books and examine ant hills.The central plot of the book is weird, too; I guess it's supposed to be some sort of satire on society and religion, but I didn't really catch most of that through the mess of the story and the boredom of pages and pages of descriptions of building a giant barn-ship that barely lasts through 50 miles of transit down a flooded river. I don't particularly understand why an angel would show up in Mr. White's kitchen to warn him about the Flood and to tell him to build an Ark, and since it smashes to bits, loses all their provisions and farm equipment, and drowns all the animals on board anyway, the entire thing is just a giant exercise in futility.Maybe that's the point of the book, but it didn't make it very fun to read.Mr. White does appear to be having some suspicions by the end that his Irish landlady, Mrs. O'Callaghan, despite being utterly unintelligent, lazy, and useless most of the time, has turned out to be correct about a number of things, including her refusal to sell her farm to outfit the Ark. There is one little passage where Mr. White almost seems to be learning from his mistakes:What a pity it is that people will go in for racial criticism. The whole thing is perfectly simple. All human beings everywhere are more or less horrible, but they have different fashions of being it, in different places. Consequently, one notices the horribleness of foreigners without noticing one's own - which they notice - just because it is different.But the narrative sinks back into bitterly anti-Irish ranting after that (including some of the bits I quoted earlier), so I'm not sure it sank in. At best, both Whites seem to attribute much of the problem to the too-wet climate of Ireland, which serves as a convenient but disagreeable buffer for England's milder shores. (Thus, an Englishman living in Ireland will eventually become as violent and disagreeable as one of the native inhabitants.)I had this as two stars as I was writing, since I did sort of find the beginning chapters readable, but I've changed my mind as I've been writing this. Not nearly as funny as it wants to be, rather dull in many spots, and with few redeeming features. The best part, really, was Mr. White's dog, Brownie, whom he treats with more kindness and sympathy than any of the humans around him.
