This is a hard book to get into and a hard book to love. Like much Higgins, much that happens is going on between what is visible. The gaps between the characters, their dialogue, their intentions, and the truth are wider than ever. Are the stories true? Maybe. Are they self-serving? Certainly. Do you empathize? No! Or uncomfortably, yes.

It’s worth troubling over because the central theme of the book is the fancy pastry Frances denies herself, then eats: people with money will get what they want. Higgins’ book “Bomber’s Law” is based around the old policeman Bomber’s adage, “They did it for the money.” In “Swan Boats at Four”, they did it because they HAD the money.

But what about the picture of the swan boats? What happened to Rutledge and who believed that he was swindling then? Are the resentful complaints about the black bank regulator meant to be taken seriously? There are so many open-ended tales in this book that it frustrates, and fails to satisfy. That may be a virtue, but it is an uncomfortable one.

The more I think about it, the more I like it, but it’s not the pleasure of it. It’s the detail, and the sordid sad way the illusions of the working upper class decay under the hard eye of the prosecutor.
