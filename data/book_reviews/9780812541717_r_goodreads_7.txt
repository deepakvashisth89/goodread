The White Order (#8) takes place 10 years before The Magic Engineer (#3). For the first time in the series the protagonist, Cerryl, is a white/chaos mage. He was established as a secondary character in The Magic Engineer and subsequently referred to as Cerryl The Great in The Order War (#4), which took place centuries afterwards. The White Order covers Cerryl's childhood to becoming a full mage. I assume the next book, Colors of Chaos (#9), will be about how became known as The Great.

As to be expected, much of the story is Cerryl doing trade work, at a woodmill and as a scrivener. His labors continue even after becoming part of the magic academy. There's far more about his task of cleaning out Fairhaven's sewers with magic rather than school matters. That wasn't something I expected to read. Doing so served as a way to better develop his practical magic skills.

I like how Modesitt's continues to have characters who are mentioned as myths, legends, or are otherwise exceptional secondary characters and then have them be the protagonist of their own story. I appreciate even more so when the viewpoint changes to the antagonist's side, which provides a different perspective. Maybe Modesitt began to believe that he hadn't written a moderate position between chaos and order despite advocating for it. The series thus far has entirely been biased in favor of the perspective of the order mages.

With each book it becomes more clear that the personality of those aligned with Order or Chaos is very much affected by it. It seems to really tilt the scales in favor of nature over nurture, though exceptions exist. Cerryl is one such exception and it's regularly remarked upon. He's not all that different from the previous Order protagonists and I don't know that they would've done differently in these circumstances. The primary difference with Cerryl is that he really wants as much power as possible because he believes it's necessary to survive. He's not wrong in that. The chaos wizards really like killing each other. The disconnect between what they profess to believe and what they actually do is immense. Most of Cerryl's problems are caused by him being sincere and wanting to carry out their highest ideals.

Some of the chapters start with excerpts from an in-universe book written by the chaos mages that describes their view of historical events. It's very biased, bordering on propagandistic, but the factual content of what happened in the previous books is probably about as much as can be expected between hostile civilizations.

There's war, as required to set up the events of The Magic Engineer but it's limited to the last 20% or so and does well enough. Surprisingly it doesn't end with an act of immense magical destruction.

I'm now a bit less than a third of the way through the series. I don't think my opinion is going to change that this is a series to read for its familiarity. Basically it's a comfort series.

Rating: 3.5/5
