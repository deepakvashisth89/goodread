It has been a long time since a book made me physically sick, ad nauseam. It almost never happens, but The Deerslayer has been a remarkable exception, and not because of its deeply ingrained racism, misogyny, prejudice and constant preaching. After all, this was only to be expected, racism and misogyny being the "gifts" of Fenimore Cooper's time (using his own favorite gifts-based terminology). No, my main problem was with the blatant unrealistic idiocy of the whole proceedings. And don't tell me about romanticism and early 19th century -- other authors who wrote even earlier than Cooper did not necessarily depict reality as an absurd theater of preaching idiots, unless they were after a parody or a comedy, which Cooper obviously wasn't. This peculiar depiction of the world is clearly Cooper's own "gift".

Any Shakespeare's tragedy or comedy is a masterpiece of realism and credibility compared to The Deeerslayer. Some examples:

1. "Simple-minded", as Cooper loves to call him, 21 year old illiterate Deerslayer speaks like a 50-year-old preacher on a sermon. Even Harry, who is depicted as the rude man of unscrupulous action, likes to deliver long and rather preaching monologues. Feeble-minded Hettie is fluent in reading and interpreting the Bible, nothing in her looks betrays her mental deficiency and nothing in her speech either, except constant "I'm feeble-minded", "Mom said I have no sense", "I know I have no sense" etc. -- an ideal feeble-minded female character for Cooper's noble purposes.

2. In a scene where two people are being taken prisoners by Indians, with multiple Indians practically hanging on them like grapes on a vine, these characters find plenty of time to shout lots of long and detailed instructions to another character who is waiting for them in a canoe on a nearby lake.

It goes something like this:
"Oy vey, we are being imprisoned by these nasty Injuns whose scalps we wanted to sell blah-blah-blah. Deerslayer, blah-blah-blah, please take care of bla-blah-blah, because you are the only one left now and blah-blah-blah."

Deerslayer replies from the canoe, "I am still young and unexperienced, blah-blah-blah, but I will do my best because blah-blah-blah and blah-blah-blah."

There are many more exchanges of the similar kind before the Indians finally decide to shut up the talkative prisoners.

I hope nobody decided to apply heroic behavior patterns learned from "The Deerslayer" to the life in the wilderness -- the Indians might have scalped them out of sheer boredom.

But, never fear, after all the magic word "gifts" is mentioned only 97 times.

It is a mystery for me how I could like The Deerslayer and two or three sequels when I read them
at the tender age of 10 or 11, but probably one needs to be a naive little kid in pre-Internet era to enjoy Deerslayer's "exciting" adventures.

Read in 2017.
