The basic plot : The U.S. is preparing to enter World War II and Howard Hughes suspects that guests at a dinner party have been surreptitiously photographing plans for his most recent military aircraft. And Hughes decides to hire the just-scraping-by private detective Toby Peters to nail the culprit.

Anything to do with the enigmatic Howard Hughes is bound to catch my eye. But in this book he is distant and unknowable, swaddled in his huge corporate empire and far removed from life itself. Perhaps that's the way it was in real life but it doesn't make for an interesting main character in a novel. Kaminsky gets the details right: He's hard of hearing, incredibly secretive, unknowable to his employees. But these parts don't ad up to a very compelling whole.

Toby Peters is much better drawn but I don't find him that likeable. He seems to make enemies quite easily, e.g. his brother, his ex-wife and with his sardonic view of life it's not hard to see why. Also he seems to get beaten up an awful lot and doesn't miss an opportunity to tell you how much pain he is in.

This, being historical fiction, familiar names come up. Bugsy Segal is a threatening presence that causes Toby to keep his distance but is ready to step forward to battle the Nazi threat, being Jewish hemself.

Bertolt Brecht shows up but doesn't leave much of an impression.

I found the rendering of Basil Rathbone the most engaging. He resembles Sherlock Holmes, the role he is best known for, in his use of ratiocination to draw useful information out of seemingly insignificant details and Peters rueful admits to himself that, without him along for the ride he would know a lot less about his suspects.

Kaminsky spent some time working on the nostalgia angle for 1940's Los Angeles, This succeeded to some extent but I soon found it annoying when he includes the prices on the shopping list e.g. three bars of Lifebuoy for 7 cents.

On the whole I found this book pretty ho-hum with Basil Rathbone being the lone bright spot.
