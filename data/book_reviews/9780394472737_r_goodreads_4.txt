I gave the first in John Updike’s Rabbit series (Rabbit, Run) – 5-stars. Too easy.

The second in the series Rabbit Redux - I found to be a trickier affair.

Harry “Rabbit” Angstrom returns 10 years after the first novel, where he spent considerable time going walkabout, and now we see him back with his wife Janice. They have a teenage son, Nelson. He works for a printing company, and he doesn’t really like his job – he works with his dad, and they do go for a drink at a local watering hole once a week. This seems to be one of Rabbit's only outlets.

His marriage isn’t happy – the couple are still recovering from the death of their baby daughter Rebecca and other events that occurred in Rabbit, Run. Janice drinks a lot, they argue. It’s all pretty miserable.

Janice leaves Rabbit for a guy she works with, leaving Rabbit to his own devices with Nelson at home. Rabbit meets a young girl called Jill, and through her a black drug dealer called Skeeter. They end up living together.



One thing is certain – Rabbit’s downward spiral we witnessed in the first book of this series continues. There’s not a lot of joy here but the writing is powerful, there are some very strong themes, and the sex is explicit.



The strongest themes, depicting 1970s America, in this piece, for me were:

• The Vietnam War – Rabbit is ‘pro’, very ‘pro’ – and will argue the point with anyone.
• The Moon Landing – this consumed all of us worldwide.
• Racism – anti-black sentiment is thick; the racism is overt.
• Nixon – and all that
• Sex and Drugs – seems to be everywhere

There are only four chapters in this book – I found those involving the comings and goings of Rabbit and his relationship and issues with Janice fascinating. Updike really does dig deep into the dramas between the pair. I would describe their marriage as vicious.

But the chapter regarding Skeeter, which covered the time when Rabbit, Nelson, Jill (the young girl) and Skeeter all lived together, was really hard work. I really was not looking forward to picking the book up during that ordeal, but it went on and on and on. The living arrangement between Rabbit, Jill, and Skeeter was twisted – and a bit too much. It wasn’t so much that I was confronted by it – I just found it all a bit unlikely, over the top and repulsive. I just could not imagine a father letting that happen. Anyway – maybe it is me.

But there is no doubt Updike deserves the accolades attributed to him as one of America’s greatest modern writers. There were so many passages I needed to re-read and digest. The prose, some (to be honest) I couldn’t figure out.

Either way – this was worthwhile, but I didn’t enjoy it as much as the first. Does one have to enjoy a book to rate it high? I think so – but that’s just my view. But if you’re after an uplifting experience, forget it. If you want your mind to gnaw away on the gristle of depravity, sex, misery, and old fashioned bad luck – this will be worth a look.



Did I leave this book with any hope? Nah – not really.
