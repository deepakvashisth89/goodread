I'm sorry I can't help it. Like, it's clear Feanor is supposed to be so bad: he's coded with all of the characteristics Tolkien doesn't like. He's an inventor, passionate, prideful. But like if you didn't want me to like him then why did you make him so cool?!A couple of things I got from this reading that I hadn't picked up on in the Silmarillion: the Valar wanted to use the stones to resurrect the trees, which would destroy the stones in the process. To me there's a great symbolism in this. Effectively all the light in the world came from Valinor, even the light the people in middle-earth would need to live by. This light was created by the earth/nature goddess Yavanna but because it took a part of her to make it, the act of creation cannot be repeated.Feanor created a light as well, and similarly these light's are a part of him and he can't just make more. He could have sacrificed his own will, and resurrected the trees...and effectively kinda become a god of middle earth in that way: in that he did something only a God can do. Instead he chooses to become amanand go to middle earth and forge his own destiny.(Now I know technically Feanor is still an elf, but it totally seems like he's really reallytryingto be human. Aside from his personality, he's also the Elf most closely associated with death at this point. And it's the death of his one remaining parent that spurs him along to do the things he did in this book. I'd always thought it was just the theft of his stones, but he actually didn't make the decision to leave Valinor until he found out Morgoth killed his dad. That's when he finally comes to the realization that "OK, these earthly gods cannot be trusted, we need to find our own way in the world." In Tolkien's world, death is "gift of men": it is both a motivator and a liberator. For elves (although an awareness of it certainly motivates) there is no liberation in death: they are told very explicitly by the Vala that "If you go to middle earth, you will most likely die and then you will be in our power." Feanor gives exactly zero fucks.)In my re-read of "The Children of Hurin" last year I was surprised to kind of find this kind of theme of the growing free agency of mankind: Human beings deciding that they're not elves and they have to live different from them and strive to impact the world in their own way. Well in this book, with Feanor effectively rejecting becoming an honorary god, we see the beginning of that theme: the destiny of the world at large begins to pass from the hands of the gods into the hands of the children of God. Later on, this trajectory will continue in the lives of the legendary heroes like Luthien, Beren, and Turin as they strive to make their respective imprint on history. Eventually capstoning in the "every man" heroes of "The Hobbit" and "The Lord of the Rings" as the shapers of destiny in a new age of the world.Also, just gotta mention how friggin brutal Feanor is to the sea-elves after they refuse to help him: They tell him, like, "we love our ships in the same way that you love your stones". Not only does he slaughter any of them that stand in his way, but he alsoburnsthe ships once he's done with them.
