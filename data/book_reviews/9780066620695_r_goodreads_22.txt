I thought this was a great book with fantastic insights. Christensen puts forward a theory on disruptive technological change that is very contrary to modern business thinking. It’s easy to see how this books’ ideas (published in 1996) have influenced entrepreneurial pop culture today.Christensen separates innovation into two types, sustaining and disruptive. The key marker of a disruptive innovation is that it does not suit the demands of existing customers, and requires a new customer base. Most innovations are sustaining, and existing businesses are very good at applying them. Successful businesses struggle with disruptive innovations because they seem to be lower margin, and rejected by their existing customers.I was wowed by this point- the very strategy that is taught in business schools is the reason that established businesses fail when disruptive innovations come along. Market and product planning cannot predict future applications or customer profiles of disruptive innovations. Resource allocation insufficiently supports development of disruptive innovation because resource dollars follow customer demand, but existing customers don’t need technologies that are categorized as disruptive. Thus, incumbent companies ignore disruptive opportunities, waiting around for “the opportunity to be large enough” to support their interest.Another key point in this book is that technological advancement tends to be faster than customer demand. Technologies produce better and better products, to the point that improvements no longer matter to customers. We might be seeing this with each new generation of smartphone. Newer and fancier cameras seem to be one of the major selling points of new smartphones, but the camera improvements don’t influence most people’s purchase decision.Also because of this- disruptive technologies tend to start out with far less capacity than incumbent tech. Because technology scales faster, the disruptive tech eventually reaches the level demanded by customers and starts to compete with (and replace) the incumbent tech.Large businesses struggle to properly invest in disruptive innovations because their value networks don’t align with the required value structure of the new market. For example, major banks have retail branches all throughout the country. These are expensive operations, but have been required to run a bank. In-person interaction was the only way to get customers. Now, fintech companies are competing with banks for deposits and can offer a low-cost solution because these new companies don’t need to pay the cost of an extensive retail network. Incumbent banks can’t compete with the fintech companies because they’re trapped in the value network and cost structure they developed.To solve these problems, Christensen explains that an independent organization must be created to tackle the disruptive innovation, with the focus on quick and inexpensive iterations to find the correct market for the technology. Any time the attempt has been made from within an existing company, insufficient resources are allocated to the project, or its sales are seen as insignificant compared to the size of the overall company, so the effort is abandoned.If the project is the sole focus of a new small organization, they can get excited about small wins. The new company must prove that it can be independently profitable and it can develop a new value network and cost structure based on the innovation. The new company cannot look at the primary market of the existing company to find customers (if it could, then this would be a sustaining innovation, not a disruptive one). They will have to tinker to find the right customers and the right way to develop the product for those customers.As time goes on and technology improves, the new company may start to steal sales from the parent company. This provides a natural transition to the new technology.This book felt like a challenge to many of the things I learned in business school. This felt like an intermediate difficulty of book, and I found myself constantly learning new things. I can see myself referencing back to this in the future.
