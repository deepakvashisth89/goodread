You shouldn't expect much from Greyhawk classics; some familiar references toDungeons & Dragonsand a few rollicking good combats.The Temple of Elemental Evilprovided those, yes, but it managed to underwhelm nonetheless, probably because it's written in the style of a highly literate 14-year-old boy.Author Thomas Reid tried to cram into this modest-sized novel everything but the kitchen sink: some world history, the death of a revered master, a romance, and, naturally, the World in Peril. Because he didn't spend enough time on any of them, he failed at just about all. The main character suffers the death of his master on page 3, but by page 10 he's pretty much over it. The romance commences about six minutes after the protagonist meets the only significant female character in the story. The great threat to the world is made less urgent by the villains deciding they no longer need The Evil Artifact, but then, oh, wait, maybe they do. They're not sure, so neither is the reader.I don't know if Reid has been on an elevator with a woman; if so, you'd never know it fromThe Temple of Elemental Evil. There are only four or five named female characters in the novel, and they serve either food, sex, or the inevitable romantic interest in the main character. (OK, there is one who does neither, but she's a fungus demon, so, you know.) None of them make decisions on their own, without reference to a man, which means this book fails the Bechdel testhard. The one female character who contributes meaningfully to the quest naturally becomes the damsel in distress, which pretty much takes the Bechdel test and throws it into a lake of fire. Maybe I've got it wrong and Reid was writing for adolescent boys; if so, my bad.This novel was published in 2001, but it does its best to mimic 1981, which maybe is theDungeons & Dragonsinfluence, I don't know. So readThe Temple of Elemental Evilwith your expectations set low--you'll still be disappointed, but at least not surprised.
