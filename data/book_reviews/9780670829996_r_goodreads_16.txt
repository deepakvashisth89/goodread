4.5/5 because she was a tad too long, but when do I NOT hold that opinion of any book over 350 pages these days.I didn't think a Murdoch story would leave me feeling disconcerted and uncomfortable - in a good way! It's really interesting to think that this book followsThe Book and the Brotherhood, whose overall message/impression I located a lot easier, and whose general ethos is a lot more positive. It was also funnier to me, but somewhat less plot-driven.The Message to the Planet, meanwhile, comes with one of the most bonkers but clever plots I've read in a long time, packaged inside a long downward spiral into a very non-Under the Netcommentary on meaninglessness. Huh.Admittedly, at least a fourth of Murdoch's musings went over my head here, and I'm caught wondering if I'm in her deliberate grey area, or if I've just zoomed so far ahead into her late career that I'm not quite down with the sickness. This is a criticism of myself - not her. I'm powerfully affected by the message being a whole lot of Nothing, and I think what leaves this book feeling gorgeously vacant at the end is that nobody even CARES that it's a whole lot of nothing. Everyone has their own means of (highly selfish) escapism, apart from Gildas, who's actually been materially suffering, and Ludens, who, while admittedly quite awful, has had just about every potential reparative/restorative thought beaten out of him, long before the events of the novel, because he is so impossibly in love with This Guy and everybody hates him for it.The messiness of everyone involved was equal parts maddening, depressing, and thrilling. I won't lie and say that I wasn't sympathetic for Ludens for the majority of the story, even when I knew he didn't deserve it. I'm a sucker for earnest, and I like the ways Murdoch imbued the historian with a classicist's way of thinking - new events demand a new historical record, a new objective length of events, to be written down, etc. Self-indulgently, it's kinda neat how Murdoch put a historian who believes in objectivity in a novel about the impossibility of human truth DURING THE NUCLEAR AGE. But I digress.Ludens was my favorite character, even when he made my stomach turn, followed by Gildas, and then Maisie. Nobody talk to me about Jack, nor about Franca's conclusion. If my book wasn't a hardback, I would have thrown it across my studio at that reveal. And also nobody talk to me about the letter tampering.
