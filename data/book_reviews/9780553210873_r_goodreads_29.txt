First published in 1886, it's a book that has inspired many a TV and film adaptation based upon its main story arc of the dual personality of Dr Jekyll and his dark alter ego, Mr Hyde.Mostly told from the point of view of lawyer Gabriel Utterson, he recalls a conversation when on one of his regular Sunday walks with his cousin Richard Enfield. Richard relays to him a tale of when he sees a man and girl collide on the street; the man, however, doesn’t stop and tramples on the girl—to which nearby passers-by take umbrage and chase after the man. The odd little man turns out to be Mr Hyde.Gabriel, as a lawyer, is in possession of his friend Dr Jekyll's Will; the Will states that everything should be left to a Mr Hyde. Mr Utterson becomes concerned for his friend and begins to worry about who exactly Mr Hyde is, given the story his cousin relayed.We roll forward a year, and a young maid witnesses the murder of a prominent MP. Mr Hyde disappears, and Dr Jekyll becomes reclusive. The narrative then changes, and the conclusion of the book is recounted via letters and documents written by some of the characters detailing how events came to pass."All human beings, as we meet them, are commingled out of good and evil."It's an allegorical exploration of the good and bad sides of the human psyche and how Dr Jekyll was attempting to separate the two. It leads us to deduce that one side always needs the other for balance; when you upset said balance, it ends up going horribly wrong, as it did for Dr Jekyll.I quite liked this one; it has a dark gothic feel that, for its time, gave us an original interpretation of how monsters can take shape. It has elegant classical-style prose without being over-elaborate, and it didn't feel as dated as some classics I've read either. Comes in at around the 60-page mark, so is quite short and can be read in one sitting."I learned to recognise the thorough and primitive duality of man; I saw that, of the two natures that contended in the field of my consciousness, even if I could rightly be said to be either, it was only because I was radically both."
