I feel strangely sad that Kant's gloomy 3/4 profile will no longer greet me every morning. Much of my summer has been spent poring over this tome with a large mug of tea and a bowl of peeled, sugared grapefruit.
There aren't a lot of feelings that compare to reading such a landmark work of philosophy. Kant's Copernican turn revolutionised Western thought. He probed into the way that our experience is structured at its deepest levels, and reached the conclusion that-
The structures of space and time are a priori conditions of our sensible experience rather than independent features of the external world. In fact, there is very little that we about the external world. Although we can deduce natural laws, and arrive at synthetic a prior knowledge regarding possible experience of the world, we cannot know anything about these things in themselves (Noumena). All we can know is that there an external world.
Although it may seem pessimistic, Kant was actually trying to save science and knowledge from the Humean skepticism that had rocked metaphysics. Hume argued that there was no intrinsic link between an effect and its cause, or the past and the future. Kant replies that notions such as causality and induction are built into our empirical experience of the world itself.
Of course, he does this in a typical moody 18th century German philosopher way. His writing is dense and rigorous. The book is notoriously hard to read, but it get significantly easier as you go on. Kant is extremely precise about the terms he uses. It would be a great idea to keep notes of the categories, important definitions, etc. This is also a great edition. It has large margins, subscripts to denote the precise German word he uses, and just the right amount of notes to add background without making the reading experience drag on even more.
