I read Nietzsche's books and essays in chronological order from this book, Basic Writings, and The Portable Nietzsche, which I read concurrently. So, this review serves for both.Walter Kaufmann translated both of these books. There's an abundance of explanatory footnotes in this book, as opposed to hardly any in Portable N, and this collection has longer and more useful introductory essays.Nietzsche's works are entirely grandiose aphorisms and hollow superlatives, never specifics. "Zarathustra" (i.e., Nietzsche) spoke thus: "Whoever writes in blood and aphorisms does not want to be read but to be learned by heart." His narcissism comes into play. And you were either worthy of understanding him or an idiot. (He fell in love with the word "idiot" after discovering Dostoevsky.)His (delusionally) self-styled, sublime perspective is Dionysian (free-spirited); but the "god" (himself) rarely, if ever, deigns to cite specifics, to expound on his contempt for the German character and culture, or anything else. He's the oddest philosopher I've ever read.He does not elaborate on any particular incident with Richard Wagner that drew his ire (other than deciding that he'd had enough of him after receiving a signed copy of Parsifal from Wagner--a stupid book, in Nietzsche's opinion), but he does tell us generally why he came to hate him (that is, for his antisemitism and for promoting "névrose nationale"--national neurosis). He responded by sending Wagner a copy of his latest book, signed, of course, which contained his railings against antisemites, of which Wagner was a leading one. The break was thence complete.Thus Spoke Zarathustra is Nietzsche's grand philosophy, metaphorically spoken. (I couldn't help thinking that it's probably a better read in the original German.) A compendium of awkward, choppy aphorisms, some astute, most obscure, many complete nonsense. Some seem to be indirect attacks on his detractors: "As I lay asleep, a sheep ate of the ivy wreath on my brow--ate it and said, 'Zarathustra is no longer a scholar.' Said it and strutted away proudly. A child told it to me." (Part 2, On Scholars.)Nietzsche hated Christianity and saw it as the bane of human existence. (I wonder if he'd redirect his ire against muslims if he could see the "global intifada" being perpetrated against the Western world today.) "On Old and New Tablets" (Zarathustra, Part 3) is his quintessential diatribe against Christianity, in aphorisms--but not the first or last.He was grossly ignorant on many perspectives and, often, his knowledge of history.Beyond Good And Evil is eminently more readable than Zarathustra (as is every other book, actually!). See, for instance, sections 211 to 213. He makes very interesting points and gets more personal.And as Kaufmann said, this is clearly the work of a very lonely man. In fact, as the years progress, in succeeding works, Nietzsche gets more personal and less inhibited.The mad philosopher sometimes nails it with uncommon incision: "Leibniz and Kant--these two greatest brake shoes of intellectual integrity in Europe!" Excellent. But of course, Nietzsche hated all things German and prominent Germans (despite being one)--Bismarck, the Kaiser (Wilhelm II), "1848", their "Wars of Liberation" (against Napoleon), their "great crimes committed against culture, "the damnable German antisemitism", their "poisonous boil of national neurosis", Kant, Luther, and, of course, Wagner. He also deeply resented the unpopularity of his books, but was prescient in that his real fame and success would be posthumous.Nietzsche assigned different, sometimes opposite, meanings to words. For this reason (needless to say), he's difficult to understand. Most of the time he seems to be employing sarcasm, but other times the alt-meanings are Nietzsche coinages, and he often doesn't provide much context. Familiarity with his philosophy and biography enhance understanding, but with Nietzsche, it can also breed contempt. I'm all for inside jokes (albeit with close friends) and the occasional grandiose philosophizing, but with Nietzsche, it's all he does.I think that by the time Nietzsche wrote Ecce Homo (which means "Behold the man" - John 19:5), he was thoroughly insane. His abounding narcissism has reached a fever pitch, yet the "god" (his self-description, p 772) allowed himself "the leisure of walking along the Po river", on the Seventh Day, before he resumed his work on teaching us mere mortals how to live. (psshht. Move!) (And, no embellishment here, either.) He needed to--because "in all seriousness: nobody before me knew the right way."(I realize that most of my criticism here is from Ecce Homo, but it's freshest in my mind, and does exemplify Nietzsche's attitude throughout his texts.)Nietzsche collapsed on the street in early January 1889 and shortly thereafter went insane. After the collapse but before he went silent, he wrote a few letters, which are included at the end of The Portable N. (Recall: "When Zarathustra had said these words he became silent, like one who has not yet said his last word; long he weighed his staff in his hand, doubtfully.") They are sad exhibitions of his madness, but in my honest opinion, not very much more dissociated from reality than most elsewhere throughout his texts! And, as quoted above, he eerily seemed to know his own fate.Walter Kaufmann was clearly a preeminent Nietzsche scholar, but one should exercise caution in his notes and commentary where political overtones are concerned. Any scholar who's as well-read and familiar with Marx, Engels, Sartre, and Hegel warrants it--especially where he groups Marx and Nietzsche together as two philosophers who "have usually been misrepresented as if they had boasted that THEY [italics] had stood everything on its head." Doesn't exactly inspire confidence in his scholarship. If Kaufmann didn't believe that Marx stood everything on its head, he was ignorant of the whole of human history, or just perhaps "more subversive and evil", and mendacious, than he'd want us to believe. He also says that Marx "corrected Hegel"; it was his attempt to "put things right-side up." In my view Kaufmann reveals himself unequivocally as a Marxist (fn, p 770), but only after the reader's read 99% of the book and his commentary. Again--politically speaking--User Beware.A postscript and suggestion: Since I chose to read all of this Nietzsche together (about 1500 pages' worth), I needed some levity. Molière did very nicely as a concurrent read and cool balance.
