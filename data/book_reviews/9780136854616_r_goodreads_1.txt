Another book (in that old Twentieth Century Views series originally published by Prentice-Hall) that I've been carrying around for half a century. I turned to it for help decades ago, but now it seems most interesting as a record for how reading (and my reading) has changed. Pound doesn't seem nearly as important now. Even though I still get great pleasure puzzling over moments in the Cantos, it seems more like a game, rather than the necessity it seemed once. Pound, even to someone like me who spent years working on him, seems most important now for the gifts he gave to modernism rather than for his own work. And even when I enjoyed these essays, they no longer seemed entirely important.

But they were helpful. It was good to be reminded of the essays by Yeats, William and Eliot, and about how important Pound was to them. Central critics of Pound who overwhelmed me when I was very young with their erudition--Leavis, Kenner, and Rosenthal--seem much more approachable now, and sometimes seem kind of facile. Is that because I've internalized what they have had to say?

The other critics, who make up the bulk of the collection, have mostly disappeared. They did bring back the controversies, and added helpful comments on the poems occasionally. The only essay that completely excited me was the one by Earl Miner--"Pound, Haiku, and the Image." He starts out by noting that wonderful poetry has sometimes arisen from only partial understanding of other texts, in this case poor translations of Japanese and Chinese poems. And he quotes Pound--"The 'one image poem" is a form of super-position, that is to say it is one idea set on top of another." And many other quotes. Pound got from Asian examples ideas about poetry that he was already thinking about. Those traditions gave him spectacular examples.

So it was good to go back to this book and read it all the way through. I think one would have to have a history with Pound and his poems to turn to this book, but if that were the case, it might be rewarding.
