the queen of taboos and psychosexual dread!!!! did it before the other girls tried doing it better (and failed). approached it with a curiosity that renders the deeply unsettling rich and complex, rather than the edgy-for-edginess'-sake register that's become so commonplace in anglophone litfic (particularly the subset following disaffected sex-obsessed women). a collection that doesn't feel like it focalizes or venerates men to the exclusion of women, in spite of the heavy presence of men on the page. if you read an emma cline collection, then try this, you'll never want to go back, because it quickly becomes obvious this is the blueprint all the girls are working off of. everything else pales in comparison.

gaitskill stages her interpersonal encounters out of what seems to be a genuine fascination with the how and why of our 'unseemly' habits or inclinations. her work isn't purely aesthetic. it isn't the product of commodity fetishism, where you can tell every image or article of clothing is meant to signal a certain contemporary class affiliation—and one the writer hopes will buoy their book as a stylish status-affirming 'product.'

i don't think i enjoyed this collection as much as , but i really fell in love with gaitskill's gesturing this time around, where before i'd found her gravitation towards describing bodies and their every minute movement comical. i get it now, and i appreciate the skill it requires to make symbols on the page become muscles and ligaments and joints, to convincingly fill out a conversation in a way that enhances our understanding of character rather than stunting or inhibiting it. she's a masterful study. i can imagine her taking notes while sitting in public spaces, that's how precise her gesturing is.

i especially loved the last section of this collection, where we got a rare and extended first person romp. i get why gaitskill is a lover of third person narratives (it's her strength), but the change of pace at the end was a welcome one. really enjoyed this (and i continue to love her delillo-esque prose, in all its psychological abstractions + knifepoint precision).
