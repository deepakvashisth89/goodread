Initially published in 1899, McTeague is timeless; it is just as relevant today as it was when it was first published.

On its surface, McTeague is a gripping wonder-of-a story about broken people attempting to improve their stations in life while the undercurrents gloriously challenge The American Dream. What good does “making it” if only to live a life of destitution and suffering to amass a small mountain of gold that can’t be carried to the next world?

Norris’s characters are vivid, memorable, and captivating. And – oh – such a surprise ending! This book will be remembered long after the last page.

Shortly before his death, F. Scott Fitzgerald wrote to his friend, Edmund Wilson. Fitzgerald accused John Steinbeck of stealing “a whole scene” in . Fitzgerald then included a marked copy of McTeague in support of his accusation.

Plagiarism? Them’s fightin’ words.

Fitzgerald marked passages where Zerkow requests that Maria tell him about her family’s glory years, specifically about a gold dinner service with over 100 pieces.

In Of Mice and Men, Lennie also requests that George repeat their goal: to own their own place where Lennie can raise rabbits.

“Go on, go on, go on,” cried Zerkow, drawing his chair closer and shutting his eyes. (McTeague, p. 127)

“Go on now, George!” (Of Mice and Men, p. 29)

“Come again, come again,” he croaked. “Don’t wait till you’ve got junk; come any time you feel like it, and tell me more about the plate.” (McTeague, p. 48)

Lennie pleaded, “Come on, George. Tell me. Please, George. Like you done before.” (Of Mice and Men, p. 28)

Should every author be accused of plagiarism for repeating a character’s quest?
