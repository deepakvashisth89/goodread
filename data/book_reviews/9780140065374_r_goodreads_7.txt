'Death of an Old Goat' is slow reading, but has moments of cleverness that make it at least worth reading to the end.The novel is a bit of an Aussie x Agatha Christie mystery in tone and style. It's obviously set in Armidale and at the University of New England, which was formed in 1954, 20 years before the novel's release. The fictional setting is 'Drummondale' (read; Armidale) names after David Henry Drummond who was elected in 1920 to represent the Northern Tablelands in the NSW Legislative Assembly. He was a firm believer in education, and one of his major achievements was establishing the Armidale Teachers' College which laid the foundations for his successful campaign in 1934 to start New England University College, a 'northern university' with affiliation with the prestigious University of Sydney.Although Barnard claims 'All the characters in this book are entirely fictitious, and no reference to any living person or actual institution is intended,' his novel is notable in Armidale circles as a thinly veiled reflection of the town coming to grips with the fledgling university in the early 1970s.The plot revolves around the visit of Professor Belville-Smith, a decrepit English scholar who wants to be anywhere but delivering a lecture at Drummondale. He delivers the lecture, the subject of which somehow transforms from Gaskell to Austen, and then attends a tense party trying to make small talk with the petty faculty who despise each other. The next day, he is found brutally murdered in his hotel room, and the local inspector is put on the case. Inspector Royle is a philanderer, who keeps weekly 'appointments' with the powerful wives of the town, sleeping with them for a mutual exchange of favours and information. Royle is a corrupt character, but we want him to succeed with his investigation so we know who killed Belville-Smith ad why - and also why the men of the town are leaving home after midnight nearly every night and returning to their wives in the early hours of the morning.The murder-mystery was passable, though I think the red-herrings with the female principal of the Ladies' College and the female housemaster at Daisy Bates College went on a bit too long and didn’t add much to the narrative.What I found most interesting was the novel as an example of an Australian campus novel that explores the tense relationship between the university and the townspeople. The university is often viewed as an elitist institution, and its students as entitled brats undeserving of education in an Agricultural town such as Armidale. I imagine the tension between research that benefits agriculture and humanities studies rings true for several regional towns in Australia. Barnard explores only the humanities department, but raises interesting questions about the significance of ongoing humanities scholarship and whether it is possible for the university and the university town (keeping in mind is was an Agricultural town before a university town) can ever get along. In my view, they can - and if we're thinking about the real UNE today, they do - but Barnard's novel explores a time when perhaps they didn’t, and imagines a possible future where there is always a divide between those who work the land and those who work in education.Not a thrilling read, but one that raises thoughtful questions at least.
