When this book was first written, not only were there no climate change deniers, there wasn't much appreciation for climate change. There was more worry about running out of carbon to release than about its long term effects.

Today's pressing climate concerns -- and our indifference to it -- meant that I couldn't focus on the other main themes of this novel.

It's written in a workmanlike fashion, with the devastating effects as clear then as this month in Louisiana:

"She wandered across a swamp and stumbled into ditches. . . She saw grotesque sights -- trees whose leaves had been stripped off . . . Dead birds whose feathers had been plucked clean by the wind. Shoes and clothing draped whimsically over twisted telephone poles, as if someone had put them out to dry, even though there wasn't a trace of their owners, or their owners' houses. She saw a sailboat with a bathtub in it and an upside down truck with a kitchen stove balanced upright on top of it. There was even a coffee pot on the stove. She saw the bodies of a drowned baby, a drowned wirehaired terrier, and a drowned man in a raincoat and hip boots with a broken umbrella clutched in his hand. But she didn't see anything alive" (p. 125).
