A good friend recommended Redburn as superior to Moby Dick! First off, I completely disagree. Even as a book about life at sea, I find Moby Dick superior. That book wrestles with the human condition much more thoroughly. That said, I did find Redburn an interesting read.The main character Wellingborough Redburn is a naïf who lets his romance about the sea convince him to embark on this most rugged and perilous career. He is portrayed as wearing a red hunting coat meant for fancy men. It becomes threadbare and ratty making him stick out as a clownish figure. However, during the crossing to England he becomes quite adept at the high-wire tasks of sailing that it quells the ridicule of other sailors.There are a couple strange sections in the novel such as Redburn presenting his father’s outdated travel guide to Liverpool as another character in the book. This section interrupts the book’s narrative in a way that prefigures Moby Dick with its entire chapters devoted to some aspect of whaling. However, in Redburn the purpose of such an intrusion feels out of place in a book primarily about sailors and shipping.The most moving and intriguing aspect of the novel is Redburn’s friendship with Harry Bolton, a pseudo aristocrat, and similarly situated in the worldas Redburn but merely the English equivalent. It’s Harry that takes Redburn to London and it is this section with the boys living it up in apparently a high priced bordello that feels oddly specific and yet didn’t land for me as fitting in with the the themes and actions of the rest of the novel.While Harry does acquire competence at some sailing tasks, he can’t overcome his fear of heights to be effective doing the delicate work high upon the ship’s mast. I see him as Redburn’s doppelgänger and a cautionary tale of how close Redburn gets to Harry’s watery demise.
