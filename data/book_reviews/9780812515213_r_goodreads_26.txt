The second time I have read this, I think, and it is a book very much of the moment in which it was written -- the early 90s, duringone of the earlier waves of books (especially for teen girls) with dark, sexy, mysterious vampires with hearts of gold. This book sits very uncomfortably with those, I think on purpose, for while Agyar is clearly a vampire (and yes, the word is never mentioned in the text, it is a neat trick but not the best thing about the book) and we are in his perspective, it is clear that Brust knows that he is writing a monster. Does the monster's golden heart -- his falling in love with a beautiful young dancer passionate for life redeem the fact that he is a thing that rapes, tortures, and murders to meet his own needs? Certainly not, says Brust, although he says it in a balanced way, walking the line of making Agyar sympathetic enough that one keeps reading, but never to let that sympathy overwhelm the truth about who he is and what he does. I think even without the frame story one could see it, but I like the frame as well, and the way that it wraps around -- Susan is so easily the manic pixie dream girl, but the frame makes it clear that is Agyar's view, not Brust's. This is not the story of a monster who is redeemed by his love for a woman, it is the story of a monster who falls in love, destroys several lives, feels some shame and regret, and then dies before he has to deal with the consequences of having destroyed the life of the woman he loves. Susan may be able to put together a new existence for herself, or may not, but she would not have had to in the first place if Agyar had not involved himself with her, and as he lied throughout, there was no point in which she was able to consent to what he eventually did.I like it very much, but it is bleak -- and it should be, a good corrective to the idea that someone who does terrible things is all right because they also love.
