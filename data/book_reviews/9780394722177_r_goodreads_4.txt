Unique and excellent, if a bit repetitive.

The wonderful thing about this book, unlike most of the ones on Nixon criminality, is it isn’t completely overshadowed by the inevitability of Watergate. We get a pretty good coverage of the earlier years of the administration, almost contemporaneous in their beat-by-beat care. (Unlike Nixonland, though, this isn’t a broader political-cultural history.)

Schell also hammers, throughout, the separation of image and substance during the Nixon administration and its creeping side-effects. He gradually plays out—and at the end, wholly restates—a unified theory of how since nuclear weapons could never be used, the question of bluster and credibility became paramount, first in foreign affairs and with Vietnam growing to consume domestic politics as well.

This overall thesis is what makes the book so interesting and repetitive at times, but it added a lot to my understanding of these years. Reminds me of Wills’ Bomb Power on nuclear policy consuming the executive branch, Craig & Logevall’s book on foreign policy as an expression of domestic pressures, and James William Gibson’s The Perfect War on the self-perpetuating logic of the Vietnam War. (Schell would also continue the thread in his anti-nuclear books, famously in 1982’s The Fate of the Earth.)

It also, especially for a book written in 1975, is stunningly prophetic and reminiscent of the current Trump administration—from reorganizations to consolidate executive branch power, to the casual double-speak and a press willing to go along with it. Like, maybe even more present-feeling than Perlstein’s work on the era, which is a wild achievement.

Biggest downside is, being written in 1975, Schell didn’t have access to the information that’s come out since, especially for non-Vietnam subterfuge earlier in the Cold War. He pushes Kennedy-Johnson-LBJ as a novel, if inevitable, aberration from mostly-honest policymaking; the record of Truman and Eisenhower aiding anti-communists abroad makes that a harder sell.

Still great, though! I can’t believe this is out of print, but hopefully NYRB Classics will pick it up down the road.
