This is a fine collection of short stories and novellas by one of my favorite and most respected authors.
My favorite aspect of this collection is that so many of the stories deal with his youth, a perspective not contained in many of his novels outside of The Big Rock Candy Mountain. Some of the youth stories here involved the Mason family and were then integrated into The Big Rock Candy Mountain. A few of the adult-centered stories also deserve special mention, my favorite being:

A 50-page mini-novella story about Joe Allston, the 66-year-old, retired from being a New York literary agent and living in the California hilly and spacious Los Gatos burbs. It begins with a descriptively beautiful passage about the birds inhabiting the tree-filled hills surrounding his house and separate office facility. It ends with a beautifully descriptive passage of a drive home from a neighbor’s party through heavy fog-filled early morning/late night air and the next morning’s anticipated bird performance.
In between these beautiful narrative bookends is a wonderful depiction of an elegant and large dinner party thrown by some wealthy neighbors to showcase the wife’s piano protegee. Stegner’s depiction of the conversation and attendees’ personalities are so revealing. There is insightful chatter about artists, agents and critics. Through Joe, Stegner makes psychological insights and sociological observations that are just so on point and so exquisite that, at the end, I could not help but say to myself, “Boy, this one was good.” It was even more satisfying as I was not that into the story at first but when I eventually did get in to its rhythm, I became thoroughly engrossed. Boy, this one was good.

An intended highlight of the book are the three cowboy stories set in early 1900s Saskatchewan, “Genesis,” “The Wolfer” and “Carrion Spring.” “Genesis” and “Carrion Spring” were later included in “Wolf Willow,” Stegner’s combined history, memoir, and fictional account of the Cypress Hills country of Saskatchewan where he spent some of his youth.

“Genesis” is an 80-page novella about an English tenderfoot becoming a full-fletched cowboy by assisting in the recovery of a herd of cattle during the blizzards and weather atrocities of the winter of 1906, which Stegner reports ”drove the open range cattle outfits out of business.” Stegner refers to it as an ‘ordeal story” in the vein of Joseph Conrad’s ordeal novellas. His son, Page mentions that many readers consider “Genesis“ one of the best pieces of fiction Stegner ever wrote.”
Unfortunately for me, my reading felt almost like an ordeal as I struggled through the bleak blizzardy descriptions of the wintry cattle wrangling, had trouble with visualization and at times found myself just reading words. “The Wolfer” was better, though, and I very much enjoyed “Carrion Springs” where the cowboy dynamics are enhanced by Stegner’s always probative insights into marital couple dynamics. My inability to appreciate “Genesis” as the gripping and compelling read many consider it to be is my major disappointment with the book.

Another one is the length and organization of the book, which I found similar to short story collections of Katherine Ann Porter and John Cheever, both of which were Pulitzer Prize winners. Both of those are collections seemingly put together to seek a Pulitzer Prize for life-long work rather than any specific work. In fact, Porter’s collection is really a compilation of three previous collections that hadn’t been considered Pulitzer worthy.
I think they are all too lengthy and lack the cohesiveness needed to rank as a truly great short story collection. Those tend to be shorter and more limited in scope. Two such favorites for me include Winesburg, Ohio and The Dubliners, both, coincidentally, titled after the place that act as the stories’ connections, as the stories are vignettes involving various residents.
Despite not having the brevity and internal cohesiveness of these two classic collections, this collection did have some real gems. And Stegner’s exquisite writing is a pleasure as always. Overall, though, I rate it as a 4-star book.
