I wanted to like this book more than I actually did. While I think that Goldstein’s prose and character-building are both excellent (two consistent features across her writings), they unfortunately don’t save this work.Very briefly, Late-Summer Passion is about a rigid professor of philosophy and her infatuation with an undergraduate student over the course of a summer where the former tutors the latter on Spinoza’s “Ethics.”The professor, Eva, is 47, and the student, Michael, is 20.I think it’s possible to write an age-gap infatuation (or even a relationship) in *a right* way. But I don’t think Goldstein succeeds here. Despite the constant references to Michael as a boy (and the very sudden switch to man-boy at the end of the book which reads as underdeveloped), Goldstein never actually writes the characters in a way that allows them to properly engage with the fruits of their age-dynamic. And this a shame because it makes the infatuation seem one-dimensional and flat, which makes it easier to dismiss (which I don’t believe to be Goldstein’s intention).Along similar lines, I think Goldstein’s plot is developed unevenly. While the backstory given to Eva, particularly her father, is developed in great detail, her present story is not developed enough. Particularly, in certain interactions she has with Michael, such as his having access to her phone number and home address which seems like an absurd thing for an undergraduate with little connection to his professor prior to the present summer to have. Events in the present plot seem to happen for the convenience of page count as opposed to happening out of organic plot development. And worst of all, the past and the present don’t seem to connect in the way that Goldstein clearly intends. Perhaps that’s my own failing as a reader with little background in psychoanalysis and the significance of Wagner in the Third Reich.Nevertheless, despite being a big fan of Goldstein, I’m sorry to say, this book fell flat for me.
