I'll be honest, I've read a lot about apartheid. History books, political analysis, the kind of things that give you the facts but leave you feeling like you're observing from a safe distance. So when I picked up Inside Apartheid, I wasn't sure what new perspective it could possibly offer. But Janet Levine doesn't give you facts. She gives you her life.And that changes everything.What struck me most about this book isn't just the horror of apartheid, though that's there and it's unflinching. It's the complexity of what it means to be born into a system that benefits you while every fiber of your being knows it's wrong. Levine is a white South African. She could have looked away. She could have accepted the comfort that the system offered her and gone about her life. But she didn't. And that choice, that refusal to be complicit, is what makes this book so powerful.Here's the part that really got me, and I haven't seen anyone talk about this specifically. Levine doesn't present herself as a hero. She's honest about her own contradictions, her own fears, her own moments of doubt. She writes about the tension between wanting to fight for justice and wanting to keep herself and her family safe. She writes about the exhaustion of constantly pushing against a system that seems immovable. And that honesty, that willingness to show the messiness of activism, made her feel so human to me.She was a student activist, a journalist, a member of the Johannesburg City Council. She tried to fight apartheid from the inside. And eventually, she had to make the painful decision to leave. To go into exile. That's not a clean, triumphant story. That's a story about someone who gave everything she had and still had to accept that she couldn't fix it all on her own.But what stayed with me is that she didn't give up. Even in exile, she kept telling the story. She kept bearing witness. She kept making sure that the world would remember what happened in South Africa, and that those who suffered would not be forgotten.This book made me think about what I would do in her position. Would I have the courage to risk everything? Would I be able to look at an unjust system and say no, even when it cost me my home, my safety, my future? I don't know. And that uncertainty, that uncomfortable question, is still sitting with me.I'm recommending this book because it's not just history. It's a mirror. It asks us to look at ourselves and ask what we're doing, right now, to stand against the injustices we see. It's a reminder that doing the right thing is almost never easy, and that sometimes the most important choice we can make is to refuse to look away.Janet, if you ever read this, thank you for telling your story with such honesty and courage. Thank you for not pretending to be perfect, for showing us that activism is messy and exhausting and often feels like it's not enough. And thank you for reminding us that even when we can't change everything, we can still refuse to be silent.
