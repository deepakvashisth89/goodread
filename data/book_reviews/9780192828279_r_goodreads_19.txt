I only read the two stories I had not previously come across in other collections. Those were:(1898) “The Man Who Could Work Miracles.” Middlebrow Englishman who… just like the title says. It’s funny how within one day he sends a bully to hell and then runs home and hides. The story becomes a satire on the “benevolence” of social engineering. The artist Wells always clashed with the political Wells in this way. But let me ask: which one is the reason we remember him? We get a typically vivid picture of what it would be like if the Earth abruptly stopped spinning. The ironic ending is cute.(1905) “The Empire of the Ants.” A creature feature/colonial horror story about an invasive species of intelligent ant. Right up my alley. I think The Birds took a lot from it. There’s a touch of Conrad in Wells’ description of the Amazonian immensities, and an irony in the gunboat, with its one big gun utterly useless against this enemy. I also liked Wells’ description of ant movements: their “mechanical fussiness.”The 1911 collection includes an intro by the author where he says:“…no short story of mine of the slightest merit is excluded from this volume.”Well, that is complete bullshit. He excluded many, many stories of great merit—some of my favorites!He goes on: “The bulk of the fifty or sixty tales from which this present three-and-thirty have been chosen dates from the last century.”By now Wells had almost entirely given up writing short stories. This is a shame not only because they are perhaps his best work but also because they’re some of the best short stories ever written. He himself seems to regret it, and reminisces (as if it were many years ago rather than just a few) about the inspired productivity of that time in his life:“Little men in canoes upon sunlit oceans would come floating out of nothingness, incubating the eggs of prehistoric monsters unawares; violent conflicts would break out amidst the flower-beds of suburban gardens; I would discover I was peering into remote and mysterious worlds ruled by an order logical indeed but other than our common sanity.”The ‘90s, we are told, were an especially good time for short stories. There were Kipling, and JM Barrie’s Window in Thrums. Then there were “Stevenson, Frank Harris; Max Beerbohm wrote at least one perfect one, ‘The Happy Hypocrite’; Henry James pursued his wonderful and inimitable bent; and among other names that occur to me, like a mixed handful of jewels drawn from a bag, are George Street, Morley Roberts, George Gissing, Ella d'Arcy, Murray Gilchrist, E. Nesbit, Stephen Crane, Joseph Conrad, Edwin Pugh, Jerome K. Jerome, Kenneth Graham, Arthur Morrison, Marriott Watson, George Moore, Grant Allen, George Egerton, Henry Uarland, Pett Ridge, W. W. Jacobs (who alone seems inexhaustible).”Wells says the following decade saw a decline in quality, and that only Conrad would better himself. He attributes the flourishing to something I didn’t expect: literary criticism.“People talked about [short stories] tremendously, compared them, and ranked them. That was the thing that mattered.” Even more than money, what drove writers was being part of this lively discussion. At the same time, Wells blames critics for making a once free art form academic, inhibiting writers with their rules. “It crept into one's mind, a distress as vague and inexpugnable as a sea fog on a spring morning, and presently one shivered and wanted to go indoors... It is the absurd fate of the imaginative writer that he should be thus sensitive to atmospheric conditions.”Wells sympathizes with critics whomay have been tired and looking for excuses to discount works. However, “this world is not for the weary.” For his part, he defines the short story as “the jolly art of making something very bright and moving” that takes between 15 to 50 minutes to read aloud.___________________Marginalia:There are still a few uncollected stories I haven’t read, and some later ones, but for now, and for what it’s worth, this would be my A Treasury of Short Stories of HG Wells:The Country of the BlindThe Lord of the DynamosMiss Winchelsea's HeartThe Treasure in the ForestA Slip Under the MicroscopeThe Sea RaidersThe Obliterated ManJimmy Goggles the GodMr. Ledbetter's VacationAepyornis IslandThe Crystal EggThe Flying ManMr. Brisher's TreasurePollock and the Porroh ManThe Truth About PyecraftThe Door in the WallA Deal in Ostriches
