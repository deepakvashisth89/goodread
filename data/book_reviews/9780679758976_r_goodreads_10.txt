The premise was promising and the opening chapters held potential, but things quickly unraveled. The characters were poorly written, the action scenes bordered on the ridiculous, and the romance was downright cringe-worthy. I’ve read a Robin Cook novel with a similar theme, and while I don’t remember much of it, I’m fairly certain he didn’t turn it into a sleaze-fest.One small positive: the mention of a search engine was quite futuristic for a book published in 1996 (Google was founded in 1998), which shows some forward-thinking.But the characters—especially Jeannie—were a major letdown. Just because she has a PhD, is six feet tall, looks like a model, and plays tennis like a pro doesn’t make her a strong character. She’s self-centered, morally inconsistent, and irrational. She uses her search program to dig into the lives of twins raised apart and claims it’s not an invasion of privacy, yet freaks out when she uncovers unethical experiments. Her treatment of her friends is equally troubling—manipulative and dismissive.The rest of the cast behaves just as irrationally. A man accused of rape is released on bail and immediately professes love to a woman he barely knows? Really?The final third of the book, where the protagonists uncover the conspiracy, is laughable.Classified Pentagon data? Just walk in on a Saturday night and grab it. Getting hold some witnesses? Just flip through a directory and call them at midnight and they all turn to your press conference without any questions.If this is your first Ken Follett book, please don’t judge his work by this one. His historical fiction (Pillars of the Earth, for example) is in a completely different league. This book, sadly, is not.
