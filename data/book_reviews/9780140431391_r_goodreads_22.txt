Romolastarts off slowly, with a Proem featuring a Shade who had passed away seven years before 1492. The Shade hovers above Florence, observing the commotion from afar, but the narrator warns him to not go down. In those seven years, the narrator says, Florence has changed greatly, though he acknowledges that, yes, the “great river-courses which have shaped the lives of men have hardly changed; and those other streams, the life-currents that ebb and flow in human hearts, pulsate to the same great needs, the same great loves and terrors.” However, the politics, the speech, the scholarship, the trade, “would sound as a riddle in [his] ears.”Set in 1492, George Eliot’sRomolaexplores a politically tumultuous, power-hungry Florence post death of Lorenzo de’Medici. Against this backdrop, Eliot weaves in the fictitious story of Tito Melema, a young Greek scholar whose tragic story emblematizes what happens to a self-interested life pursuing pleasure and avoiding pain. In repeatedly neglecting the right, or moral, course of action, Tito allows “poison” to work in him “by degrees.” His is the tragedy of good intent turned into baseness. He is a sort of foil to his wife, Romola, the fair-haired beauty of Florence who seeks to do good after Savonarola adjures her. “My daughter,” he tells her, “every bond of your life is a debt: the right lies in the payment of that debt; it can lie nowhere else. In vain will you wander over the earth; you will be wandering for ever away from the right.” Contrastively, Tito has “borrowed from the terrible usurer Falsehood, and the loan had mounted and mounted with the years, till he belonged to the usurer, body and soul.”Though the first few chapters describe streets, fountains and familial histories in excruciatingly minute details, Eliot’s knowledge of Florence is, admittedly, impressive. As Susan M. Greenstein notes, “The novel was painstakingly worked up, as if Romola’s world would not come to life without endless details of Florentine dress and vernacular. For a while she was even troubled by the thought that her characters, who had finally begun to live for her, ‘ought to be speaking Italian instead of English’” (“The Question of Vocation: From Romola to Middlemarch” 496). Despite this, or because of it, all of Eliot’s characters come to life in an arresting, exciting way.When Baldassare cries out, “Lost, lost, lost!”, whose heart does not cry out for justice with him? Whose heart does not seize with the pain of all that is lost before him? Who does not want to exclaim before the intelligentsia seated around the table,but he whose mind has darkened and closed now with grief at one point had cultivated the wild garden of scholarship and knowledge!Though the ending was a little too didactic, I thoroughly enjoyed the read and was invested in the lives of Romola, Baldassare and Tessa among a few others. I groaned in annoyance, I huffed in frustration and at one point, I even slammed the book shut because I could not handle what I anticipated would happen to my favorite, beloved characters. 4.5 stars.
