My latest re-read of this fascinating biography and I enjoyed it as much as the first time. From a childhood on a very traditional ranch, where horses are 'broken' using the mantra that if you don't get them first, they'll get you, he felt that there must be a better way. Just watching horses, both in the wild and those around him on the ranch, he discovered a 'language' of eye contact, body positioning and non-verbal cues that could be used to have a discussion with the animal instead. This way of thinking was not accepted by a lot of people around him, forcing a break with his family and the struggle to make it on his own. A bit of luck along the way, finding the right horses and showing them at the right time, meant that succeed he did. Culminating with him bursting onto the scene in the last 1980's, endorsed with no lesser person that the Queen of England herself.

This book is less about Monty's methods and more about the man. His story is one of hard work and hard knocks - from a child stunt rider, to rodeo performer to racehorse trainer. In some ways, it's an enviable life, getting to spend so much time with the animals that he loves, but there's a lot of people along the way that I'm glad I would never have to put up with.

As a teenager, I remember his entrance onto the horsey scene and watched more than one of his demonstrations (my copy of this books was signed at one!). He always came across as a funny and knowledgeable man, happy to highlight his mistakes but willing to show off his successes - he was definitely the start of a movement towards finding different ways to handle and train horses, even if it's hard to imagine how harshly they could be treated in the American 'wild west'.

A fascinating and interesting read, with lots of food for thought.
