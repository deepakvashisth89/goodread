In the first two essays of this collection, Tolkien examines criticism from other scholars on Beowulf and provides some notes about the difficulties of translating Beowulf from the Anglo-Saxon language. He complains that too many scholars view Beowulf as just a historic document giving us crucial information about kings and battles of the past, but they overlook it's literary significance as a poem. Tolkien is actually kind of comedically harsh with these critics, saying things like "That's all nonsense." and "...the seekers after history must beware lest the glamour of Poesis [poetry] overcome them."Tolkien spends a lot of time defending the use of mythical monsters (Grendel and his mother, and the dragon) in the poem. Some critics seem to think that the inclusion of monsters demotes Beowulf to a mere folk tale, but Tolkien says again and again that the monsters are essential to Beowulf's deep themes. Tolkien scorns critics who look down on fairy tales as an "unprofitable theme: as if Milton had recounted the story of Jack and the Beanstalk in noble verse." He praises the "high tone and sense of dignity" and the "weighty words, lofty sentiment" in Beowulf as giving a great significance and meaning to the story.First of all, tales about dragons are actually very rare among Northern Europe's ancient tales. The only other "significant" dragon is Fafnir, which is mentioned in the poem as a comparison to the heroic deeds of Beowulf. Folk tales CAN be serious literature just as the great Greek myths, but Tolkien warns that we shouldn't spoil the story with too much analysis. "Unless he [the student of Beowulf] is careful and speaks in parables, he will kill what he is studying by vivisection."Secondly, the deep themes of life and death and courage save Beowulf from being a weak fairy tale about monsters. Tolkien spends a lot of time talking about the "paradox of defeat inevitable" - the theme of "man at war with the hostile world and his inevitable overthrow" in death. Tolkien says that Beowulf "is a man, and that, for him and many, is sufficient tragedy."Tolkien scoffs at critics who complain that the plot doesn't "advance". Tolkien asserts that the plot is not meant to advance but to function as bookends to Beowulf's life, showing the balance between the two greatest moments of his life, his rising and setting as a hero. Beowulf's story is ultimately a tragedy, a dirge for a man at the end of his life, celebrating his first heroic achievements in his youth, and then eulogizing his final battle.There is also a balance in the foes that Beowulf faces, first a monstrous Grendel and then the fearsome dragon. He fights against a humanoid ogre monster and then a soulless animal. The first is hand-to-hand combat, and the second is sword against fire. The plot moves from a lesser foe to a greater foe, because the man who conquered Grendel could only be defeated by some massively powerful creature. By battling supernatural monsters, Beowulf transcends beyond the petty wars of little kings of men, and the themes transcend as well. The Bible says "we do not fight against flesh and blood, but against principalities and powers" in the spiritual world, just as Beowulf fights against supernatural evil. He is larger than life, and his enemies are too.I also found it interesting that the poem mentions man's life in "middle-earth" just as Tolkien uses the term for his fantasy world. This is that Norse geography of the world as an inaccessible sky and the unknown depths of the earth and man living on the crust of the world ringed around with the shoreless sea.Tolkien also talks about the fusion of Norse and Christian beliefs and poetic thought. The author of Beowulf was writing at a time of change for his culture, looking back at the ancient stories and also looking to the Christian framework of morality. Beowulf holds these two cultures and belief systems on the edge, blending them beautifully and meaningfully, taking the best from both. From the Norse, we get the extreme courage and "unyielding will" in the face of unrelenting evil. From the Christians, we see a hope in the power of God to strengthen the heroes for battle, as Beowulf believes his great strength is a supernatural gift from God to enable him to defeat Grendel. But nothing is specifically mentioned of Christ or of the old Norse gods. It is purposefully kept mysterious and vague, and somehow becomes even more powerful in its imagery.One of the main themes of Beowulf is that man and all his deeds will eventually die away. The pagan setting makes this theme even more powerful because of what Tolkien calls "the shadow of despair" and the "worth of defeated valour." With the monsters in the center of the story and our hero facing death, Beowulf surpasses the myths of the Greeks with their immortal gods who are above the mortal world. Zeus and the Greek demigods are safe in their immortality, but Beowulf must grapple with the fear of death and face the problem of all mankind. Tolkien says that "the wages of heroism is death." Tolkien even compares Beowulf's heroism to heroes in the Bible such as David, the "shepherd patriarchs and kings of Israel".In the second essay, Tolkien examines the difficulties of translating from Anglo-Saxon. He talks about how hard it is to pin down the connotations of certain Anglo-Saxon words that carry a weight of meaning that is lost in English. One of those is 'eacen' which can be translated as 'mighty', 'stalwart', or 'huge' but really carries the meaning of 'enlarged' in a supernatural or extended manner with an "addition of power", just as Beowulf's strength is supernaturally enlarged by God.Tolkien talks about the compound words used in Anglo-Saxon sometimes feeling like riddles or enigmas to be solved. 'Swan-road' is a word for the sea, and 'flood-timber' or 'swimming-timber' means a ship. But if you just translate the word as 'ship' or 'sea', you would lose the full meaning that is being expressed.Tolkien insists that any good translation must be in keeping with the tone and solemnity of the original. You can't use certain modern words in translation, because they lack the proper tone. You wouldn't say 'hitting' or 'whacking', when you ought to use 'striking' or 'smiting'. You wouldn't say 'talk' or 'chat', when you ought to say 'discourse' or 'speech'. But you also shouldn't use words that are too archaic or obscure, because that wouldn't reach your modern audience at all. There must be a careful balance to find exactly the right words. It's easy to see how Tolkien thought very carefully about each word he used in his Lord of the Rings, and the exact expression and tone that he wanted to evoke for each scene.There is a large section about the original metre of Beowulf in Anglo-Saxon. It does not rely on rhyme and rhythm in the way that modern poetry does, but builds opposites of words with strongly stressed syllables that rise and fall in tone. And the poetry uses alliteration at the beginning of words rather than rhyming at the ends of words. It's really beautiful, and Tolkien explains it very cleverly with charts that show where the accented stresses lie for each syllable. These "lifts and dips" in the metre of the language create what Tolkien calls a "tough builder's work of true stone" that is "strong to stand."After reading about Tolkien's deep love for this beautiful poem, I can't wait to read Tolkien's own translation of Beowulf!
