Excellent overview of 500 years of seeking the Northwest Passage. Naturally, for a book of just over 200 pages, it is impossible to go into great depth on any one expedition, save Franklin and the subsequent searches for Franklin, which got about 1/3 of the book. That's fair, since much of the NW passage was figured out in that frantic period of 1849-59 when everyone realized something had gone very wrong with HMS Erebus and HMS Terror and crew.

This volume was written before both ships were rediscovered in 2016 and 2014, respectively. Fortunately, there are many other good, modern historians that cover the Passage, particularly Ken McGoogan and Glyn Williams, who will bring you up to speed. Michael Palin's book Erebus is also excellent.

I very much appreciated the inclusion of maps, although not every expedition was given a track on the maps, and not every map named the islands, bays, and straits mentioned in text, making it more difficult to follow. I would have suggested a master map on the inside cover and flyleaf for reference, plus making the interior maps more detailed.

These are minor quibbles on the whole, for a book that covers a tremendous amount of territory and people. It also gives a fair shake to Inuit and their knowledge, although it elides through any real discussion of how these expeditions affected their culture. Also, we know a lot more now about how climate change is negatively affecting the Arctic than when this book was published. There is no real discussion about the natural history of this space.

The contemporary paintings and photos included are excellent, as are photos of period equipment and even a piece of almost 200-year-old edible hard tack! It's a fun read, and for someone's first foray into Northwest Passage literature, it would be an ideal place to start.
