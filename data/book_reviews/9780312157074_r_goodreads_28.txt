Remember that intriguing, original story that Jane Austen wrote about the redemption of a spoiled snob? Some one who won the sympathies of many readers--even in the modern audience--and decided to use her resources to be a better person? Well, let's take all that's interesting in that story and suck it out to make something cliched, judgementally dull, and too revisionist for *this* Austen fangirl.

First,
the characterization of Emma. Ms. Aiken has completely forgotten that the only reason we frown on Emma at all is because we had access to her interior life and thoughts and (via Mr. Knightley) the history of her feeble education and undisciplined childhood. Those who don't know any of this (excepting the grotesque Eltons) Emma, including Jane, who accepts Emma's overture of friendship once her fate has been settled and even apologizes to Emma for being such a coldfish earlier:



Remember, Emma is a charitable, lively, fun, hospitable person who happens to think she knows everything, because that's what her elders taught her to think as a child. I can forgive and even pity anyone for errors that the people who knew better instilled in him/her as a child, and that's why Miss Jane Austen made her the protagonist. The only time the veneer drops is when Emma gets carried away with Frank--someone too similar to Emma and hence a very bad influence. That brings me to my second qualm.

Jane doesn't love Frank?! Getting the details of their romance was the only thing that made me want to read this book. It was also the only unconventional element in Jane's poor princess ascendant story. She was the hard-working, reservered, realistic, and rational person (alliteration was completely unintentional there :D), and Frank was the fun, but flighty, flirt (might as well sink myself here).

We all know relationships where a woman of substance marries a man who doesn't seem to deserve her. It's often for one of two reasons. 1, he carries her out of herself and helps her appreciate the simple and immediate things in life. He brings her balance, and unless he is extremely irresponsible or immoral, he also brings her great happiness. (Frank is not a bad person, nor is he profligate in a way that Jane can't handle.) 2, or women of substance marry flimsy men, because men of substance are uninterested.

And that brings me to the other thing that I didn't like! Jane has a crush on Knightley. Ugh. I know that it often happens in real life that women get stuck with a lesser man, because they are too similar to the men they actually admire. Their connection with such men is often very strong, but they can never get past the friendzone, because these men don't want a copy of themselves to love. That's too boring (see all the novels where boy ignores said girl only to brutally learn his lesson later, , , etc. :D).

I personally feel it's better to make the best of our natures, and other people's, rather than trying to redirect them. Jane explicitly states the same near the end:



This to me says that she embraced Frank, virtues and vices, as a whole on their engagement and only became irritable because of the misery of it being a secret.

If you like simply being in the Austeniverse and the integrity of characters and ingenuity of plot are dispensable, then sure, this could be enjoyable. Pax!
