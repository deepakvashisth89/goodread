In Slouching Towards Gomorrah, Robert H. Bork offers a polemical yet systematic critique of what he perceives as the moral, cultural, and institutional decay of late twentieth-century American society. Written by a prominent jurist and conservative intellectual, the book extends themes familiar from Bork’s legal writings—especially his originalist constitutional philosophy—into the broader terrain of culture, ethics, and politics. Bork’s central claim is that modern liberalism, understood less as a coherent political doctrine than as a diffuse cultural ethos, has eroded traditional moral norms and thereby undermined the foundations of democratic self-government.


The book is organized around two principal forces that Bork identifies as agents of decline: radical egalitarianism and modern liberal individualism. According to Bork, radical egalitarianism seeks not merely equality before the law but equality of outcomes, leading to resentment of excellence, hostility toward authority, and the politicization of cultural institutions. This impulse, he argues, distorts public policy and jurisprudence, particularly through judicial decisions that elevate group rights and identity-based claims over democratic deliberation. Liberal individualism, by contrast, dissolves shared moral standards in the name of personal autonomy, producing a culture of relativism in which individual desire replaces moral restraint as the primary guide to action.


Bork’s analysis ranges across a wide array of cultural phenomena, including popular entertainment, education, sexual ethics, and family life. He is particularly concerned with what he sees as the normalization of vulgarity and violence in mass media, which he believes reflects and reinforces moral degradation. Universities, in his account, have abandoned their role as transmitters of cultural and intellectual inheritance, becoming instead sites of ideological conformity hostile to Western tradition. The cumulative effect of these developments, Bork contends, is a weakening of the moral preconditions necessary for liberty itself, since a free society ultimately depends on self-restraint and shared norms.


A distinctive feature of Slouching Towards Gomorrah is its attempt to link cultural decline to constitutional interpretation. Bork argues that the judiciary, influenced by liberal individualism, has overstepped its legitimate authority by discovering rights not grounded in the Constitution’s original meaning. Decisions concerning privacy, sexuality, and expression are presented as emblematic of a broader judicial usurpation that removes contested moral questions from democratic resolution. In this respect, the book serves as a cultural companion to Bork’s legal originalism, illustrating how his jurisprudential commitments are rooted in a broader moral vision.


The strengths of the book lie in its clarity of argument and its willingness to articulate a comprehensive moral critique at a time when such claims are often avoided in academic discourse. Bork writes in a direct and accessible style, and his synthesis of legal, cultural, and political analysis gives the work a coherence that distinguishes it from more narrowly focused conservative critiques. Moreover, the book captures an important strand of late twentieth-century American conservatism, making it a valuable document for understanding ideological debates of the period.


Nevertheless, the book is not without significant limitations. Bork’s analysis frequently relies on broad generalizations and anecdotal evidence, particularly in his discussion of popular culture and social behavior. Critics have noted that his depiction of liberalism tends toward caricature, underestimating its internal diversity and its historical contributions to civil rights and democratic inclusion. Furthermore, Bork’s normative framework presumes a relatively stable and homogeneous moral tradition, leaving unresolved questions about how such a tradition can be defined or sustained in a pluralistic society without coercion.


Slouching Towards Gomorrah is a provocative and influential work that extends Robert Bork’s legal philosophy into a sweeping cultural diagnosis. While its arguments may persuade readers already sympathetic to its conservative premises more than skeptics, the book remains a significant intervention in debates about morality, liberalism, and constitutional government in the United States. As an expression of cultural conservatism at the end of the twentieth century, it continues to serve as both a touchstone and a lightning rod in discussions of American decline and renewal.
