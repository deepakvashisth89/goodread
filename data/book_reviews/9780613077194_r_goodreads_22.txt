“‘I’m not going to die, Christopher,’ I said gently.
He sat back down on his stool and lowered his head. ‘People are so fragile,’ he muttered.
I felt such love for him In that moment. It swept over me like a warm wave. Yet - and this was odd - his comment also bothered me. I didn’t know why.
‘This - people - is not so fragile,’ I said, meeting his gaze. He held my eyes a moment before lowering his head as if embarrassed.”

“The Eternal Enemy” is an early 90s horror/thriller story by Christopher pike wherein a girl accidentally buys a VCR that can record future news casts, with devastating results. While some of the tech might initially sound dated, with a cool chic protagonist that was fun in the first person to read, this one was way better than I had expected. And, with little te’-ta-te’s like this below, the older tech is not a problem:

“He looked at me shrewdly. ‘But it doesn’t excite you, does it?’
‘I wouldn’t say that. I’d say, rather, that it worries me.’
‘What? Why? Specifically?’
‘I worry that we’ll become so dependent on computers that we’ll cease to think for ourselves. We’ll lose our humanity.”

I found this little interchange pretty prophetic and very relevant today, some 30+ years after this book was written, in the big AI boom
If the 2020s of the present. While Pike may have his critics, there can be no denying his intellectual, philosophical chops that contributed, along side his humor and wit, to the sparkle of his stories. Noteworthy is also his pragmstism, which can be observed on the conclusion of the above dialogue:

“Christopher nodded. ‘Possibly a machine could become smart, but we’d always know where the off switch was.’”

Good to know, and hey - still relevant!

Later on in the narrative there were some trippy cool existential SCI-FI bits. Here’s a passage from one of those parts I really liked:

“But who was this I?
Empty space, of course. The supreme element. Immortal.
But anything else?
The question that only the most delicate of feelings could answer.”

And I liked the grim tone on this one:

“His eyes took on a glassy state for a moment, as if he were looking into the future right then. Whatever he had seen, I knew then it had been to much for him.
‘At first what I saw for us, all of us, was tragic,’ he said. ‘But that didn’t surprise me. Man has always been his own worst enemy.’ He coughed. ‘But maybe I should have left things alone. There are tragedies and there are tragedies.’
‘What did you do?’
‘Sara,’ he said, and there was a note, faint but nevertheless chilling, of despair in his voice. Now, finally, he was scaring me. I clasped his old hands and felt how they trembled.
‘What did you do?’ I asked softly.
‘I wanted to make things better,’ he said”

And this here is a keynote passage:

“‘. . . They will work ceaselessly to end every conceivable form of human suffering. There’s just one problem with their desire.’
‘To be human is to suffer’ I said.
My grandfather looked at me with fresh appreciation. ‘You knew that without being told. I only knew that after suffering through everything I have. You amaze me, Sara.’
‘More than Sam and Susan?’ I asked, and I hated the bitterness that entered my voice. I couldn’t help it.’”

There were some other passages I wanted to share, but I fear I’ve lost them. In any event, I’d say … mmm….the first half to two thirds is a pretty typical Pike horror/thriller as far as the lead up, but it eventually sways over to the SCI-FI/speculative fiction for a good blend, reminding me most of something I’d find in Weird Tales or one of those sorts of collections from the 20s-50s, and as a fan of those I mean that as a compliment …it would have fit in great in one of those, having a similar story pattern to those sorts of stories. While I enjoyed that a lot, as it’s my cup of tea, I suspect some Pike readers may have been less keen on the overarching plot and tone of the last half-third. What I particularly liked about this, however, is classic weird tales stories usually had a young male protagonist, as that was there target audience, so flipping it to a female protagonist was really cool.

While I don’t know that I would mark this is one of Pikes top ten works, to me it was a solid piece with both some SCI-FI and philosophical chomps. Probably a 3.5 I’m going to round it up to 4 VCRs.
