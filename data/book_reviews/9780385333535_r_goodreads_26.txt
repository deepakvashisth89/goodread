Frannie Hardy is nothing if not reliable. She’s always on time to pick up her kids from school. So the one day she’s late, her husband, attorney Dismas Hardy, realizes something’s wrong. He reclaims his kids, then sets out to reclaim Frannie if he can.Running through the book is the troubling theme of how far the Hardy marriage had drifted. They weren’t in trouble of real collapse, but he had used the need to keep everyone happy and fed as an excuse to avoid trips to and from school and other kinds of involvement that would have kept the marriage stronger. Infidelity isn’t the only way to throw deadly water on the coals of marital passion, and you see that troubling theme throughout the book.An over-ambitious assistant district attorney has sent Frannie to jail because she refused to answer a question he posed when she appeared before a grand jury. Ron Beaumont, a Mr. Mom who took his two kids to and from school daily and who’d become friendly with Frannie, had told her something in confidence and asked her not to divulge it. It wasn’t a friendship that included any commingling of body parts or even a body parts display—the two weren’t sexually involved. She took him seriously. When the cocky attorney demanded answers, she refused to provide them.The case is complicated by the fact that Ron’s sister Bree is dead at the hand of a murderer. The cops are sure it’s Ron, but Dismas Hardy believes otherwise—eventually.I thought this was one of the weaker books in the series. It felt easily a hundred pages too long, and it was anemic on courtroom drama, which is why I read the series.Hardy digs deep into what Beaumont didn’t want disclosed and, in the process, uncovers inappropriate secrets about fuel additives and San Francisco’s water supply with a gubernatorial election just days away.Before the book ends, Bree isn’t the only dead character, and the Hardy house burns to the ground thanks to arsonists who are convinced Dismas is getting too close to the truth.
