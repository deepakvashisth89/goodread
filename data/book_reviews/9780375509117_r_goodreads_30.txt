W. E. B. Du Bois’s ‘The Souls of Black Folk’ provided the intellectual and cultural foundation that helped inspire the Harlem Renaissance two decades later. In fourteen essays and under two hundred pages, this remarkable sociologist and historian speaks plainly, but also poetically, about the challenges of being black in America. Published in 1903, it is here for the first time that the term “double consciousness” is introduced, a concept previously referred to as divided identity by Frederik Douglass.It is a peculiar sensation, this double-consciousness, this sense of always looking at one’s self through the eyes of others, of measuring one’s soul by the tape of a world that looks on in amused contempt and pity. One ever feels his twoness, –an American, a Negro; two souls, two thoughts, two unreconciled strivings; two warring ideals in one dark body, whose dogged strength alone keeps it from being torn asunder.Not since I read “Caste: the origin of our discontents” by Isabel Wilkerson in 2020 have I read anything so powerful and forceful, tragic and hopeful, unabashedly thoughtful and provoking. Du Bois will stir your emotions through facts and anecdote, through the sensibility of his poetic phrasing, and through the knowledge that we are all bound together as brothers and sisters in this world. He undertakes to show Reconstruction and Jim Crow in its horrible destructiveness.Here at a stroke of the pen was erected a government of millions of men,--and not ordinary men either, but black men emasculated by a peculiarly complete system of slavery, centuries old; and now, suddenly, violently, they come into a new birthright, at a time of war and passion, in the midst of the stricken and embittered population of their former masters.Du Bois takes on the arguments of Booker T. Washington for his program of industrial education, for acceptance of segregation and disenfranchisement. Du Bois said that three things must be had: The right to vote, civic equality, and education according to ability. Du Bois wanted to go much further than Washington. He said,“We must strive for the rights which the world accords to men.”As a young man educated at Fisk University, Du Bois went to the hills of Tennessee to find a school where he could teach.Got a teacher? Yes.” So I walked on and on– horses were too expensive–until I had wandered beyond railways, beyond stage lines, to a land of “varmints” and rattlesnakes, where the coming of a stranger was an event, and men lived and died in the shadow of one blue hill.At last, Du Bois finds a job at a little school. He also finds Josie, twenty years old, with a longing to learn. Josie and her brothers and sisters were the first to attend the little Tennessee school. Du Bois writes thatshe studied doggedly.He loves Josie’s family for trying to be clean and decent and for knowing how badly they need schooling. Deftly, Du Bois draws word sketches of other children and families. In its isolation, this community was its own world. Even so, Du Bois writes, they were aware of their lack of opportunities, of how “the veil” (segregation) kept them from a better life. When Du Bois returns ten years later, it is to an accounting of how the people have survived, or have not, and how they have changed. For the descriptive passages and how Du Bois took me into their world, this was my favorite chapter.Although there is much sadness in ‘The Souls of Black Folk,’ two chapters stood out to me for particular sadness.One is ‘Of the Passing of the First-Born,’ which tells of the birth and death of Du Bois’s baby boy. Here, Du Bois addresses Death:A perfect life was his, all joy and love, with tears to make it brighter,--sweet as a summer’s day beside the Housatonic. The world loved him; the women kissed his curls, the men looked gravely into his wonderful eyes, and the children hovered and fluttered about him… He knew no color-line.”The other reads almost like an allegory and is titled ‘Of the Coming of John.’ In it, a college education leaves John Jones deeply unhappy upon returning to the South. Now, he recognizes the level of evil in subjugation under white men's rule. The mayor also has a son named John, who has returned from college. The two Johns had been playmates as children. Circumstance will bring the two Johns to a tragic reckoning. As I read, I had a sense of futility, of fighting against the inevitable, and thinking it does not have to be this way.We must find our way. America must find her way. It’s not by whitewashing the past or opposing diversity. In the fields, monocrops are the most vulnerable. It is diversified agriculture that stands the best chance and reaps the richest harvest. Diversity has been one of America’s greatest strengths. It still is.
