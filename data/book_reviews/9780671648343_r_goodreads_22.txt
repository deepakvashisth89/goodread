This is a fascinating and often very moving account of Sacks' famous experiments using a new-at-the-time dopamine medication to treat the "sleeping" victims of the encephalitis lethargica outbreak in the early 20th century. Excoriated at the time for cautioning on L-DOPA's "miraculous" nature, Sacks takes the reader on a case-by-case study detailing the medicine's effects on patients who had been virtually catatonic/comatose for decades. These case studies are startling, weird, disturbing, and in the end frustrating. Most of the patients had horrible side effects after their brief "awakenings", and only a few persisted on L-DOPA with varying degrees of success. Some of Sacks' observations and findings will startle even now in our more "caring" age: Parkinsonian effects can be countered by less clinical care and environments; surrounding the supposedly catatonic with what they love (including and probably most especially family) can have as much of an effect as medication.Perhaps the big takeaway here from Sacks' post-case study chapters is on how little we still understand about how the individual brain actually works and how treating things as pathologies might be less valuable than understanding the person undergoing them.
