In writing this book Thomas Carlyle set the tone that would come to dominate all future accounts in reference to the story of the French revolution: he focused on the time when terror was the order of the day, and he did so with the express purpose of putting a lid on the social forces which sought to change the existing power-structures and damp down the ambitions of those that who would vitalize the power of the people to change the conditions of their lives. In my opinion, Carlyle's historical account, which is not so much really a flowing narrative but a highlighting of the archetypes of the mythological consciousness of the 18th century peasant-oriented consciousness of the early reading public, has remained in print for nearly two hundred years because the lesson it instills is one that has continually sought to be reinforced by the powers that be; specifically, that any deviation from the agreed-upon structures of power in bourgeois society is a movement in the direction of anarchy, and anarchy, Carlyle says, is basically a suicidal move that brings about social destruction and, inevitably, self-destruction as well, which Carlyle suggests is the most appropriate ending for and a revenge upon the assumption of power by the common people. Reading this book, it struck me as significant that a credible, progressive Jewish writer like Simon Schama, who also wrote on French revolutionary history, almost literally transcribed the story of Charlotte Corday's murder of the revolutionary writer Jean-Paul Marat from the earlier history of Carlyle's. Both Carlyle and Schama insinuate that this event may be seen as a result of the people's dissatisfaction with the revolutionary scheme to remake society according to a more appropriate conception of justice and equality that, they suggest in their books, cannot be obtained with driving the general public mad with power. In my opinion, this basic trend in dissuading people from assuming the political power that they have won through the long history of struggle, including global wars and, locally, general disobedience to power in its many forms, continues today in the structure of left and right, which makes up the binary positions and the pretensions of individual choice as being the only directions available for political and social assertiveness: either the ideology of the Republicans, whose ultimate end-point is Nazism, or the ideology of the Democrats, whose ultimate end is Communism, is the basic choice given to young people as early as secondary school and this is the anchoring schematic of our political system whose number one concern is the stability and permanence of the voting public's willingness to give their assent for the reinforcement of the prerogatives of the ruling powers. In a sense, Carlyle's book, too, is a permanent symbol of the fixity of our political world, so I guess it has earned its three stars.
