“The old man smiled. 'I shall not die of a cold, my son. I shall die of having lived.’”I decided to read something vastly different than the comics and thrillers I had been reading and saw a friend’s rave review of this book so reread what many have seen as Willa Cather’s masterpiece, The Death of the Archbishop (1927), historical fiction based on the story of two priests that moved to establish the New Mexican diocese in the late nineteenth-century, Jean-Baptiste Lamy and Joseph Projectus Machebeuf, renamed Father Latour, the Apostolic Vicar to New Mexico and the younger but devoted Father Valillant, (whose goal was to build a great cathedral--as they are often built--in the midst of great poverty, and of course by those very poor people).In 1851 Father Jean Marie Latour comes to New Mexico, and dies in 1988. The novel would seem to have various purposes for the Protestant prairie writer depicting two Roman Catholics playing their small part in the "expansionist" (or colonialist) drive to conquer the West. I think it’s an elegantly simple hymn of praise, a tribute to faith , even as it attempts to show the complexities of bringing one cultural view to a place with another view of the world, the Spanish view that meets the Mexican and Indigenous residents there. Cather admits the faults of this enterprise even as she affirms the sweetness and goodness of LaTour, who in her story tried to navigate this terrain with grace and thoughtfulness.La Tour sees the Indigenous respect for the environment even as tries to develop a European style garden: “They ravaged neither the rivers nor the forest, and if they irrigated, they took as little water as would serve their needs. The land and all that it bore they treated with consideration; not attempting to improve it, they never desecrated it.”Cather (in 1927, note) constructs a subtle, gentle, and perhaps somewhat romanticized view of LaTour’s missionary work even as the settlers remake this area in some ways into a westernized image. LaTour respects native peoples, their language and culture. But others there, exemplified by among others, Kit Carson, are more brutal representatives of settler colonialism, of course, killing and pillaging the area. The atrocities at Acoma are mentioned here very clearly, too. More aligned with Cormac McCarthy’s Blood Meridian, let’s say. I appreciated Cather's honesty based on her research into the history.The language of this book is often shimmering, incandescent, reminding me of The Bridge at Sun Luis Rey by Wilder and some of Steinbeck’s moral allegorical novels such as The Pearl. Lovely language throughout that honors the land and spirit and interdependence and cultural diversity even as it advances an impossible goal. Whose land is it?“The sky was as full of motion and change as the desert beneath it was monotonous and still, — and there was so much sky, more than at sea, more than anywhere else in the world. The plain was there, under one’s feet, but what one saw when one looked about was that brilliant blue world of stinging air and moving cloud. Even the mountains were mere ant-hills under it. Elsewhere the sky is the roof of the world; but here the earth was the floor of the sky. The landscape one longed for when one was away, the thing all about one, the world one actually lived in, was the sky, the sky!”In the end, the good archbishop is buried in the Cathedral Basilica of St. Francis in Santa Fe, which many readers will read with great sorrow, but it's a mixed message for me at best. I’m an agnostic, raised in the Dutch (Calvinist) Christian Reformed Church in western Michigan. I have a “complicated” history with missionaries, having family members I love who did this work in parts of the world, and my church raised money for missionary work in the Philippines as I grew up. Still, I read it with an eye to Cather’s lovely prose, and an appreciation of her attempt to balance spiritual commitment with an admission of moral compromise.
