There are only four starters (suspects) and any one of them, given the right circumstances, might have committed the crime. That knocks out forcibly the element of surprise. Nevertheless there should be, I think, an equal interest attached to four persons, each of whom has committed murder and is capable of committing further murders. The deduction must, therefore, be entirely psychological, but it is none the less interesting for that, because when all is said and done it is the mind of the murderer that is of supreme interest.
- Agatha Christie on Cards on the Table

Agatha Christie set herself another challenge with this particular premise of finding the murderer among four people with equal opportunity, means and similar motives - and has succeeded with flying colors! There are few, if not none, who can produce such remarkable locked room mysteries.

Poirot relies on the psychological analysis of the four suspects, gleaned through their memories and strategy at the card game Bridge, to find the murderer. He does this despite the similar motives all four of them had, looking beyond evidence and testimonials - satisfied only when the murder could be reconciled to the psychology of the murderer.

He is supported by competent officials here, even if one of them is predominantly racist which was a bit jarring (the times!). But this is balanced by the tongue-in-cheek inclusion of the murder mystery author Ariadne who swears by feminine intuition (The Body in the Library being one of her books!). Christie's writing is laudable as always for its economy while still painting vivid pictures.
