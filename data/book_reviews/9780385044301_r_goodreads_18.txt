This is not the kind of thing I would ever have read were it not on Boxall’s List. At first, there were things that put me off: the cast of characters is vast and at times confusing, and some are not that well developed and hence not easy to keep track of. Set in the French African colonies, this is a time and place unfamiliar and strange to me, and there is a strongly anti-colonialist and anti-capitalist message. Having endured turgid agitprop from the likes of Gorky and Brecht I am disposed to an allergic reaction to anything similar – but although this is of course propaganda, it is by no means turgid. In fact it is a very powerful novel which challenged me and eventually seduced me.The strengths of it are that the many characters contain all sorts and conditions. Yes, there are heroic revolutionaries and evil racist Frenchmen, but there are also a myriad of nuanced characters in between, in conflict with one another and themselves, and illustrating a fascinating mixture of motive, character and choice. The novel’s most powerful part is the “March of the Women” – an extraordinarily moving event which in the hands of a lesser novelist would be simply a sentimentalised heroic narrative: but Sembene strips it of sentimentalism and makes it into something genuinely and movingly heroic.The events of the novel, though fictionalised, reflect conditions in Francophone Africa in 1948. The preface, written by the author in 1962, says “Since then, Africa has made progress.” And indeed it has, though the tragedy of Africa is that progress has been uneven and – for a very large number of Africans – has failed to deliver them from poverty, violence and injustice. Why this should be is a complex matter beyond the subject of this review. But, although not sharing Sembene’s political vision and prescription, I do share his moral perspective and his longing for justice and freedom for all, and I can understand why this novel is such a valuable part of the African literary canon.
