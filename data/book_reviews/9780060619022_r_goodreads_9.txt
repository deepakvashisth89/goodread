I found too much of this vague, airless, and hermetic. I guess coming to this after reviewing Kathleen Norris' chatty, eclectic, uneven but engaging combinations of memoir, Benedictine and Protestant rumination, and literary critique, De Waal's carefully phrased, reticent, and politely earnest style is far more British proper than U.S. of A. brash. I'd commented that her commentary on the Rule, A Life-Giving Way, didn't bowl me over, but at least she doggedly trudged through each of the 72 chapters with an attempt, at times inevitably strained, to align our contemporary (as of the 1980s) sensibility with the instructions and contexts of the early monastic movement as it transitioned from the Middle East into Italy. Her take benefited from being grounded in a response to a challenging set of rules and counsel. She tried to link its sometimes remote regulations about liturgy, discipline, and practical management to today's audience, encouraging her readers to find connections...however tenuous.But in this follow-up, she's throat-clearing too much. Jean Vanier, poets, the Celtic Christian verses, and various Trappists and Benedictines get frequently quoted. Some, if the last category, work fine. Yet, others thud like the standard fare of the tumultuous period in which I was raised, half a century and more ago now, when Christians felt they had to adopt the human potential movement's style and appeal. De Waal emulates the C. of E. modulated, affirmative, and broad minded public voice of those brought up well, determined to convey views to those much resembling herself, but not so often me...if not you. Trouble is, it tends to come off as if vetted by a committee of Oxbridge parish vicars.However, beneath sometimes platitudes or self-help bromides, apt truth filters through. I found her discussions of contradictions of the cross (vertical-horizontal) echoing CS Lewis or GK Chesterton if without the donnish polish of the former or the maddening chiasmus of the latter. A few mentions of the vows of stability and "conversatio morum" stuck, and on p. 51 a passage leapt out to me about unwanted change which "spoke" to my current state of disordered life powerfully if coincidentally.There's not as much about the Rule of St Benedict as other readers have asserted. It begins with its lively Prologue, but Life-Giving Way pairs (in its later ed.) the entire source text with her explication or elaboration as rumination. You won't get a strong sense of the Rule if you rely only on this slim collection of brief and reflective chapters. Her afterwords direct you to the standard 1980 translation, although I prefer Terrence Kardong's Benedict's Rule as an American alternative, with valuable notes.I'd suggest Christopher Jamison's Finding Sanctuary (2006) and a participant on the BBC series set as The Monastery, Nicholas Buxton, in his Tantalus + the Pelican (2009), as better suited to our online era. (Recently reviewed by me.) Her own book feels musty and self-explanatory, too caught up in an insular concentration on topics too generalized in her presentation (retreat talks for like-minded Anglican visitors to English sites). This rarely got out of warming up, and I wanted higher octane.
