In “Snow White and Rose Red,” fifth in the Matthew Hope series, McBain explores the Kafka-esque world of civil commitment. “Sarah Whittaker was nuttier than a fruitcake. Or so I’d been told by an attorney named Mark Ritter, who’d handled the involuntary commitment on behalf of Sarah’s mother,” Hope narrates. He meets her at the institution and finds her to be competent, enchanting, smart, funny, and not the least nuttier than a box of crackerjacks.From Sarah’s point of view, one night she was reading in bed when her mother, her mother’s psychiatrist friend, and a police officer whisk her out of bed in her nightgown and handcuff her and take her off to the facility. Her delusions, Hope hears from the doctors when he interviews them, is that her mother conspired with the psychiatrists to lock her up and get her hands on Sarah’s $650,000 inheritance from her deceased father. Hope doesn’t know what to make of it. Sarah is charming and clearly sane, but such a vast conspiracy hardly makes sense, particularly when mom inherited a cool billion.It’s a scary proposition though that someone can arrange for you to be locked up perhaps forever and your alleged delusions are explained as the conspiracy to lock you up. How do you get out if all the experts agree to your astonishment that you are delusional and, to put it plainly nuts.A second case is separately pursued by Hope’s police detective buddy Morrie Bloom investigates a corpse found floating in the swamp, badly decomposed after six to nine months, with a bullet hole in the throat and her tongue missing.In this one, McBain’s Florida feels more like Willeford’s Florida in his Hoke Moseley novels, full of nuts and crazies and offbeat characters.
