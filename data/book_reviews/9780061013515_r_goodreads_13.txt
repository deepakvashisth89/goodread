This is an engaging true story about six fishermen who were lost at sea during a terrible storm back in 1991, and I especially appreciated the research and care that went into telling the tale. The author made a point of sticking to facts and hearing the remembrances of friends and family of the men who died, while trying to surmise what really happened out on the missing ship.I had watched the movie "The Perfect Storm" before starting this audiobook, and was surprised to learn that some of the incredible scenes featured in the film were based on real-life incidents, including a Coast Guard rescue crew that had to abandon their helicopter during the height of the storm, and also an accident involving one of the fishermen getting his hand hooked on the line and being dragged off the boat and out to sea.This thrilling adventure reminded me of another true tale about a doomed fishing vessel, Nathaniel Philbrick's "In the Heart of the Sea," and I highly recommend both works for those interested in the subject.Favorite Quotes"How do men act on a sinking ship? The people of Gloucester must willfully extract these men from their lives and banish them to another world."BackstoryI ended up reading "The Perfect Storm" because of an intriguing passage in another book by Sebastian Junger, "In My Time of Dying." Sebastian opened that book with a story of a surfing accident he had when he was a young man, and he came very close to drowning because of huge wave:The wave wanted me and was going to keep thrashing me in the darkness until I finally gave up and breathed in. What amazed me was how malevolent the whole thing seemed —Me? Why do you want me?I was young and had no idea the world killed people so casually. Oddly, I remembered that there was a pile of dirty dishes in my sink that someone was going to have to deal with. Files and notes for a book I hoped to write on my desk ...Sebastian was able to make his way to the surface and back to shore. Collapsed on the sand, he imagined himself dead, and how that news would spread amongst his loved ones. His family would have to let themselves into his house, and there they would find a makeshift desk piled with research on every topic related to death at sea:I was writing about a swordfishing boat that had gone down with six men off the Grand Banks in 1991 and wanted to reconstruct their last days and hours and minutes as closely as possible. I didn't know any of the men, but through my research I'd gotten to know their siblings, their girlfriends, their mothers. The process eventually came to feel so intrusive and wrong that I started dreaming about them — that is to say, the men occasionally visited me while I slept. One dream was particularly vivid: I was walking along the beach where I surfed when I spotted them sitting in a circle in the sand. I hesitated because I was sure they were angry with me for writing the book, but they just waved me over to join them. Don't worry, they seemed to be saying. We've been expecting you.
