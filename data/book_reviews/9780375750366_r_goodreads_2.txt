Brian Magee used to be a wonderful populariser of Western philosophy in Britain in the 70s and 80s. I've met him first when I've read the book basedThe Great Philosophers: An Introduction to Western Philosophyon those forecasts. I was astonished by the lucidity and clarity of his explanations and discussions with other philosophers. Unfortunately, I do not think of anyone of the similar statue who replaced him in our time.This book is his intellectual autobiography. It is a sheer delight to read. He talks about the big questions he grappled with since his childhood and how he came to philosophy to help him with those. He also talks about his discoveries of Kant and Schopenhauer, the two of his major influences. His discussion of Kant's philosophy is especially enlightening. He also talks at length about his friendships with Russel and Karl Popper. He tells anecdotes from their life as well and ideas of those two. He considers the perceived lack of status of Popper in the modern pantheon as very discouraging. In general, the book is another great excurse into philosophy, especially its state in the 20th century. But this time it is coloured by his subjectivity and great wit. He does not consider analytic philosophy and philosophy of language as the main way forward. He is convinced analytic philosophy does not pose questions fundamental enough. But equally he is not impressed by contemporary continental philosophy as lacking the rigour. At the time of his writing, the middle 90s, he was convinced that there was noone who would move our understanding of metaphysics significantly forward. But he was not very upset: there were 2000 years between Plato and Kant.Apart from philosophy, he was a big music lover. He especially loved Wagner and Mahler. He has written at least two books about Wagner, both of them are still in print.Reading this book felt like having a lengthy elucidating conversation with a very wise older person. Due to him, I think I finally have a wide-angle picture how different parts are connected in the Western philosophy. It is a very eliminating read but little sad at the same way as his generation is almost out of our tangible grasp now. The culture of intelligent fruitful debate, the desire of beauty in knowledge seems to be disappearing with them as well.
