The story was good and relatable, but at some points it got long and tedious.

Silas is in no way a heroic character. He is not notably intelligent, nor is he courageous, nor unselfish. He is a product of Eliot's desire to arouse sympathy for ordinary and imperfect humanity going about its day-to-day business.
Silas changes greatly during the book. His original love and trust seemed crushed by the evils which befall him, but they finally return with even greater strength. The betrayal by William Dane costs Silas his faith in men, and the betrayal of the drawing of the lots takes his faith in a just God.
His faith is gone, and his gold is lost. But God has greater plans for this man, and by sending a child to his house, he finally finds solace.
The man who had become almost dead to the world finds joy through the love and innocence of a little girl. Silas has been robbed of his material gold but thinks that he has it returned to him symbolically in the form of the golden-haired Eppie.
He appears in the final pages as a man with new faith. His sufferings which he found ominous cruelty of God, shaped his maturity and inner strength. His faith is not based on unquestioned doctrine; rather, it deepens and survives despite doubts. His is no overwhelming triumph but a believable, human one.

Sometimes, we curse our fate and spit on God, feeling everything is against us. Yet, rejections are sometimes protections, and we, lonely and lost humans, are weak when it comes to keeping faith in our hearts. After all, faith is believing in the flickering light while walking in the dark and cold valley of despair.
