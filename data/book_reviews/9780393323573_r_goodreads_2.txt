In 2020 I read Andrea Barrett’s short-story collection Ship Fever and Other Stories. I thought it was fantastic. I picked it up because it won the 1996 National Book Award for Fiction. I loved it because in my experience the short stories were unique. Each story’s subject was somehow related to hard science: botany, biology, chemistry, mathematics, medicine, physics, etc. Although I was never great at any of these sciences, I enjoy them to whatever degree I can comprehend them, and I found that stories about these sciences grabbed my attention. These stories incorporate historical figures prominent in these sciences, mostly as minor characters, and are based on actual historical events, making them historical fiction (historical science fiction?; descriptive, but not accurate). The stories are also linked to each other through characters, relationships between characters, relationships across generations, and events. I enjoyed coming across these links; they made the collection bit of a puzzle, and each story became bigger than itself.

But this review is supposed to be about the short-story collection Servants of the Maps, and it is, because everything I loved about Ship Fever and Other Stories is present in Servants of the Map. In Servants of the Map there are not only links between some of the stories in this collection, but there are also links to stories in Ship Fever and Other Stories and to a novel by Barrett. Apparently, there is a third collection of stories, Natural History, that follows the same approach and is on my reading horizon.

The stories in Servants of the Map span the 19th and 20th centuries, so of course, the sciences addressed in the stories vary considerably. Barrett’s stories work so well because you meet people who are passionate about learning and who are seeking answers so that they can help others. Despite their aspirations, the characters in these stories are very human with flaws and troubles that make their efforts even more admirable. I strongly recommend Barrett’s short stories.
