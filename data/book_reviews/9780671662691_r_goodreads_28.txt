William Steig was a good painter and, in my judgment, a better writer. I don't love this particular book like I love Steig'sAmos and Boris, but it's still quite good. The author evokes the hopelessness of Sylvester's situation--"stone-dumb"--and by extending it a little longer than you'd expect on first reading he heightens the drama and therefore the satisfaction at Sylvester's second and better metamorphosis. My favorite aspects of Steig's writing are his love of the long sentence, his often mellifluous prose, and his use of advanced vocabulary--all of which issue from his fundamental judgment that children ought to be taken seriously. There is a merriment to his writing, to be sure, but it is "that kind (and it is, in fact, the merriest kind) which exists between people who have, from the outset, taken each other seriously" (C.S. Lewis).
