Washington's laconic, unsentimental account makes for generally unexciting reading; but this is a book that you read for the ideas, and they certainly deliver. I certainly don't agree with all of Washington's philosophy. Conservatives tend to glorify it too much (it's OK to be a little angry and passionate, right?) and Du Bois makes some excellent points in his responses (contained inThe Souls of Black Folk) that Washington is a public-school pragmatist as opposed to one who sits in the classical tradition of truth, beauty, and goodness. This is very fair. But I far favor Washington's thought over most modern discourse about race and think that it is worth a resurrection in modified fashion. After all, "vocational" training and the "dignity through labor" mindset is not at all incompatible with liberal learning, even if our culture would prefer to make it so. I like that towards the end, instead of describing all the great things he did, he quotes newspaper articles: a smart alternative of sounding snobbish in listing off all his personal achievements (although he isn't shy about spreading the word about his influence). However, this doesn't save those last few chapters from being essentially advertisements. One can easily skip them without remorse. For the aforementioned reasons, it gets 3 stars. Still, Washington is one of the great voices of virtue ethics in American history and a critical contributor to the black intellectual tradition, and this book is a thorough outline of an idealistic yet abundant vision for the prosperity of a moral community that has continually been severed by ugliness.
