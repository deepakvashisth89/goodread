Excellent in-depth study of five famous American cinematographers comprising of a detailed interview with each and a chronological listing of their films.

--- Arthur Miller .... was making shorts from 1908 and features, mainly with director George Fitzmaurice from 1915-1925; from 1932, a leading cinematographer for 20th. Century-Fox, winning Oscars for "How Green Was My Valley," "The Song of Bernadette" and "Anna and the King of Siam".
In 1950 he formed Horizon Pictures with Sam Spiegel and John Huston and was set to photograph "The African Queen" when illness forced his early retirement. He then worked tirelessly at the offices of the American Society of Cinematographers where he was a vice-president, administrator and historian. This book features the last interview he made prior to his death in 1970. *
Other notable features in which he was DOP include "The Mark of Zorro"/ "The Ox-Bow Incident"/ "The Keys of the Kingdom"/ "Dragonwyck"/ "The Razor's Edge"/ "The Gunfighter".

--- Hal Mohr .... an eleven-year-old when he experienced the San Francisco earthquake and fire, and as a youngster experimented with his Magic Lantern slides. When he entered the film industry, he worked on independent productions outside of Hollywood and for many years at various studios as a cinematographer but mainly at Universal where he won his second Oscar for "The Phantom of the Opera" (1943). His first Oscar was for "A Midsummer Night's Dream" shot at Warners in 1935.
Other notable credits include Stroheim's "The Wedding March"/ "The Jazz Singer"/ "The Front Page"/ "Captain Blood"/ "Watch on the Rhine"/ "The Wild One" and his last as "photographic consultant" on Hitchcock's "Topaz".

--- Harold (Hal) Rosson .... was one of many Rosson's in the film business, and after working mainly at Paramount during the silent era (e.g., von Sternberg's 'The Docks of New York') became
chief glamour photographer at MGM during the studio's golden years (he was briefly married to Jean Harlow), photographing such films as "Red Dust"/ "Treasure Island"/ "Captains Courageous"/ "The Wizard of Oz"/ "30 Seconds Over Tokyo"/ "On the Town"/ "The Asphalt Jungle"/ "The Red Badge of Courage" and "Singin' in the Rain".

---- Lucien Ballard .... his distinguished career as cinematographer began in the mid-1930's at Columbia photographing three von Sternberg films; very much a journeyman craftsman working on a mixture of Westerns, B-films and lesser-known features with notable credits including "The Lodger"/ "A Kiss Before Dying"/ "Buchanan Rides Alone"/ "The Sons of Katie Elder"/ "Hour of the Gun"/ "True Grit" and "The Hawaiians". Came to major prominence via his collaboration with Sam Peckinpah on the films "Ride the High Country"/ "The Wild Bunch"/ "The Ballad of Cable Hogue"/ "Junior Bonner" and "The Getaway".

---- Conrad Hall .... the most contemporary of the artists interviewed. At the time this book was printed, only 18 credits were listed but it was obvious from this short list that Hall would become one of the greatest modern cinematographers. He had already won an Oscar for his brilliant work on "Butch Cassidy and the Sundance Kid" and in this interview session he discusses his early career as a wildcat filmmaker and Disney nature photographer plus other early credits including "The Wild Seed"/ "The Professionals"/ "Cool Hand Luke"/ "In Cold Blood" and "Hell in the Pacific".

What is noticeable about these interviews is the extensive nature of them. Leonard Maltin compiled these originally in 1971 in his book 'Behind the Camera: The Cinematographer's Art' and this large-scale Dover edition was published in 1978. No simple Q and A session with brief responses. Maltin asks leading questions and allows his subjects to tell their story in remarkably detailed fashion, providing not only a fascinating insight into their lives and craft, but also giving the reader an absorbing history of the film industry in general.

The first section of the book contains a near 50-page introduction "A Survey of Hollywood Cinematography" a superb fact filled summary of the fabulous artists behind the camera in the halcyon days of Hollywood.
And as with all these early Leonard Maltin tomes, you know you are getting an informative, entertaining and loving treatment of film history.
The 105 photographs, most half page stills, featuring these rarely seen artists on the set are priceless.
Review based on Dover Publication, 1978. 140 pages.

* See also "One Reel a Week" a fascinating dual biography and history of early filmmaking by pioneer studio founder and producer Fred J. Balshofer and cinematographer Arthur C. Miller (Univ. of California Press: Berkeley & Los Angeles, 1967)
