The macabre premise of this book was the reason I was initially intrigued enough to want to read it, a rather grim, nightmarish concept that made me curious to see just how unpleasant it might be -- a man trapped inside his own body. And sure enough, those elements were as bleak and disturbing as I thought they might be, but the rest of the book, the vast majority of it, in fact, is a more straight-forward narrative which sees him reminiscing about his life prior to this horrible war injury.

The book is about a young man named Joe Bonham who is badly maimed in the first world war. And by badly maimed, I mean to a horrific and repulsive degree. To begin with, he realises that he can't hear before gradually discovering that he also has no arms, no legs, no mouth, and no eyes. As you can imagine this is a hellish existence, one which he flits in and out of, going back and forth in his mind looking over his life, his past, his parents, his upbringing, his girlfriend Kareen, and several other fragmented memories. He has no real way to communicate with the nurses, no exit, and is imprisoned within himself, inside his mind, in a manner that is too terrible to even contemplate. Towards the end, he eventually does communicate with one of the nurses by banging his head in Morse code while she writes letters on his chest. But even this brings no relief. For understandable reasons, he wants to be euthanised and just when you think his suffering might end, he is denied this escape. As anti-war novels go, it's pretty much as grim and as awful as it gets. There is always a sense of something truly horrifying being experienced as you read, something utterly inhuman. It's effectively done to the extent that you simply want to grimace.


As powerful as the book is, however, I didn't really enjoy it. Trumbo's writing was never very engaging to me, not to mention the fact that he refuses to use commas for some reason. Plus all the stuff about Joe's past, his life before the war, was never that interesting to me. I understand why it's there (to make Joe sympathetic, to give him a real life, a real soul, so that the reader knows that he's more than just a sentient stump of flesh), but ultimately it was never that exciting to read about. We know that Joe is, or rather was, a full human who existed within the world and knew its pleasures, joys, and experiences, but Trumbo deliberately makes them prosaic and bland which isn't always gripping. Each chapter essentially has these reminiscing scenes, where we go back to Joe's memories, and while they're necessary, they don't really make for an entertaining read.

The parts of the book in the hospital, however, were always far more visceral, more engaging, but of course this is only because they were so troubling and repulsive. To such an extent that it becomes almost sickening. Ultimately, I found the book a little slow and meandering, but as true as that is, and as much as I disliked Trumbo's writing style, the fact remains that this book will stay with me for a long time. It is a truly horrible concept. And every time I think of it, I will feel slightly nauseated. As such, approach it with caution.
