For as far back as I can recall, I have been a fan of Doctor Who. I can’t say exactly when I first watched the Doctor and his companions travel on adventures throughout time and space, but it was early and lasting. As an American, however, my ability to appreciate the show was long constrained by what my local PBS affiliate aired, which usually meant a steady diet of the Third and Fourth Doctors with gradual expansion into the later runs as time went along. Only rarely would I catch a glimpse of the first two Doctors: a surviving serial here, a brief appearance in a subsequent story there, and that was about it.The release of the series on home media freed me from my dependency on local broadcasts, and provided me with the opportunity to sample everything that was available. Yet one constraint remained: thanks to the BBC’s practice in the late 1960s and early 1970s of wiping videotape recordings of their shows, many of the early stories are unavailable for viewing. While the BBC has invested considerable effort in recovering missing episodes and reconstructing unavailable ones using animations and audio recordings, this has only partially filled in the gap. The only way to experience many of these serials today, some of which are legendary by reputation and their contribution to the Doctor Who mythos, is by reading the novelizations published starting in the 1980s, some of which were authored by the scriptwriters for the series.John Lucarotti was one of these scriptwriters. This, the first of three serials he contributed to the series, is the first of the stories that lacks surviving episodes, and is missing in its entirety. The reader is thus forced to rely completely on their imagination to envision the events of Lucarotti’s tale, in which the Doctor and his companions materialize on a frigid mountain on Earth in 1289. They soon discover that they have landed on the Pamir Mountains, where they encounter a caravan led by the Venetian explorer Marco Polo. Only Polo is homesick after many years in the service of Kublai Khan, and upon learning of some of the TARDIS’s miraculous qualities decides to make a gift of the travelers’ “flying caravan” to the emperor in the hopes of being rewarded with his freedom.The crew’s quest to reclaim the TARDIS becomes the central conflict in the story, as the Doctor, Susan, Ian and Barbara match wits with Polo as they caravan travels to Kublai’s court. By itself, such a tale could get tedious, but Lucarotti enlivens it with a budding friendship between Susan and a young woman being escorted to the court and an antagonist with his own designs on the ship. Taken together, it makes for a fine example of the early historically-focused episodes reflected the intent for the show to serve as an educational tool for young viewers. This is also why it is more than a little surprising that Barbara does not play a more prominent role in the plot – even Ian proves more of an asset on the adventure than the crew’s historian, thanks to his scientific knowledge and ability to deliver a good kick.The result is a good adventure that proved a well-received episode when it was originally broadcast in 1964. As a read it, though, I couldn’t help but think that it might have benefited just a little from the erasure of its episodes, as it was unlikely to have aged well. While viewers have long become used to adventures performed on sound stages with limited budgets, the fact that nearly every Asian character was played by a British actor in yellow face is cringeworthy today. Of the entire cast only Zienia Morton could claim any Asian ancestry, making her casting at that time as Ping-Cho somewhat remarkable. Just as the reader can enjoy Lucarotti’s story free from the financial constraints that plagued early Who, they also can cast it with individuals far more appropriate for the story’s setting. In that respect, it reaffirms the value of relying on the novelizations for revisiting the missing tales.
