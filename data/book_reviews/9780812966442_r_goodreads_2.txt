What a powerful and sad story.Martin Edenis something of an AmericanGreat Expectations, which from me is a huge compliment because I adore that book. Both Pip and Martin get a glimpse of something grander than the life they've experienced, through their encounter with an upper class girl. Both of them experience a sudden awareness of themselves— a growing class consciousness that compels them to seek a "better" life: for Pip, that of a London Gentlemen; for Martin, that of an educated, well-spoken man of high society.But is it better?The more educated Martin becomes, the more he adapts his grammar and develops his writing ability, the more disillusioned he grows with the world he has been striving to enter. Like Pip, he starts to see the pretensions of upper class society for what they are: shallow performances. He sees that they value success over integrity, wealth over actual talent. He realises, too, that literary success has little to do with ability, and far more to do with finding the right audience at the right time.It is one of the most class-conscious novels I have ever read. The barriers between Martin and Ruth are not solely, or even mainly, about wealth, but they are also separated by education and grammar. The novel reflects on how education, or lack of, divides people through language, and the way a person's work affects even the way in which they carry themselves, manual labour influencing their posture or gait.There is something heartbreaking at the centre of this story. Martin is made to feel small when he is rough and uneducated, but years spent ostensibly bettering himself do not offer the peace of mind he had imagined they would. In a way, he sees an exclusive club and fights desperately to get in, only to discover that it's all a sham behind the doors.
