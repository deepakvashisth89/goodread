Considering how much I have read on the Civil War and even about events leading up to it the gap in my knowledge and understanding of the post-war period was nearly complete. If they taught about this is school in Connecticut it was cursory and superficial. Perhaps embarrassment was part of that. It is not a 'proud' moment in American history. I had no very preconceived notions (something about Carpetbaggers and Lost Cause) and was certainly not a student of the excoriated (by Foner) 'Dunning' school of Reconstruction history from the early 20th century. I also broke one of my own rules not to read abridged volumes, of anything. But this copy was at hand (borrowed) and so I went to it. The original work from 1989 was 'Reconstruction: America's Unfinished Revolution 1863-1877' and runs to 752 pages! Don't think I was ready for that. He has apparently written some 10 books on the subject and won several prestigious book awards so he has lived and breathed the subject. Despite the abridgment this a fairly dense tome in terms of information and appears to be an excellent account. So much happened and while I learned a whole lot, was just scratching the surface I suppose. The election of 1876 for instance--crazy! This is not just 'dry and dusty' history either, the issues dealt with in this book resonate in current affairs to an uncomfortable degree. Without reiterating the facts and his arguments which are obviously sort of unassailable, the question arose in my mind about the war aims of the North. They changed so drastically between 1861 and 1865 that it seems that Reconstruction on the scale attempted was doomed to failure in one way or another. In other words 'the goal posts moved' and not just incrementally. Lincoln's often repeated statement was that if he could save the Union without freeing a single slave he would (but also would free them all). There is simply no question but that the North in 1861 would never have gone to war if the the stated aim was to achieve the society envisioned by Reconstruction. That is as factual as anything Foner said. So when it ended in complete victory there was little to no plan for 'what now'? Slavery was such a monstrosity and embedded so deeply in the southern mindset and economy that killing it and moving to complete social equality in a few short years was never going to be easy. Was there another way? Probably, but sort of unanswerable like most hypothetical questions. Today the definition of equality itself has been almost completely redefined (just as the war aims of 1861 were) and for many in the current 'woke' crowd it means punishment and retribution for the past and equality of 'outcome' today. We will see how that works out and maybe someone will read about it in history books, if in fact historians are willing or even permitted to state unpopular truths as that becomes increasingly difficult.
