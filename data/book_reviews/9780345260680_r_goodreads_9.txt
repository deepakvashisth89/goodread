This is the first of Heinlein's juvenile SF novels written under contract with Scribner's in 1947. I read it as a part of the multi-month Buddy read endeavor in November 2025 atHugo & Nebula Awards: Best Novelsgroup. Scribner's published the first 12 novels between 1947 and 1958, but rejected the 13th,Starship Troopers. That one was instead published by Putnam. A 14th novel,Podkayne of Mars, is sometimes also listed as a "Heinlein juvenile". Actually, onlyHave Space Suit—Will Travel(1958) andStarship Troopers(1959) were nominated for Hugo Award, but this was partially because most of them were published before the award was introduced in 1953 and partially because it was considered more an award for ‘adult’ SF.This novel starts by introducing the reader to a bunch of boys (their exact age isn’t supplied, but they are still going to school, so 15-17 years in the likeliest range): Ross Jenkins, Art Mueller, and Morris Abrams as they are launching a self-made rocket. They are members of the Galileo Club and their distant and unreachable goal is to reach space. However, the goal becomes attainable as Art's uncle, the nuclear physicist and potential Nobel prize winner Dr. Donald Cargraves, offers them the opportunity to construct and crew a nuclear-powered rocket to journey to the moon. Unlike a lot of stories in a similar vein, where the union of an older person and a bunch of younger ones is quick and easy, here the author spends time to, on the one hand, address the point “why schoolboys and not say MIT’s graduates?” and on the other, to persuade boys’ parents that the enterprise won’t harm their other plans.The group successfully launches the rocket and reaches the destination only to meet a lot of surprises and dangers there. This was a debut in juvenile SF for Heinlein, and the story feels a bit inchoate, plus too close to the present day. All the later novels are set after interplanetary and sometimes even interstellar travel is an ordinary thing, which allowed them to age better. However, this book has some very interesting ideas. For example, the Doctor suggests that the USSR could be first in space (10 years before Sputnik), for neither the USA gov’t nor business is interested in such a waste of resources. Also, on margins, readers can note the campaign to give the UN nuclear arsenal and to make the organization a much stronger peace-keeper. In reality, the campaign failed after the Soviets tested their bomb in 1949.Overall, I acknowledge the nostalgia value of this book (I first read it in translation like 35 years ago), but I doubt that a lot of the present-day juveniles will like it. It is a product of its time, and back then, it was quite good.
