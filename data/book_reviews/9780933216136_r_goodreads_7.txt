A really interesting look at 1980s queer sex therapy--obviously this one is for lesbians but it is very clear that the author was seeing asexuality in her rooms as well. She does not use the word 'asexual' as an orientation, because the concept and word did not fully exist. However, she is careful to ask her clients whether they actually want to change their desire before embarking on sex therapy with them. Even in 2025, you'll be lucky to find a therapist who fully understands asexual orientation. Therapy rooms can function as yet another site of sexual coercion for asexuals.“Lack of sexual desire is the most common sexual problem lesbians come to me with. If you are experiencing lack of sexual desire, you are not unusual.“Many lesbians go their whole lives not having sex; some go for long periods of time. Not desiring sex does not make you not “normal”; it simply means you don’t have a desire for sex. Not wanting sex doesn’t have to be perceived negatively. You are not cold, frigid, uptight or invulnerable just because you are not wanting sex."Loulan also talks about "The Willingness Model" which“[s]tarts with the premise that women can begin to have sex because we are willing. We do not have to be experiencing great desire or physical excitement; we can initiate sex because we want to. What a revolutionary idea! Women don’t have to be overcome by the heat of desire to want to have sex. .... Whatever the reason you are willing, that is the first step of this response cycle. It is not a passive stance. It is not meant to be looked at as “Oh well, I guess I’ll lie here and go through with sex.” Rather, this is a very active stage, at least in the mind. You are consciously deciding that you do want to have sex and you are willing to do so even if you have no physical or emotional desire."Emily Nagoski is the current popular voice talking about responsive desire versus spontaneous desire--a model which I find entirely falls short for sex-averse asexuals. (Although Nagoski says time and again that however you feel and experience sex, you're fine as you are--but she never explicitly acknowledges the existence of asexuality in Come As You Are--she seems to have only cottoned on to the existence of asexual orientation between that book [2015] and Come Together [2024].) Love to see it, but popular contemporary sex therapists are clearly still very much in the learning phase themselves. Nagoski's subchapter called "I Don't Want It" should have been included in Come As You Are and wasn't. JoAnn Loulan includes it here, in her 1980s book--so there was never any excuse not to.Modern sex therapists talk about consent. Loulan's older Willingness Model may be useful for sex favourable asexuals in mixed-orientation partnerships with allosexuals. However, not all aces are sex favourable. The Willingness Model is a step-up from the older twentieth century models she adds to here, and may be useful to a limited number of modern readers experiencing no-to-low sexual attraction and/or desire. Pair with Angela Chen's chapter on consent in her book Ace (2020).I'm not suggesting this retro sex therapy book is the answer for modern (lesbian) asexuals. In many ways it's out of date, and Loulan does write numerous times "everyone is a sexual being". So while she saw asexuality in her clients, she also didn't quite understand it.However, as a document of how asexuals have always existed (invisibly) in the wider queer community, this book is it.
