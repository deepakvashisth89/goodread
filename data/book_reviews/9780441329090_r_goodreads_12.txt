I enjoyed this one so much more than the first book -- most of it was engaging and interesting and from time to time it was quite numinous. I still find Laura's POV sections a problem, though; I think she's a realistic portrait of a certain sort of shy, anxious, unhappy little kid, butrather than resolving that by having her grow into herself, Dean shows her being very, very passive (since most of the time she's too scared to take action), and then compensates for it by repeatedly making her special. Laura has plenty of room in the story to *do* things but she rarely does; things happen to her, instead -- the unicorns talk to her, she has visions, she gets to play the magical flute.... she's necessary to the story because Dean makes her necessary, but she never takes her specialness in her hands and runs with it. It's a strange choice, and I spent a lot of the book wondering why Dean was doing it -- could she not envision a version of Laura who actually decided to take meaningful action? Would it have messed up the arc of the plot if Laura went and *did things* instead of just having them happen to her? I feel like most of the pacing issues in these books are because of the sections in the 'wrong' POV -- it's always better when we're seeing things from Ted (or Ruth or Patrick's) POV, because they go and do things, whereas when the exciting plot moments are happening Laura tends to go hide in her bedroom and avoid them. If Dean wanted to surprise the readers by not showing us directly what was going on, why not just *skip ahead* instead of showing us Laura hiding and feeling bad?For all of that, there was a lot I enjoyed here -- I feel like the preise is still very sound, and there's a good backbone despite all my disagreements with execution. I really likedthe repetition of the Fence/Randolph scene that starts the first book -- it was a nice moment of uncanniness.I probably will reread these eventually; the good parts really do shine.
