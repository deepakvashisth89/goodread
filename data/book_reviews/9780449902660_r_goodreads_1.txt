My very first exposure to the late cartoonist John Caldwell, strangely enough, was on a Children's Television Workshop show (I don't remember which) sometime during the early 1980s; I'm pretty sure Caldwell animated a couple educational segments on public TV featuring the unmistakeable body shapes of his characters. And in 1988, devoted an entire feature to selected cartoons from this book, , celebrating arguably his breakthrough outside of in his syndicated cartoons.

And in turn, the overwhelming majority of the cartoons in are in fact laugh-out-loud funny, if almost entirely a bit peculiar, as befits the style of John Caldwell himself. As the introduction to this review suggests, Caldwell had several wells he delved into frequently; Caldwell's male characters almost invariably wear plaid pants (at least when they're not in a suit or uniform), Caldwell loves to show middle-management executives acting very much out of character for anything resembling the business world, and Caldwell found seemingly endless humor in the idea—and business—of shepherding. (These examples of Caldwell's wells are also by no means exhaustive.) There's something downright in Caldwell's oeuvre, insofar as just about all of his cartoons very seriously defy anything we'd expect from normal life, and if the book offers a glimpse into Caldwell the cartoonist's mind, oh what a fantastical and creative and mind it is.

It's a testimony to how beloved so many members of The Usual Gang of Idiots are or were that whenever any of them passes away (as did only a few months ago) that they're almost universally celebrated when they do leave an appreciative world.
