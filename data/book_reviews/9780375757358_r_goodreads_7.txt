‘I think that the Russian revolution has made my Western Eyes look terribly outdated,’ Conrad wrote to André Gide in 1918. The dissolute Frenchman might have told him, if he was in a bitchy mood, that it was outdated long before that.

Which is disappointing, because metatextually, Under Western Eyes is fascinating. Conrad, whose parents essentially died as freedom fighters against Russia – Conrad, who said he found Russian literature ‘repugnant’ and whose books all show a strain of Russophobia – here finally addresses the country directly, in a book intended to be a portrait of ‘the psychology of Russia itself’. Not only that, he finished and revised the book in the midst of a full-on nervous breakdown, in which he wandered from room to room, arguing with his characters out loud in Polish and accusing his family of trying to section him.

So far, so intriguing. The problem is that the book doesn’t actually work. It doesn’t work as a story, and it doesn’t work as a sustained piece of English prose.

Conrad takes a promising concept – an innocent student caught up in a revolutionary plot – and bogs it down it in a giant quicksand of moody soul-searching, overstrung characters, and tortuous interior monologue. There was a moment in the first part of this novel (when a St Petersburg cab-driver shouts, ‘Oh thou vile wretch!’) when I thought, – only to suddenly remember, hang on, this was written in English! And that’s the thing – it all feels like a certain kind of porridgy translationese.

Which is not something I’ve ever thought about Conrad before. Although English was not his first language, I generally find his writing, however chewy, to have a dark, stately aesthetic appeal. Here I didn’t find that at all. I thought it was not just infelicitous, but often barely clinging to literacy by its fingernails. Even the pull-quote on the back of this Penguin Classics edition—

And so it goes on, with every single line of dialogue hedged around with descriptors and adverbs and interior brooding. Every brief conversation takes pages and pages to get through in this way, and the result is, in the end, a lessening of tension, because when line is the subject for portentous internal monologue, then no line really seems to matter. It’s like trying to watch a film shot all in extreme close-up. Zoom out for a second, let me get an overview of what’s going on!

I sympathised with the narrator, who at one point tries to explain his confusion at a political speech: ‘I haven’t understood—I won’t say a single word; I’ve understood all the words.’ That was pretty much my feeling about the book as a whole. I understood all the words. But they just don’t add up.
