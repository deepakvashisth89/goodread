If Lincoln was the Great Emancipator, what does that make John Brown? To some he was a madman, to others a terrorist, to many African Americans he was the greatest white man.Here's a man who grew up in the Puritan tradition, descendant of American Revolutionaries, living as a yeoman farmer in the Ohio River Valley. He grew up rarely seeing black people, let alone slaves. His time was spent on various abortive business ventures, as a tanner and later a wool trader, and especially as a father. Brown was an authoritative and in some ways overbearing father, but also one who clearly loved and respected his children. He showed a clear sensitivity to how his deep religious beliefs interacted with his personality and his social environment. In the 1840s he wrote to his daughter: "I feel considerable regret by turns that I have lived so many years and have in reality done so little to increase the amount of human happiness. I often regret that my manner is not more kind and affectionate to those I really love and esteem."Over the next decade, Brown would show the depth of his regret. He would devote himself to increasing the sum of human happiness by laboring to liberate the wretched of the earth and in the process show that his love and esteem extended to all black people. There's a reason one of the most brilliant African Americans, WEB Du Bois, devoted an entire book to Brown, just as there is good reason why black leaders from Harriet Tubman and Frederick Douglass to MLK Jr and Malcolm X praised the man. His life is one of uncognizable commitment. His willingness, in Douglass' words, to "die for the slave" in "his zeal in the cause of freedom" goes beyond all reason. It was pure compassion and faith. From this perspective its easy to see why he was regarded as mad: no person amongst the millions of faithful and Christian parties to the Civil War, not the southern slaveocrats, not the northern middle-class abolitionists, not even the freedmen, hadthatlevel of faith in their cause.Du Bois' book is great because it paints this fulsome picture of Brown. He is not just a violent act at Harper's Ferry. His life is as significant as his death, even if his death ultimately sparked the most deadly war in American history. In particular, Brown's character and his relationship with the black community are emphasized. Brown was industrious, entrepreneurial, and, ultimately, tragic. He had many children, many of whom died at young ages. He tried many business ventures, including one where he fashioned a precursor to the modern farmer's co-op, all of which failed. Brown's economic story is relevant because it defined his path in life. Just as he neared economic prosperity he was ruined by financial panics, colluding industrialists, or bad turns of luck. Had his schemes worked, its hard to see Brown following his eventual violent path-- he would have been a funder of a different John Brown instead.At the same time, Brown was shaped by the new world's black liberation movements. He read voraciously about the Haitian Revolution, assisted fugitive slaves, and was eventually drawn into freedman and abolitionist circles. Du Bois stresses the bilateral nature of these relationships, Brown respected his black friends as true equals ("He came to them on a plane of perfect equality-- they sat at his table and he at theirs") and they, in turn, informed and emboldened him. It was a black preacher named Fayette who led him to solemnly swear to "make active war on slavery". This forged a commitment that was impressive even to other passionate abolitionists: "He thought ... he had no better use for his life than to lay it down in the cause of the slave ... Had some men made such display of rigid virtue, I should have rejected it as affected, false, and hypocritical, but in John Brown, I felt it to be as real as iron or granite." His devotion to black liberation led him to work in abolitionist circles even as he was occupied with business and family responsibilities. He helped resist slave catchers in the North, supported the Underground Railroad, and at one point led a New York colony for the education of freedmen.Yet, he remained a relatively unknown figure into his 50s. He would only capture national attention after Bleeding Kansas. The desperate gambit of slave states to win more support for their moribund class, the US government under a Democratic party owned by large slave owners allowed thugs from Missouri to invade a neighboring soon-to-be state, terrorize the population, and seize the government (after burning the free soil capital and rigging the election). Its important to note that Brown did not go to Kansas to fight these pro-slavery forces. He went because his sons had settled there and were unsafe without weapons to defend themselves from Missourians. He turned violent because pro-slavery forces terrorized the population with impunity in the face of free soil acquiescence. Only then did he strike back, though he did so viciously. On a silent night, Brown and his band kidnapped and executed five pro-slavery soldiers. This sparked a pitched battle in Kansas, dragging the hesitating, noncommittal free soilers into a precursor of the Civil War. Much like the Spanish Civil War and WW2, Bleeding Kansas foreshadowed the wider conflict: the reticent but more numerous northerners, sparked by Brown's actions, overcame their delusions to confront and defeat the slave power. Brown became a legend, a murderer in the south, a dangerous radical in respectable northern circles, but a hero to many serious opponents of slavery. He contributed to skirmishes, protected free soiler farms, and even led a raid of Missouri plantations. His experiences in Kansas set the stage for Harper's Ferry. He developed organizational capacity, military experience, and the reputation needed to plan his attack.Du Bois spends significant time on Brown's attack plan. His intent is to show that Brown was not crazy in attempting his raid, as was believed during Du Bois' time. The author's theory is that Brown intended to pursue a guerrilla campaign throughout the south, with the Alleghenies as a base of operations. He quotes Brown as saying "God had established the Allegheny Mountains from the foundation of the world that they might one day be a refuge for fugitive slaves". Again, Brown draws on the efforts of black fighters before him, who had long used this mountain range to escape slavers and convey runaways to the north. Brown's plan called for a band of trained men to stake out in the mountains, conducting periodic raids for supplies and to free slaves, growing their numbers with able slaves and inspiring resistance across the south's plantations. The organized forces of the US army wouldn't be able to catch them and neither would southern militias. As Brown's band continued its disruptive campaign, word would spread to more and more slaves and they, drawing on their superior numbers and the isolation of the south's rural slave economy, would soon take matters into their own hands. Brown's plan drew inspiration from Washington and L'Overture's campaigns in the Americas and pre-figured the similar successful campaigns of Castro, Ho Chi Minh, and many other guerilla fighters.However, there were significant differences between Brown's plan and these successful guerillas. The principal difference was that Brown had almost no support. He was planning to conduct his campaign in the heart of the slave power, surrounded by slave masters, militias, and the US army. He could not rely on any of the local population except for the slaves, who were severely disempowered themselves. Furthermore, Brown had little support in terms of funding or material resources from abolitionists. His work in Kansas yielded some arms and a small number of men willing to fight for him, but he had no money to feed them, house them, or otherwise equip them for this campaign. He faced numerous funding issues, such that he was forced to delay his plan for years. At one point, he recruited a troop of freedmen in Canada, but they abandoned the effort after a funding delay.When the time came for the raid, he had fewer than 30 men ready to fight for him. Despite the limited troops available to him, he seized Harper's Ferry and the federal armory there. Yet, delays in moving his arms to the Virginia side of the mountains meant that Brown and his men were surrounded and eventually taken captive or killed. Brown was beaten, but kept alive even as many in his band were killed, including his sons ("This is the third son I lost in this cause" he said after his son was shot carrying out a white flag to surrender).More important than the raid itself was its polarizing effect on the US. Brown's trial was front page news across the country. Amidst Brown's stentorian and moralistic denunciations of southern slavery, northerners were forced to confront the reality of slavery ("The cry of distress of the oppressed is my reason, and the only thing that prompted me to come here"). Brown struck fear in the heart of southerners by signaling broad northern opposition to slavery ("you had better -- all you people at the South -- prepare yourself for a settlement of this question, that must come up from settlement sooner than you are prepared for it") . Thus, from Du Bois: "from the day John Brown was captured to the day he died, and after, it was the South and slavery that was on trial". Brown was not only a subject of this process, but an active participant. "The very processes by which Virginia used John Brown to 'fire the Southern heart' were used by John Brown to fire the Northern conscience". Brown embraced his role and saw it as the culmination of his war on slavery: "I think I cannot now better serve the cause I love so much than to die for it; and in my death I may do more than in my life." On December 2nd, 1859, John Brown would hang. Less than 6 years later his captor, Robert E Lee, would surrender at Appomattox, ending slavery in the US.I read this book a year after readingThe Black Jacobins: Toussaint L'Ouverture and the San Domingo Revolutionby CLR James. The books make good companion pieces. Not only did Brown consciously follow in the path of the Armée Indigène, but he understood them. He, Toussaint L'Overture, and a great many fighters for freedom understood Du Bois' oft repeated phrase: "the cost of liberty is less than the price of repression." These are people who understood the highest ideals of the Enlightenment and worked tirelessly for their realization on behalf of the most vulnerable. This makes Brown more than a towering historical figure, he is also one of the great American teachers -- as Du Bois says he "taught us that the cheapest price to pay for liberty is its cost today."So was Brown mad? No. Was he a terrorist? Only to slaveholders. Was he a great man? Yes, if greatness means sacrifice on behalf of others. Brown did more than most, but he did so in the context of a broader struggle for freedom. He worked with and in the legacy of American and Haitian Revolutionaries, Puritans, Enlightenment thinkers, Abolitionists, Freedmen, and many more, for a better world. His import and inspiration rests in his commitment to others rather than his readiness to violence. He serves as a reminder of the most selfless actions that human empathyandhuman reason can motive us towards. He helped free the slaves and helped inspire the continuing fight for a more just world. His body is a-mouldering in the ground, but his soul marches on!
