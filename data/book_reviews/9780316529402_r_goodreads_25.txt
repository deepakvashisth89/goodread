The Arms of Krupp: The Rise and Fall of the Industrial Dynasty that Armed German at War is a centuries-spanning narrative which follows one of the most important families in the history of Germania. It tracks the members of the Barons of the Ruhr, a clan that was a private sector dynasty with more power than some European royal families of the age.

Historian William Manchester created a story which also functions as a retelling of Germany's rise to dominance over neighbors who were united and powerful centuries before the Second Reich.

The company which would eventually arm the Third Reich during the Second World War began to first dabble in weaponry when they made muskets during the Thirty Years War (1618-1638). While this was their first taste of small scale ordinance production, for their first two and a half centuries the industries of Krupp production primarily dealt in iron and coal.

This arms-mongering road was trod further down during the Napoleonic Wars. During these years (in which both pro-French and anti-Bonapartist armies were equipped by Krupps) Friedrich founded the cast steel factory Gusstahlfabrik in Essen.

Essen, the family's ancestral hometown, was for centuries their base of operations.

Within several decades this concern would employ tens of thousands of German workers, and Manchester analyzes how the Germanic notion of employer paternalism toward their employees was, if not popularized by, certainly carried out by Friedrich Krupp. (Accordingly, the organization was perturbed by Germany's Social Democrats and their efforts to push for worker welfare in the German republic's early days.)

Krupp's arming of the Prussians against France during the 1870-1871 war further cemented the respect the organization and the family whose name it bore were held in by the volk.

There is a lot about Prussia and the early founding days of Germany's nationhood. It was the Franco-Prussian War that kickstarted their weapons orders, especially within the Second Reich; the Krupp arms' performance in this conflict significantly raised the esteem in which they were held on the continent. Kaiser Wilhelm II came to realize just how critical the Krupp works were to protecting newfound Germanic national sovereignty in the latter part of the nineteenth century.

And it was not just cannons: the industrial concern profited mightily during the dawn of the railroad age thanks to the many miles of track their steel produced. The company also dabbled in numerous other iron and steel products, trying their hand out at whatever in their wheelhouse they felt could turn a profit.

A portion of the book looks at how Friedrich's son, Friedrich Alfred "Fritz" Krupp, had serious accusations leveled against him when it came to his treatment of young boys. That he did not face extreme censure for this was proof of how protecting the Krupp family was a priority for those in power in Germany.

An organization that employed very few men at the beginning of the 1800s was employing tens of thousands by the time Fritz was in charge.

The author examines the manner in which, during various late nineteenth and early twentieth century conflicts, arms from the Krupp works actually equipped both sides. Turkey and Russia were actually two of the original large purchasers of weapons from the company during the mid-1800s.

But arming the highest bidders fell out of favor after the First World War as the family became sensitive to (truthful) charges that their products were killing members of the German nation and not just its enemies.

The "Big Bertha" howitzer was actually named after Bertha Krupp von Bohlen, who inherited proprietorship of the firm following her dad's death. Her husband, Gustav von Bohlen und Halbach, although not actually a blood relative of the family, was granted the title "Krupp" and was the individual in charge of day to day operations until the 1940s.

Bertha and Gustav's son, Alfried, would be rewarded by Hitler in 1943 with the Krupp surname and ownership of the company in what would turn out to be the concluding years of the war.

And the World War Two and immediate postwar years consume much of the second half of The Arms of Krupp.

Manchester painstakingly details the manner in which the Krupps-not unique among German Third Reich industrialists-employed slave labor at their factories. Although the family made a slew of excuses to make it look as though they were not knowingly technically using slave labor, they did in fact put tens of thousands of slaves, including many from Auschwitz, to work building their weapons and steel products.

The portions of the book dealing with the treatment of prisoners at Krupp-backed workshops and labor camps during the early to mid 1940s was not designed to endear readers to the Krupps.

Their Nuremberg appeals that they were just following orders under the fear of reprisals by the government in Berlin were undermined by numerous other German industrialists who did not use slave labor but nevertheless did not face retaliation.

Alfried would end up sentenced to twelve years imprisonment at the Nuremberg trials-punishment his lawyers claimed was actually for the sins of his father-but released in 1951 after serving not even half that amount. This Nuremberg section was lengthy-and postwar Allied Commissioner for Germany John J. McCloy plays a huge role-and delves into the role the Cold War began to play as the Second World War vanished further into the rearview mirror.

From 1945-1947, to hear Manchester tell it, America and the Allied occupation government was, understandably, out for vengeance against the Nazi high command and their collaborators. But as America and her allies began to desire West Germany's cooperation with and integration within their anti-Soviet sphere, various war criminals (Alfried Krupp among them) began to get off easier than they should have.

This placation meant Alfried had been fortunate in getting his own Nuremberg judgment put off by several years to a time when containing Russia was beginning to take precedence over punishing the Reich's wrongdoers.

The portion during the 1950s and 1960s (after which the company was restructured and much familial influence diluted) is enough to make readers sick as the company continues to churn out profits and seemingly suffers little in the way of consequences for their almost unrepentant collaboration with the likes of Hitler and Goering.

The dynastic nature of the Krupps and their influence on European events comes through well in this incredibly written book. Manchester created a magisterial true story of arrogance, greed, and intra-familial selfishness which reveals the depths humanity will go to in order to maintain a place at the seats of power.

The book numbers near a thousand pages, but it is an important contribution to the canon of European and world history. From Arndt in the 1500s to Alfried in the 1900s, the arc of an important but morally comprised dynastic family is traced with careful attention to detail in what can be none other than a five star book.

-Andrew Canfield Denver, Colorado
