A Gothic masterpiece with direct ties to a real-life Gothic event—the Salem Witch Trials!

Nathaniel Hawthorne's great-great-grandfather, Judge John Hathorne, ruled many women guilty of witchcraft. He declared guilt quicker than any other magistrate and, when others later expressed regret, he remained steadfast in his decisions.

These decisions included the execution of Sarah Good and the imprisonment of her four-year-old daughter, Dorothy. The girl (practically a toddler) was branded a witch like her mother. Evidence included biting and the presence of snakes which allegedly “sucked” on her in Satanic ways.

According to trial transcripts, Hathorne summoned the little girl to the stand and boomed:

replied Dorothy, bewildered and terrified.

Though Dorothy was eventually released from a rotting underground prison, she emerged with a dead mother and permanent mental scars.

Judge Hathorne lived about 150 years before Nathaniel, but the family still resided in Salem, and it’s clear this history haunted the gifted writer. Nathaniel added the “W” to his last name to distance himself from his ancestor. He also wrote , a novel soaked in Witch Trial allusions and stand-ins for his own family.

The sensational opening chapter lays out important backstory and obvious parallels: about 150 years earlier, a man accuses another of being a wizard so that he can seize his property. Right before the supposed wizard is hanged, he places a curse on his accuser.

Eventually the book returns to the present day, where the curse seems to linger over the family mansion. But we must decide if the family truly cursed or if they have simply fallen into stagnant behavior.

After the blockbuster opening, readers should prepare for dramatically slower pacing and hints of the macabre over the previous blood-and-guts. It does pay off, but don’t stress about plot. This is not a plot novel so much as a character novel. Hawthorne crafts each chapter like a miniature epic, with slow build-up and internal melodrama climbing to a cliffhanger. Almost every scene takes place in one of three rooms, and movement within those spaces is nearly non-existent.

It’s a unique style that has tormented many high schoolers in AP English, but for pleasure reading, it’s worth surrendering to the rhythm. I could only manage a single chapter at a time before feeling exhausted, but I returned the next night without fail. I to find out what these characters would do next, and what lay beneath all the ghostly suggestion.

Despite the slow pace, there are horrific moments that left me breathless and goosepimpled. One involves mesmerism—always creepy—but another involves a chair. It may be the most unnerving chair sequence in all literature.

Hawthorne isn’t for everyone, but if willing to soak in his language like a luxurious bath, the experience doesn’t have to be tedious. He may seem excessive when describing a single room in obsessive detail, but nearly every object proves symbolic or foreshadows later drama. This is, after all, a novel that unfolds almost entirely inside one house. To describe it completely is not wasted effort.

Between the fascinating historical backstory, Hawthorne’s powerful pen, and the suffocating Gothic atmosphere, ranks among my favorite reading experiences ever. My only shame is in how little of Hawthorne’s remaining work I’ve read. I vow to mend this problem and will soon explore even his most obscure and forgotten tales.
