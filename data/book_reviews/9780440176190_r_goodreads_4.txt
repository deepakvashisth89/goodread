This review originally appeared onOff the Shelf.For the past year or so, any time I travel down to Florida for work, one of my sci-fi enthusiast co-workers has gifted me some vintage sci-fi book or another. The first was the book we’ll be speaking of today: Jeffery A. Carver’s 1975Star Rigger’s Way. I mostly enjoyed it! But the parts of it I didn’t enjoy were really bad! Star Rigger’s Way is a textbook mixed bag. Let’s dive in:The world ofStar Rigger’s Wayis probably the most intriguing thing about it. In the setting, spaceships are piloted by “Riggers,” who do not control these spaceships as terrestrial pilots might fly a plane—as is often depicted in the genre. Instead, the Riggers navigate these ships via rigger-nets—a sort of virtual-reality system by which they steer the ship with their minds and emotions. We might see this as a sort of progenitor of ideas that would later be found in works likeThe MatrixandNeuromancer, or in the drift-compatibility of mech operators inPacific Rim.This neural-operation is necessary because, within the setting, ships do not just navigate through “normal space,” but an extradimensional “Flux” space akin toStar Wars,Cowboy Bebop, orBabylon 5’s hyperspace. But within the setting ofStar Rigger, this Flux space is not as nondescript as in other stories. Here, the appearance of Flux space is determined by the minds of the riggers themselves. We get this description of starship-rigging at the beginning of the second chapter:Starship-rigging had been regarded… as clearly one of the most peculiarly demanding of professions. Piloting a starship involved a mastery of technology, of course; but more than that, it required curious aptitudes of personality, of emotional set. Star-rigging involved not only spaceflight but also the mastering of the Flux—that subjective realm underlying the normal-space of Prime Reality, a realm akin to but distinct from freewheeling fantasy, and as intricate as the mistily mapped waking dream.Our POV rigger in this story, Gev Carlyle, has this experience of the Flux in the opening chapter:…the starship floated in a vast, luminous space. Sculptured lemon clouds drifted in the distance, and russet layers of smoke twisted outwards to form a sea as board and as deep as the entire arm of the galaxy. …interstellar space rendered as an airy red and orange-yellow watercolor.Gev calls this the “subjective sea,” “partly real and partly a creation of [his] imagination.” And it’s in these moments where the book is at its most interesting. The central conflict comes from what has occurred prior to the book’s opening pages. Gev was just one rigger amongst six aboard theSedora, but an accident in Flux space killed off all the crew but him. And he’s picked up another stranded spacefarer in the form of the alien, feline-esque Cephean—a runaway who bit off more than he could chew whilst attempting to flee his home planet. TheSedora, meant for six riggers, cannot be safely piloted by one; so Gev must train Cephean, a stranger and an alien, to rig the ship with him. We are quickly informed of the dangers this poses.…no two riggers viewed the Flux identically, teamwork in the net demanded a gestalt, a near-perfect intermixing of visions, perceptions, and intuitive judgements. Several riggers functioning as a gestalt could sail a ship smoothly and speedily between stars. But working at odds in the net, they could tear a ship apart and leave the pieces boring lifeless in the Flux.Gev knows to get to a safe port, but doing so requires navigating the Hurricane Flume, a particularly tempestuous stretch of the Flux. With all this setup, and given how relatively thin the book was, I expected this would be the height of the third act, before the denouement. But, shockingly, Gev and Cephean navigate the Hurricane Flume in Chapter 4, leaving the remaining pages for the actual story: Gev trying to reconnect with his previous crew.You see, Gev wasn’t really meant to be on theSedora. He was crewed as an apprentice aboard a four-person starship called theLady Brillig; but, for reasons not told until much later, the crew of theBrilligcould not find a good sync with one another in the net. They were all good friends in normal space, but when they needed to connect to pilot the ship, things weren’t clicking. And so Gev was reassigned to theSedorato better cut his teeth as a starship-rigger, to eventually return to theBrilligas a full-fledged member of the crew.So then, upon finding safe port for theSedora, Gev’s mission pivots. He needs to get back home, he needs to find his friends and rejoin the crew. None of this comes as easily as he would hope.Throughout the rest of the planet-hopping story, we’re treated to a lot more fun descriptions of the Flux; but unfortunately, right in the middle of the story, Carver almost entirely derails the story with a misguided and unearned romance with the assistant of theLady Brillig’s former owner. Look… I have never once felted tempted to include a sex scene in any of my written work, not for any particularly prudish reason but simply because I don’t really think I—me—could properly capture such an intimate moment in words. I feel like you either go one of two ways with it: impressionistic and focused on feeling and emotion, or literal and clinical and focused on mechanics. Carver seems to favor the latter, and let me tell you, it is not fun to read. It was so overly clinical that, in conjunction with the fact that it was clear this subplot served no other purpose than to introduce a surface-level conflict re: Gev’s brief romantic commitment to Janofer, one of his formerLady Brilligco-riggers, and the fact that the assistant with whom he has this new tryst seemed to exist—as Le Guin wrote of the works of Larry Niven—only “to say Oh? and Ooooh! to the clever and resourceful hero.” (The Language of the Night, pg. 93) I did not care for it, and I almost stopped reading the book because of it.There was also—and this is super nitpicky—a moment near the end of the book where Gev answers aphone. Not a sci-fi, spacetech phone, just a phone. Like a normal landline. I’d hesitate to state affirmatively that such a detail is anachronistic to the setting, but it was definitely a moment of “Oh, yeah, this book is from the 70s.”So, despite Carver’s best efforts, I kept reading, and the book does have a fairly satisfying, if not a bit obvious conclusion. Throughout the whole story, through all of Gev’s searching for his friends, Cephean, his alien-cat co-pilot sticks around. Maybe the realStar Rigger’s Waywas the friends we made along the… way.Additionally, learned from Goodreads that this is part of a longer Star Rigger series, so I might try to find one or two of the other books to see what else Carver does with this world. Because the world is interesting. But so help me, Jeffery; one more terribly conceived romantic entanglement and I’mdone.
