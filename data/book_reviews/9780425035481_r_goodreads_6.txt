TELEMPATH
by Spider Robinson

Written in 1976 and soaked to the bone in hippie idealism, Telempath waves the old peace-and-love flag like it’s still Woodstock. We’ve got the familiar creed: smoke a little, find your Zen, and—apparently—violence can be avoided by the “truly serene mind.” That’s not me paraphrasing, that’s straight from the text.

Now, I’m all for a good philosophical thread in my science fiction, but this one wraps itself around your neck. The book doesn’t so much explore its ideas as it preaches them, and after a few chapters, you start to feel like you’ve wandered into a meditation seminar that charges by the hour.

The premise itself is intriguing—a post-apocalyptic world shaped by humanity’s own self-destructive impulses. There’s something genuinely clever buried in here about perception, empathy, and what it means to be human. Unfortunately, it’s weighed down by the author’s need to turn every page into a sermon. Not every reader comes to sci-fi to be spiritually corrected.

Then there’s the writing. Spider Robinson’s style can sparkle in his Callahan’s Crosstime Saloon stories—those are warm, funny, human. But here? The pacing lurches. New characters drop in from nowhere, then get a quick info dump like a resume read aloud. No buildup, no mystery, just “Hi, I exist, here’s my tragic backstory.” It’s clumsy, and it breaks whatever momentum the story manages to build.

What could’ve been a sharp, thought-provoking novel ends up wobbling between half-baked mysticism and rough editing. I admire the ambition, but execution matters—and this one misses the mark by a wide shot.

I know Spider has a loyal following and has often been hailed as the next Heinlein. I respect that, and I respect him. But not every experiment is a success. I wanted to like Telempath. I really did. But when the last page turned, I was left with more sighs than insight.

Three stars—for the idea, not the sermon.
