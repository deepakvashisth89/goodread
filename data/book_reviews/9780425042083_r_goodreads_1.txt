Philip Jose Farmer was the Wild Man of science fiction. He delighted in slaying sacred cows and tweaking social norms. He was knowledgeable in religion, and used that knowledge to question and challenge it. At a time when Sci-Fi was essentially sexless, Farmer routinely included not just sex, but often taboo and transgressive sex into his tales. And he suffused all of this with a broad and often outrageous humor. All of this is displayed in this fascinating collection.

Make no mistake, you are here for the title cut, the novella Riverworld. This novella was the origin of Farmer’s greatest creation, his wildly ambitious Riverworld series, where all humanity who had ever lived was resurrected in a river valley stretching beside a 20 million mile river that snaked around an artificially engineered planet. And while that series unfortunately began to deteriorate after its excellent initial two books, the novella here has the force and energy found in the series’s best books.

But there is much more here — stories that demonstrate Farmer’s wild and quirky creativity. There are stories written as if by fictional characters that often double as pastiches of the works in which they appear. Perhaps the wildest is a double pastiche, where Farmer presents a Tarzan story as if written by William, rather than Edgar Rice Burroughs. Crazy, crazy stuff. This volume also contain two, bonus Riverworld stories not published in the original collection, and an essay about the inspiration for Riverworld.


5 ⭐️

Farmer’s Riverworld series had one of the most stunningly creative and ambitious concepts in all of SciFi — all of humanity who ever lived mysteriously resurrected in a river valley along a 20 million mile river on artificially engineered planet. This novella is set on that world, and finds Tom Mix, 20th century American movie cowboy, and Yeshua, 1st century Jewish messiah (yes, that one!) in deadly conflict with fanatical late medieval German inquisitor Heinrich Kramer. This tale is dramatic, action packed, thought provoking, and has an absolutely devastating final line.

A 79 year old former World War I fighting ace wanders the halls of his old folks home at night fantasizing about flying and dogfights as he sexually assaults old women with dementia in their beds. (Depending on how seriously he takes his fighter ace fantasy he’s also not likely in his right mind.) His “bloody baron” arch villain is the head nurse that attempts to stop his frequent sexual assaults.
This story is well executed, creative, disturbing, raunchy, and funny. But…You might be able to write a story on this theme today, but definitely not with the humor to play it for laughs. It was pushing boundaries when he wrote it. It just doesn’t fly at all in today’s more enlightened world.

Throw some alien sci-fi and a dollop of eldritch horror into the mix, allow Holmes and Watson to hover around just off stage in the background, and here you have a Cracker Jack of a story.
