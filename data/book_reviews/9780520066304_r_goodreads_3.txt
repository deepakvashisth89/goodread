Really interesting book. I read Chapter 7 ("The Inquisition" in Literature and Art) and Chapter 9 (From Myth to History), and I skimmed the rest of the book. After reading some quantitative approaches to the various Inquisitions, it was difficult to switch to Peters' more qualitative approach. (That's why I got it - I wanted more information on the historiography of the Inquisition).I have two concerns: Peters' treatment of Juan Llorente and of Henry Charles Lea. The scholars who have done statistical research on the Inquisitions usually mention that Llorente's numbers are ludicrously over-exaggerated. William Thomas Walsh argues (and I have not checked to see if others say this) that Llorente stole inquisitorial documents, and burned those that did not fit his polemical narrative. Peters' is forthcoming about Llorente's wild polemic, but does not mention that many more modern scholars have discredited Llorente's numbers.Similarly, Peters gives a (justifiably) glowing presentation of Lea's work, but in my opinion understates Lea's shortcomings. Lea didn't know these shortcomings because he wasn't aware of archival holdings that would come to light later. However, Peters should know that, and I wish more time had been spent on what longstanding misconceptions come from Lea's scholarship.That being said, it is an interesting book and Peters covers a truly impressive amount of history, philosophy, literature, and art.
