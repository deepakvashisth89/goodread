The soul and strength of Black America, and the travails and injustices confronting Black Americans, come through with undeniable force in W.E.B. Du Bois’sThe Souls of Black Folk(1903). This collection of essays is every bit as powerful in its examination of American racism as it was when it was first published more than 100 years ago.Du Bois, the first African American to earn a Ph.D. at Harvard University, distinguished himself as historian, sociologist, and civil-rights activist. And of all the prescient ideas that he sets down inThe Souls of Black Folk, this statement near the beginning of the book may be one of the best-known and most widely quoted:Herein lie buried many things which, if read with patience, may show the strange meaning of being black here in the dawning of the Twentieth Century. This meaning is not without interest to you, Gentle Reader; for the problem of the Twentieth Century is the problem of the color line. (p. 3)ReadingThe Souls of Black Folk, one is continually impressed by both the stylistic grace of Du Bois’s work and the perspicacity of his observations, as with this well-known observation regarding a “double consciousness” among African Americans in the United States:After the Egyptian and Indian, the Greek and Roman, the Teuton and Mongolian, the Negro is a sort of seventh son, born with a veil, and gifted with second-sight in this American world – a world which yields him no true self-consciousness, but only lets him see himself through the revelation of the other world. It is a peculiar sensation, this double-consciousness, this sense of always looking at one’s self through the eyes of others, of measuring one’s soul by the tape of a world that looks on in amused contempt and pity. One ever feels his twoness – an American, a Negro; two souls, two thoughts, two unreconciled strivings; two warring ideals in one dark body, whose dogged strength alone keeps it from being torn asunder.(pp. 6-7)Du Bois, a strong and constant advocate for true equality in American society, also takes pains to make clear that, in their search for recognition of equal rights, the African American does not wish to “bleach” away a proud African heritage: “He simply wishes to make it possible for a man to be both a Negro and an American, without being cursed and spit upon by his fellows, without having the doors of Opportunity closed roughly in his face” (p. 7). In the American “Land of Opportunity,” Du Bois adds, “To be a poor man is hard, but to be a poor race in a land of dollars is the very bottom of hardships” (p. 10).Writing in a time when segregation laws had disenfranchised virtually all African Americans in the Southern states, when lynching was endemic throughout much of the South, when African Americans in the North faced discrimination of a generally quieter but no less real kind, Du Bois looks back at the legacy of the American Civil War and concludes that “The Nation has not yet found peace from its sins; the freedman has not yet found in freedom his promised land” (p. 7).Du Bois describes the “dawn of freedom” during the Civil War era, and then focuses on the betrayals and broken promises of the Reconstruction era and afterward. It is in this connection that Du Bois launches his well-known attacks upon Booker T. Washington, the other of the best-known African American leaders of that era.Du Bois called for a “Talented Tenth” within the African American community to move into intellectual leadership in areas like law, medicine, business, and academia. His essay “The Talented Tenth,” included in this volume, goes into detail regarding his reasons for this advocacy: “The Negro race, like all races, is going to be saved by its exceptional men. The problem of education, then, among Negroes must first of all deal with the Talented Tenth; it is the problem of developing the Best of this race that they may guide the Mass away from the contamination and death of the Worst” (p. 203).Washington, by contrast, emphasized education in more basic aspects of life for larger numbers of African Americans. And Du Bois was outraged by Washington’s “Atlanta Compromise” statement that seemed to acquiesce in segregation of the races: “In all things purely social,” Washington said, “we can be as separate as the five fingers, and yet one as the hand in all things essential to mutual progress.”“This ‘Atlanta Compromise,’” Du Bois notes acidly, “is by all odds the most notable thing in Mr. Washington’s career” – note the ambiguous tone of the word “notable” – and he follows up by calling Washington “certainly the most distinguished Southerner since Jefferson Davis” (p. 35). To thus compare an African American leader – one who himself was a survivor of the slave system – to the president of the Confederacy must be one of the most elegant insults in American literary history.Du Bois denounces Washington for asking that African Americans “give up, at least for the present, three things” – aspirations to political power, insistence on recognition of civil rights, and advocating for higher education for college-age African Americans – “and concentrate all their energies on industrial education, the accumulation of wealth, and the conciliation of the South” (p. 40). This program, Du Bois argues, has brought African Americans nothing but disfranchisement, inequality before the law, and a withdrawal of funding for historically black colleges and universities. Even if Washington’s supporters might have thought that Du Bois was oversimplifying Washington’s position, Du Bois comes across as a formidable prosecutor in the court of public opinion.Du Bois writes evocatively, in “Of the Meaning of Progress,” of his time as a young educator in Tennessee – and notes with sadness the reversals that have occurred since his earlier times there, with lynchings on the rise, education for African Americans neglected, and racial injustice and discrimination against African Americans clearly visible everywhere he looks. He concludes this assessment of the United States’ halting and insufficient “progress” in race relations by noting that, “Thus sadly musing, I rode to Nashville in the Jim Crow car” (p. 57).Through his travels in the South, Du Bois provides trenchant observations of the injustice that he sees around him. In “Of the Black Belt,” he writes of traveling in Georgia, and hearing a poor black laborer of one Georgia community describe being cheated by a white man who issued cardboard checks for 37 days’ work, failed to pay, and then had the sheriff confiscate the laborer’s mule, corn, and furniture. When Du Bois points out that “furniture is exempt from seizure by law”, the unfortunate laborer implacably replies, “Well, he took it just the same” (p. 101).I also appreciated when the great sociologist put aside his academic objectivity and told a personal story in his elegant, poetic style. “Of the Passing of the First-Born” tells the story of the birth of Du Bois’s first son, emphasizes the hope that the parents had for their child, and then evocatively chronicles the child’s death from fever:He died at eventide, when the sun lay like a brooding sorrow above the western hills, veiling its face; when the winds spoke not, and the trees, the great green trees he loved, stood motionless. I saw his breath beat quicker and quicker, pause, and then his little soul leapt like a star that travels in the night and left a world of darkness in its train. The day changed not; the same tall trees peeped in at the windows, the same green grass glinted in the setting sun. Only in the chamber of death writhed the world’s most piteous thing – a childless mother.(p. 157)I was expecting thatThe Souls of Black Folkwould prompt me to think long and hard about the ongoing problems of racism in American life. I was not expecting that passages from the book would bring tears to my eyes.This Penguin Classics edition ofThe Souls of Black Folkbenefits from an introduction by Ibram X. Kendi – one great anti-racist scholar contextualizing and commenting upon the work of another. I liked, for example, Kendi’s commentary on the idea of Du Bois’s idea of “the Veil”: “The Veil separated black folk from white folk. Within it, black folk endured racism, exploitation, and heartache that white people did not see, or did not want to see. The Veil doubled as both the oppression and the mirror of oppression. But the mirror – the Veil – could really be seen only by black people. Black folk could see the freedoms white people enjoyed outside of the Veil. White folk struggled to see the slaveries black folk recoiled from within the Veil” (pp. ix-x).ReadThe Souls of Black Folk. Not only was W.E.B. Du Bois right when he wrote that “the problem of the Twentieth Century is the problem of the color line”, but his words regarding the 20th Century apply all too clearly to the 21st Century as well.
