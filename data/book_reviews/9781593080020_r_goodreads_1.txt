Everyone knows this book, but no one is talking about it, and I think that needs to change.I picked this book up on a whim, and it (very unexpectedly) took my breathe away.It tells the story of a domestic dog, named Buck, who is cruelly taken from his home in California and forced to become a sled dog in the harsh Yukon during the Klondike Gold Rush. These events cause Buck to channel his primal instincts, and the reader watches as he transforms into a brutal survivor.Through this narrative, Jack London explores the relationship between a dog’s “call to the wild” and a dog’s loyalty to man.The story is heartbreaking, touching, and unforgettable. It also has some of the most beautiful natural imagery I have ever read.Here is one of my favorite passages. It is from Chapter 5:“It was beautiful spring weather, but neither dog nor humans were aware of it. Each day the sun rose earlier and set later. It was dawn by three in the morning, and twilight lingered till nine at night. The whole long day was a blaze of sunshine. The ghostly winter silence had given way to the great spring murmur of awakening life. The murmur arose from the things that lived and moved again, things which had been as dead and which had not moved during the long months of frost. The sap was rising in the pines. The willows and aspens were bursting out in young buds. Shrubs and vines were putting on fresh garbs of green. Crickets sang in the nights, and in the days all manner of creeping, crawling things rustled forth into the sun. Partridges and woodpeckers were booming and knocking in the forest. Squirrels were chattering, birds singing, and overhead honked the wildfowl driving up from the south in cunning wedges that split the air.From every hill slope come the trickle of running water, the music of unseen fountains. All things were thawing, bending, snapping. The Yukon was straining to break loose the ice that bound it down. It ate away from beneath; the sun ate from above. Air-holes framed, fissures sprang and spread apart, while thin sections of ice fell through bodily into the river. And amid all this bursting, rending, throbbing of awakening life, under the blazing sun and through the soft-sighing breezes, like wayfarers to death, staggered the two men, the woman, and the huskies.”What I find especially interesting is that this story was inspired by London’s own experience during the Klondike Gold Rush, in the years 1897 and 1898. He sought his fortune and found literary gold. I’m endlessly grateful that he has shared his treasures with us!
