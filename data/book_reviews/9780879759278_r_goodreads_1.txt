THE THOUGHTS OF 80 ‘EX-CATHOLIC’ WOMENAuthor Joanne H. Meehl wrote in the Preface to this 1995 book, “Why did we leave the Roman Catholic faith? Why won’t we raise our children as Catholic? Why do we fail to laugh at ‘cute’ Catholic jokes? Why do we call ourselves ‘recovering’ Catholics? What has made us so angry? And why is that anger still alive after so many years? How did we fell about leaving the fold? Why won’t we go back? And how have we changed since then? ‘What’ are we now? I have written this book to answer those questions, both for myself and for all who have left the Catholic faith, or who are still stuck in ‘lapsed’ status.” (Pg. 15)She explains in the Introduction, “my curiosity about other women who had left the Catholic faith sent me to the library… there was no literature, there were not studies. It was as if being an ex-Catholic was something … that no one had ever bothered to explore… I simply had to find other voices, other women who felt like I did, outside of my little Unitarian congregation. So I placed advertisements in various publications, including [a] Unitarian Universalist national magazine… That… generated 130 responses. The tone of so many of the women’s words was ‘I’ve been waiting to get this off my chest.’ I wasn’t alone! I sent them all questionnaires, asking them about specific incidents or stories they wanted to tell, about sex and birth control, about guilt and sin, about their spirituality… Over eighty of the women wrote or taped responses; in interviewed some who were in my vicinity… Obviously this is not a scientific sample… What is remarkable … is that … They talk about how they grew up in a church that did not---or does not---include them, and about how that exclusion and other related experiences negatively colored so many aspects of their lives.” (Pg. 17-19)In the first chapter, she explains, “The unhappy Catholic travels in ‘the Catholic Loop.’ This is a circular path that always brings one back to the same place. When someone considered exploring religious and spiritual options outside the Catholic church, obstacles usually arise… it could be a concern over some sacrament: ‘What will happen if I die without the last rites? I’d better just stay here.’ Then there is fear of the unknown outside ‘the church’… To question is to disobey. So the unhappy Catholic has only two choices: stay in the Loop and put up a good front… or just stop going to church, and do nothing else---no searching for … another church… It is making a decision without making a decision. It is the safest option…” (Pg. 28-29)She reports, “I am often asked… ‘Women leave the Catholic church because of the abortion issue, right?’ If that were true, there would one-third fewer women in Catholic churches, because Catholic women, as a group, obtain abortions in the same … proportion as other American women… the deeper reason why women stop going to Catholic church is that it makes them feel negative about themselves, both as human beings and as women.” (Pg. 42)She observes, ‘The rejection of women priests goes deeper than the hierarchy’s defense of an all-male priesthood based on the teaching that Christ chose only male disciples. The women here… have perceived an official condescension toward women, despite some parishes’ need (due to shortages of men) for women lectors and eucharistic ministers.” (Pg. 69)She states that in the church’s view, “divorce should not take place, and homosexuality is tolerate only if the person is celibate. But these views reflect a church that is out of touch with the reality …Some of the women react angrily to the church’s stance on birth control because they have … followed the church’s ban on birth control, [and were] hurt by that obedience.” (Pg. 112)She notes, “One cannot examine … women’s spirituality today… without being aware of the massive amount of research and study that has taken place in the last five to ten years… These are the women whose books now fill the shelves in the stores and libraries. Their voices have made a major difference in our thinking… I added their wise words here to help explain how women got into this patriarchal ‘trap’… their words also corroborate what the ex-catholic women say throughout this book. (Pg. 165-166)She states, “After a lifetime of being taught that Catholicism is the only true faith, and of seeing the huge numbers of Catholics going to mass alongside them, many Catholics are surprised to see many other churches thrive. Fear, taught through such methods as the catechism… has short-circuited anything more than a mile curiosity by Catholics in other religions. And, somehow, the ex-Catholic, who may have long ago disengaged herself from the church of her upbringing, still carries that fear with her.” (Pg. 179)She reports, “Telling your family, especially your parents, that you no longer attend a Catholic church… is an event loaded with emotion. Catholic parents are taught that they are responsible for their children practicing the faith… [Such news] is greeted with fear, anger, and disappointment; also, there is an overwhelming sense of failure on the part of the mother because the church has traditionally designated her as the caretaker of the family’s spiritual life.” (Pg. 193)She advises, “There are many ways you can become at peace about leaving Catholicism behind. You may wish to express your feelings, from anger to regret, to a counselor or therapist. Or, you might talk about it at AA, in a support group, with your clergyperson, or with a friend. You could write a letter that you have no intention of sending, or you may express yourself through poetry or artwork. There are many self-help methods available today… that help people to let go of their hurtful past so they can live fuller lives.” (Pg. 226-227)She concludes, “It is women who will change Catholicism and other patriarchal religions, either by leaving them to form their own, or by staying and changing them from within. Getting women to begin to question their own misery is the first step toward this freedom. Getting women to explore their own spirituality as they see it---not as it is taught to them---is the next. And perhaps this is the next stage of feminism: a breaking through, or a breaking out, of old molds of worship: abandoning the male-only approach, East and West, for a ‘whole God’—male AND female---approach.” (Pg. 258)This book will be of keen interest to (mostly) women---both Catholics and otherwise---exploring their spiritual/religious options.
