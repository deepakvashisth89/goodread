Incredibly glad to have finally made my way through this entire cornerstone of the Western tradition. Do I envision myself reading through it all again at some point? No, not really. It's the kind of work you're happy to have read, but more so for the purpose of bookmarking all the really pivotal sections to return to in the future. When Augustine isn't indulging his propensity for dry, endless, proto-scholastic lists of refutations and getting tangled up in all sorts of incredibly subsidiary questions (if you thought "How many angels can dance on the head of a needle?" was tedious, just you wait!), he offers some of the most essential and poignant doctrinal formulations of all time. Thomas Merton describes the book as "the autobiography of the Church." And that's just about the finest way of summarizing this mammoth cross-section of pretty much all relevant ideas concerning the nature and end of man. It's easy to summarize the thesis of the work, but to see it threaded through so many vistas of the intellectual, spiritual, and physical world—it's utterly exhausting, dizzyingly illuminating, and indescribably uplifting. And that's just about all I can say.By the way, the most underrated section is Book XI, where he lays an exceptionally imaginative and powerful groundwork for education.
