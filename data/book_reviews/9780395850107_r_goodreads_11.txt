A great correction for historical erasure of the importance of (mostly white) women's relationships in American history. The clarification in the previous sentence is the main reason why this doesn't get 5 stars. None of her chapter-long descriptions of women are about women of color, and the few Black women she does mention get usually about two-three pages each. She also plays down racism in the suffrage movement, claiming that Susan B. Anthony's anger at Black men gaining the right to vote was "not an issue of race," since she had "close relationships" with Black women like Ida B. Wells. She also doesn't question the patronizing attitudes that some of the upper- and middle-class women had towards working-class and immigrant women, referring to them uncritically as "big sisters." Faderman also makes offhand and uncritical references to introducing working women to "culture" and assimilation. While Faderman returns to the theme of these women as "gender inverts," being tomboys at a young age, she never investigates the overlap between what we would now call transgender men and gender-nonconforming women. The flaws in this book, then, I see as mostly being not pointing out the flaws of the women she profiles. That's more understandable since she states at the end of the book that if history has a use, one of its uses is to create role models. Faderman is treating the women in these pages as role models for lesbians, and in the process some of their flaws are painted over. I criticize so much because it is a book that is worth engaging deeply with. And it's much-needed to emphasize how women's relationships with each other could be joyful and positive historically, and how history isn't a straight line of progress.
