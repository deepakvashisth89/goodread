This was very different from the earlier Smiley books, where George was either a main character or somewhat incidental to the story. The protagonist is Ned, a spy coming to the end of his career and now assigned to training new spies. He invites George to speak to his students. Most chapters begin with Smiley addressing the students and answering their questions, often highlighting the moral ambiguity of their work. Then we leave the class for a ramble down Ned's private recollections, reviewing his entire career with the Circus. Smiley is a sympathetic and likable character, sometimes inexorable and ruthless, but no more so than an Oxbridge examiner. Ned less so. Too many details would give things away. Suffice it to say that Ned lacks both Smiley's personal probity and his high level of competence. Despite that, he manages to be a somewhat effective spy, partly with Smiley's help. My least favorite of the books so far.Smiley does get off some good lines (some almost certainly reflect LeCarré's own opinions on the cold war and on our contemporary governments). Some of these are in my updates. I will close with two more:"All I'm really saying, I suppose, is that if the temptation to humanity does assail you now and then, I hope you won't take it as a weakness, but give it a fair hearing.""We have given up far too many freedoms in order to be free. Now we have got to take them back."Good advice for us all, spies or not.
