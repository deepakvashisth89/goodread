I'd also been reviewing it piecemeal; but I've now edited the review into a (hopefully) more coherent whole.Bennett Cerf was one of the founders of Random House, which remains a pillar of American Big Publishing. Not surprisingly, he was also an avid and serious reader, and often an editor of well-regarded anthologies, a number of which remain in print. To encourage the reading of classics, in the mid-20th century Random House brought out its reasonably-priced Modern Library editions of a large number of them; and as part of the project produced "Best of" collections of short fiction by masters of the form. This book is an example of the latter, compiled in 1945 by Cerf himself in collaboration with Van H. Cartmell, a well-known writer and anthologist of the day. Together, they selected 38 of what they felt to be the best-quality examples of the more than 600 stories O. Henry wrote in his relatively short career. Although he had actually been writing stories, sketches and poetry for newspapers (including a short-lived weekly he published himself) as a hobby since the 1880s, most of his fiction was written during and after his prison term that began in 1898, and he died at 48 in 1910. The selections here are taken from the eight collections published during the author's lifetime, starting withCabbages and Kingsin 1904, as well as the three posthumous ones.O. Henry, whose real name was William Sydney Porter (there are conflicting explanations for his pen name), is best viewed as a Realist, and primarily as a regionalist Realist, if we understand the region he particularly wanted to bring to life as his adopted hometown of New York City. (He was actually born in North Carolina, and had moved to Texas for his health in 1882.) Of course, literary critics, for whom New York City is the center of the civilized earth, and "regionalism" is by definition associated with locales away from the center and populated by people different from the supposed norm embodied in that center, don't view him in those terms. But if we shed those blinders, it's clear that NYC plays the same role in his fiction that, for instance, New England, or the rural Midwest, or Appalachia play in the work of other Realist regionalists --a place with a distinct ethos, culture, speech and lifestyle, for which he had a special affection and an object of faithfully bringing to life in fiction. The great majority of his stories, and the majority of those collected here, are set there; and to a great extent that's where his heart was as a writer. But this collection also includes a significant number of tales set in Texas or other parts of his native South, and even some set in Latin America (he lived in Honduras from July 1896 until Jan. 1897).The most characteristic feature of Henry's style is his trademark surprise ending; his tales always feature a twist which is unexpected, but which at the same time is consistent with the internal logic of the story (not dragged in from left field with no grounding, just for the sake of "surprise"). He was American literature's greatest master of that technique (and perhaps the same could be said for world literature, though there I'm less familiar with possible competitors!). But his stories are not simply exercises in technique. He wrote about people with believable personalities and relatable problems and challenges; and while a few of his characters are wealthy (but human the same as the rest of us!), he mostly wrote about simple common, working class or poor people, whose speech, education and living conditions were those of his readers. Moreover, he views his characters sympathetically and compassionately; his heart tends to be with the underdog. (Interestingly, he was one of the few U.S. writers whose work the Soviet government would allow to be published in their country during the Cold War, and he proved to be highly popular with Russian readers.) A number of his stories also revolve around romantic attractions, and he has a very positive view of clean romance for deserving couples and (usually) of married life where the union is loving. (His Honduran sojourn occurred when he was trying to escape arrest for embezzlement at the bank where he worked --he claimed to be innocent, and possibly was; bookkeeping in that particular bank was lamentably slipshod-- but when he learned that his wife was too ill to join him in Honduras, he voluntarily returned to Texas, and cared for her until she died later that year.) At times, the early 20th-century slang and pop-culture references in his dialogue or narration is a bit opaque, and he can strain too hard for humor in places; but usually his diction is very clear and readable, and his humorous strain (though some stories are quite serious!) enhances the tales.This was my third read of this collection (the other two were pre-Goodreads). Six of the stories I'd also encountered in other collections: "The Gift of the Magi" (which I consider a crown jewel of English-language short fiction!), "The Ransom of Red Chief," "The Furnished Room," "The Cop and the Anthem," "A Retrieved Reformation," and "The Last Leaf." (I believe I've commented on or mentioned all or most of these in reviews of the anthologies where they appear; and all of them are gems.) Even more so than most short stories, Henry's work can be hard to comment on very meaningfully without spoilers. But to mention a few of the other selections here, some of my favorites were "The Love-Philtre of Ikey Schoenstein," "Transients in Arcadia," "The Trimmed Lamp," and "The Count and the Wedding Guest." "Roads of Destiny," one of the longer stories, is unique in that it's historical fiction set in 17th-century France (and also one of the very serious tales, as is "The Renaissance at Charleroi"). A few stories have traces of racially insensitive language, but that type of reference is particularly bad in "A Municipal Report," set in Nashville --but that one is also the selection that's, IMO, the most directly subversive of racist attitudes. Although I wasn't very impressed with "The Lost Blend," the only story that I really disliked was "A Harlem Tragedy." That one tries to find humor in brutal wife-beating, which it actually romanticizes and suggests that some women like. With over 600 possibilities to choose from, it's frankly amazing that Cerf and Carmell could have considered this one of the author's "best;" even without having read any of the rest, I'd say it's easily in contention for his worst. But thankfully this isn't at all characteristic of his usual tone and messaging; and overall, I found this a really rewarding collection, that I would recommend to both short fiction fans and students of American literature.Show more
