Without unnecessary drama, in a style that is more reflective and clinical than emotional, Pearl Buck has illustrated the collapse of traditional values through the story of one family during Mao’s communist regime. Unfortunately, the new order proves unable to provide values that replace the social structures it has tried to eradicate. Through the fairly explicit musings of Madame Liang and her son-in-law John, Buck delves into an analysis of how human nature renders Mao’s ideals impossible in the long run. Families crumble, communal farm efforts fail, and this is the socio-political backdrop against which the story unfolds.Madame Liang's "faith in her people" felt naive in such an otherwise logical character. Although I understood her well, I found it difficult to relate to her, probably because she appeared so emotionless. Grace and Mercy, with all their misguided beliefs, felt more human. I particularly enjoyed reading about the juxtaposition of Western and Eastern science models, and I would recommend the book to anyone interested in modern China.(Incidentally, I picked upForeign Babes in Beijingby Rachel DeWosking immediately afterThree Daughters. Although the genre and style are completely different, it is interesting to read about how post-Maoist China in the 1990s is coming to terms with the surging wave of consumerism and the individual's desire for independence.)
