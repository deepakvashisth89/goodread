Wow, this is intense and brilliant! Baldwin has managed to create something in a mere 160 pages that some other authors would struggle to articulate in ten times the length. Complex and nuanced, this is a book where every word counts with so much of the heavy lifting being done either between the lines or via symbolism.I was especially struck by the use of what I'm calling symbolic geographies where places encompass values and emotions far beyond their status as locales. At the heart of this technique is the eponymous Giovanni's room which comes to mean different things for the characters. For our narrator, David, who seems to be suffering from internalised homophobia and thus from a chilling sense of self-loathing and self-alienation, the first time he sees the room he describes it as 'dirty' and finds it chaotic, narrow, messy. It briefly becomes a locus for expansive pleasure, desire and love, but then it shifts again for David and is a site which stifles and strangles him, a place where his desire to be a real 'man', according to conservative conventions on gender construction, cannot breath. The room is both a stand-in for his love affair with Giovanni and an index of his feelings about male same-sex desire.For Giovanni himself, the room equally changes: a place of loneliness to a room that houses his love for David, to a place of heartbreak where he is abandoned. The four walls of the room thus serve as boundaries and borders through which the main characters pass and which also signify figuratively their emotional needs and their acknowledgement and comfort with them, as well as a wider response to cultural ideas about masculinity and male-male love, sex and desire. If the room provides a space where culture falls away and there is freedom to be authentic, that proves too much liberation for David, who craves, apparently, a retreat to a more conventional way of understanding both love and gender and how to express them in the world.This book is often described as a 'gay novel' and perhaps this matters, perhaps it doesn't, but the two main characters both also have sustained sexual relationships with women: David's with Hella seems driven by his need to conform and is as complex, in some ways, as his association with Giovanni. Giovanni's own previous girlfriend seems to stand-in for an easy ways of settling: an Italian rural village, a nurturing woman, children, doing a 'masculine' job and fulfilling a 'masculine' role.In that sense, Paris is a problematic place for both men: on one hand it offers a liberation that can allow for and hold their mutual desire; but it's also a place where beautiful, poor, Giovanni is faced with sexual coercion, predatory men and images of prostitution and death. For David, it's a location which is freeingly 'not America' even while it simultaneously disgusts and terrifies him. It is also the only place where he loves - and where he experiences a 'death of the heart' experience that the text indicates is his for the rest of his life.Hella, as a sort of commentator, adds to the dichotomy of America and Paris by explicitly pleading with David to return to the US where they can resume their 'proper' gender roles and propagate a 'normal' family life: 'Please. I want to go home. I want to get married. I want to start having kids. I want us to live someplace. I want you. Please David.' And the US is also the place from where money comes as David writes to his father for funds to support his life in Paris, finally placatingly offering up a return of marriage to Hella who, he asserts, his father will love.I realise, reading this this back, that what I haven't said is how stunning the writing is: Baldwin's prose and thinking are luminescent, unsentimental, probing and hard-hitting. I loved everything about this book, not least how its impact on me is both intellectual and emotional in equal measures.
