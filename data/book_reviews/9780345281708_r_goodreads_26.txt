I bought this book a long time ago. I think it was in 1986. At the same time I bought two other Cabell novels,The Silver StallionandJurgen.What can I say aboutFigures of Earth? Cabell has been a major presence in my literary imagination for all of those forty years. And this novel, the first in a loose trilogy that includes the books mentioned above, is really an almost perfect fantasy novel.Dreamlike, ironic, witty, rather wistful, maybe too repetitive (which is why it is almost perfect, not entirely perfect) and wise. And it seems to contain the seeds of all fantasy that followed in the 100 years after it was published. And curiously enough, the comedic tone adopted by Cabell seemingly had to be forgotten before it was rediscovered many decades later.Maybe Cabell was simply too far ahead of his time. And yet he was popular, his books sold well, at least for a period. Perhaps the most remarkable thing aboutFigures of Earth(and many of Cabell's other fantasies) is the poignancy of its stoicism. For they are stoic books first and foremost, odd as that may seem. I remember reading an article by the esteemed author Michael Moorcock in which he said that despite his admiration for Cabell, he found him philosophically too negative. "Nothing matters therefore nothing hurts." But I don't find quite that message in Cabell. I find a variant instead. "Everything hurts but we can cope."Dom Manuel is one of the most extraordinary characters in 20th Century fantasy literature. He has been criticised for having no inner voice, no thoughts accessible to us, the readers. But that, for me, is precisely why he works as a picaresque protagonist in his particular world, half hero, half bumpkin. No one can quite work him out. He often tricks the trickster, makes terrible decisions, pays far too high a price in his efforts to reverse the damage of those decisions, and yet the spark of nobility in this squinting naif resurges at the climax with incredible heat. Even when ultimately defeated, something about him remains victorious.The only unhappy thing I can find to say about Cabell is that his writing, brilliant though it is, lacks momentum. The reader reads a chapter and nods to himself when it is finished, quietly awed by the lyricism, the skill, the strangeness, the wisdom, but he feels no overbearing desire to immediately jump into the next chapter. Some writers have momentum, some don't. This has little to do with the quality of the prose, but it does have a bearing on how quickly a book will be read.
