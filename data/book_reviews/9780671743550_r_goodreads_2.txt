Only Barbara Hambly knows, but she does like the way this particular word fits into her vision of the Star Trek universe.Kirk stood in silence for a long time, gazing at the aphotic depths of interstellar space. Remote and untwinkling in the frozen emptiness of that terrible vacuum, stars stared back at him.I must confess from the start that I am notrekkieand I’m simply reading the book for a Goodreads challenge. Yet this reading outside of my comfort zone can often help me discover some pretty sharp and charming stories. As in the present case.I must also confess that I cheated a little. I looked at the very long list of fan-fiction and so-called ‘canon’ novels and I have picked the one book written by one of the writers I already admire. Maybe the reason I liked ‘Ishmael’ so much is the fact that this is much more a Barbara Hambly story than a Star Trek story: my favorite scenes were the ones in the alternate timeline on Earth in 1867 instead of the future chapters aboard the starship ‘Enterprise’. But the two timelines do mesh up nicely together, in the end.—«»—«»—«»—You can call himIshmaelfor the purpose of his visit to our planet, but his real identity is Spock, science officer on the ‘Enterprise’ . He probably needs no introduction to the millions of fans, but since the novel can be read as a standalone adventure, here it is:Spock leaned back a little in his chair, silhouetted against the red lights of the main room: a tall, thin, catlike shape, watching the human race in all its irrational glory with well-connected fascination.We are introduced to Captain Kirk, Spock and the rest of the crew in the Wonder Bar on Starbase Twelve, where they are sipping something calledAldebaran Depth Charges. Spock, of course, is not imbibing, only observing.The peaceful interlude gives way to a desperate pursuit of a suspect Klingon ship into a pulsar anomaly. Spock had infiltrated the Klingon ship and disappears with it , believed dead. All his friends have to go on are a couple of cryptic messages from before he is captured by the Klingons, suggesting a plot to change history through a time loop, or time-warp:“this – retroactive readjustment of history. Writers of speculative fiction revel in it. Scientists, theoretical physicists, examine the mechanical constructs and fear dreadful consequences.”From this point forward, the novel splits into a dual timeline. One has a wounded and amnesiac Spock being discovered in the forest near Seattle in the year 1867. He assumes the identity of Ishmael Marx and starts working with a local businessman named Aaron Stemple, as an accountant.Meanwhile, Kirk and the rest of the crew, with help from several aliens on the base, are trying to solve the puzzle of the messages and the scientific base for time travel.—«»—«»—«»—I really liked the portrait of the small community of Seattle during the Reconstruction period. There’s a side plot here that sort of overwhelms the science-fiction one. It’s about the viability of the settlement: there are too few women among the early settlers. In the aftermath of the Civil War, a daring landowner hatches a plan to visit the East Coast of the US and to convince the single ladies there to move to the rainy wilderness of the North West. Since a lot of the men on the East Coast died in the war, these single ladies are lured by the promise of marriage, and about thirty of them make the trip around Cape Horn. The catch is that the investor, one of the Bolt brothers, needed to finance his expedition and so he made a bet with the same wealthy Aaron Stemple that if any of the ladies remains unmarried by the end of the year, he forfeits his family land of Bridal Veil Mountain. There's rush to the altar for sometimes less than romantic purposes.Ishmael lands right in the middle of this marriage plot and finds it increasingly difficult to maintain his formidable detachment from emotional muddles.“All I have to say is, for a mathematician who’s always talking about logic, you’re getting damn sentimental in your old age.”Ishmael raised an eyebrow at him. “Perhaps I am.”I already knew that one of Barbara Hambly’s strong points is the way she handles mature romance, with a delicate touch, some humor and a lot of restraint. So it’s not a surprise that I loved to spend time in the company of these ladies and of their beaus, especially when I also got the chance to visit San Francisco in its boom period, gamble with Ishmael and the Bolt brothers as he tries to overlay his present vision of the Golden Gate Bay with Spock’s slowly returning memories of the future.Aaron laughed and said, “I’ll bet you never believed this much variety is possible.”Ish shook his head. “They are all human.”This is my favorite joke in the book, a reply to Aaron’s boasting about the diversity of the people one meets on the docks. Since I mentioned diversity, I must also note my favorite alien on Starbase Twelve: a very polite Drelb scientist that is made of some sort of amorphous amoeba gel, feeds through photosynthesis and expresses emotion through colours and smells.Hambly has a deft hand at feminist issues, not only at romance, and this makes it rather difficult to pick only one lady to showcase here. Between Lottie the bar owner in Seattle and the nurse Sarah in San Francisco, who holds a secret doctor degree, I might finally pick Biddy Cloom, or ‘the ace in the hole’ that Aaron Stemple is convinced will help him win the bet for Bridal Veil Mountain:It wasn’t that she was a bad girl, really, thought Stemple. Just plain as a mud fence, and screechy-voiced and featherbrained into the bargain. A good heart with an appalling lack of tact.Aaron is convinced that no man with a sane mind will propose to Biddy. He must have read too few romance novels in his time!Ishmael, with his emancipated view of the role of women in the future, is evidently outraged by the discrimination and not shy about expressing his opinions:“You, and Bolt, have no right to do what you are doing to the girls themselves.”But will he be prepared to offer for Biddy himself, since he appears to be marooned in this timeline for the rest of his life?—«»—«»—«»—I have written only about one timeline, but events are progressing also for Kirk and his allies. They link the Klingon plan to alter reality with the history of the defunct Karsid Empire, whose old records can be researched on Starbase Twelve. Apparently, the Klingons have found some inflexion point in the past that will allow them to gain supremacy in the present timeline. This inflexion point, found also in Spock’s cryptic messages, is a date.“Well then,” McCoy said, “what happened in O.R.-1867-A.D.”If they find the reason why the date is important, they will also find who the Klingons are trying to assassinate in order to change history and they can plot a converging time warp for ‘Enterprise’ in order to stop the Klingons, hopefully.If you want to find out how the two timelines intersect, you need to read the book instead of my review. My final notes are mostly about trivia or funny quirks like the fact the both the Karsids and the Klingons believe the only country on Earth worth visiting is the old US of A, even back in 1867. American exceptionalism strikes again!Apparently, the novel is full of Easter Eggs for the real trekkies and for other sci-fi conventions, with cameo appearances from iconic figures in multiple fictional universes. I missed most of them, but I did cheer for the visit with an old friend I have already met in a couple of historical books:“Mr. Marx – permit me to present you to His Imperial Majesty Norton I, Emperor of the United States and Protector of Mexico, and his two dogs, Bummer and Lazarus.”—«»—«»—«»—Will I continue with my exploration of the Star Trek universe? Or even start watching the TV series? Probably not, but I don’t regret my present visit. I share in the conclusion of a restored Spock, speaking about his alleged misadventures:“On the contrary, Captain, it was a fascinating experience to live as a human among humans. Rather like being a research worker on an anthropological field assignment.”
