Jane Jacobs' bookThe Death and Life of Great American Citieshas been on my mental to-read list since I readThe Power Broker, which was long ago. It was not the book I imagined it to be, Robert Moses here lurks mostly unmentioned darting behind some of the paragraphs, very rarely making an appearance in an actual sentence.Jacobs view of the city struck me as Darwinian - the more life there is, the more life there can be. The city is an ecosystem, and diversity promotes diversity. The problem in her view is that zoning regulations, slum clearances, road building - particularly driving express routes through neighbourhoods, and economic specialisation tend to have the opposite effect, they reduce diversity and cause parts of the city to die back.In part she attributes this to a tradition of urban reformers and town planners, from Ebenezer Howard and his garden cities onwards, who did not much like urban life and thought of the small town or the village as the ideal human community, there influence for her is much like having Teetotallers running the bars, breweries and distilleries.She also consciously or unconsciously draws on the idea of complex systems (p.452) - the city is a complex, diverse system, it's management and government require both an overview and detailed local knowledge. Political and administrative structures tend to facilitate the first, but not the second, leading to top down interventions whether slum clearance or cutting new roads through the cityscape either causing more harm than good or shifting the problem aroundliterally in the case of slum landlords who are compensated for the loss of their property on the basis of their income - so the more squalidly overcrowded their property was, the more they are compensated and with that money they buy property elsewhere in the city and they have a dataset of tenants they can use to rapidly fill up the new property with the newly homeless. She says:"The administrative structure itself is at fault because it has been adapted beyond the point that mere adaptations can serve. This is how human affairs often evolve. There comes a point, at increased levels of complication, when actual invention is required"(p.428).All in all I felt Jacobs was stronger on the death side of the equation, she is very interesting on what was going wrong in the US city at a granular level, why certain parks are hardly used, or only by the kinds of users who frighten the rest away, why certain libraries are busier than others, why some streets are safer or feel safer than others. I was not overwhelmed by the life side, which seemed limited to the idea that urban government needed to be redesigned away from vertical silos in favour of something more horizontal and decentralised in its structure.She has an interesting environmental edge to her thinking (pp.459-461), the city in her view works in harmony with the countryside, the enemy of nature is the suburban sprawl:does anyone suppose that, in real life, answers to any of the great questions that worry us today are going to come out of homogeneous settlements?"(p.462)I found it very interesting - though over long, but I wondered if her insights were most applicable to the USA and other colonial/settler societies which from their beginnings were keen to control space, and limit who could live where and to segregate. She seemed much less interested in public transport and less keen on public housing than I imagine her contemporaries in Europe might have been. Her book reminded me very strongly of a saying attributed to Dostoevsky that the two points closest together on a circle were also the two furthest apart, because in many ways the operation and activities of US city authorities in the middle of the twentieth century sounded remarkably Stalinist to me with idealisation of big concrete prospects designed for motorised traffic flanked by massive concrete blocks. Curiously after departing New York, Jacobs and family settled in Toronto, which is not generally held up as an exemplar of the kind of organic city that Jacobs argues in favour of."Dull, inert cities, it is true, do contain the seeds of their own destruction and little else. But lively, diverse, intense cities contain the seeds of their own regeneration, with energy enough to carry over for problems and needs outside themselves"As chance would have it this was also one of the book's on the late Ted Schmeckpeper'swant to read list, I imagine that he would have been positive about it, despite the glaring absence of discussion about the role of baseball pitches in major cities as cultural and economic anchor points for communities, perhaps too he might have added his own reflections on US and Australian cities to those made by Jacobs.
