The book is an intriguing integration of the real Reformation era Europe with a fantasy world; I frequently had the impression that the author knew her history, as she played with the interactions of various rulers and factions. The Roman Catholic church features prominently, but there are also Hussites and Lutherans and other early separatist groups. In the midst of it all is a small mountainous country clearly in the Alps somewhere in the midst of Bavaria and Bohemia and perhaps Austria, the Dukedom of Sumava and its Duke Ottokar. Small independent countries being offensive to huge empires by their very existence, Sumava is fighting for its life as the story opens, and its Duke, fleeing through the woods, calls for any help he can get from the trees or anyone else--and help comes from the trees, from what he would call elves, and from the leader of the Wild Hunt. This introduces him to a vast supernatural world which he had previously only feared might exist, but he falls in love with an elf maiden and discovers that he also has "talents" or "powers".This was my second time through the book, and I remembered much that was going to happen fairly early. Yet that to some degree enhanced my enjoyment, because I had not realized how well much of it is anticipated. It keeps coming back to his relationship with the trees--the first to help save him from his pursuers at the beginning, who give him significant wealth, who help him learn his powers, and who ultimately embrace him within themselves for the protection of Sumava. Yet it is also about the relationships between humans and elves, as he marries that elf maid and against all expectation has not merely a child but four children, one of whom inherits so much "elfin" power as to be tapped to be the future ruler of the elves.The supernatural realm was nicely integrated. As the elf maid Zoeminera tries to explain "where" she has taken him and why it looks just like his own country but without any human impact, I could not help thinking that it was like sliding into a parallel dimension--yet the author carefully avoids any such science fiction language and instead simply has her characters refer to them as "wheres". The point is made that the elves do not call themselves "elves" or "fairies" but prefer the name "Alsi", although it also appears that there are several races of elves and only these taller ones use that name; it is also clear that the magic abilities are like talents, that different individuals have different skills to different abilities. Zoe is a healer like her father; her brother Kalli has no such gift, but is a hunter.My wife complained that she did not find the thought life of the central character credible, that the female author made a mistake in pretending she understood how or what men think well enough to create a story around the internal viewpoint of a male protagonist. I did not have that problem, but perhaps I am not so critical on that count, and I did not ask what aspects bothered her.The book's treatment of Christianity seems fair and underplayed; differences between denominations are recognized but not stressed, and it is apparent that Duke Ottokar is not Roman Catholic although whether he is Utraquist or one of the few other denominations was not clear to me. It strikes a nice balance between the Duke's personal life, the political world by which he is surrounded, and the supernatural adventures in which he is frequently involved.
