Frank Norris was a master at painting emotions with words. The titular character is a man few would care to dine with, but Norris gets the reader to sympathize for him. You see, much like most writers circa late 19th to early 20th century, human nature was best explored through the environment of the characters (naturalism). In McTeague's case, he was an affluent dentist from San Franciso that falls in love with the wrong girl; some would argue that the wrong girl falls in love with McTeague. Any way you slice the cake, you still have two people trapped in a marriage that is slowly burning toward becoming a firey inferno. Filled with political and social commentary, greed, sex, folly, hate, jealousy, and corruption, these all meld together to bring the novel toward an ending that is not only harrowing, but, to this reader, one that still has not been surpassed since my reading of this almost seven years ago.Challenge: Read this book. If the ending doesn't knock your socks off I'll...I'll...well, I'll be wrong I guess. But you won't be able to say I didn't tell you about a great ending that is the perfect metaphor for what we call the rat-race of human life.HIGHEST POSSIBLE RECOMMENDATION
