I won’t bother giving an academic review of Michael Harner’s “The Way of the Shaman” here, but will provide a review that is personal regarding my own life experiences. I used to live in Indian Country on one of the poorest reservations in the United States, and my experiences—including what I saw with my own eyes—have naturally affected my perceptions. In particular, my life and spiritual experiences on the reservation continues to color my view of and relationships with other anthropologists and with Western consumption of Shamanic practices.I’ve never met Michael Harner, worked with him professionally (in anthropology), or read his works. His book “The Way of the Shaman” is my first time reading anything written by him.Many decades ago, I read Leah Rabin’s “Rabin: Our Life, His Legacy.” I particularly remember how Rabin talked about how she was a fan of Castaneda’s work and loved his books. When she met him, though, Rabin discovered that Castaneda was, at least from what I remember, antisemitic and particularly discriminating against Israelis. I don’t know much about Harner except that he was Castaneda’s defender regarding Castaneda’s work (which has been under scrutiny even prior to his death); what Harner’s opinions were (he’s now deceased) I can’t attest to.Harner’s goal in writing this book was to,“…help contemporary Westerners, for the first time, to benefit from this knowledge in their quest to supplement the approaches of modern technological medicine…” (pg. ii).Great. But instead of focusing on a single shamanic practice of a particular tribe, Harner sought to universalize all of shamanic practices, including those of the Northern Plains where I used to live. In short, he stripped all of the cultural nuances—except for Western ones, of course.For example, throughout the book, Harner continuously references “primitive peoples,” but then tells readers to, “…create a “cone” of power…” (pg. 185), which is a direct reference to modern-day Wicca. In fact, this term, ‘cone of power’, is frequently referenced in Wiccan books. What I found equally interesting is that one of the courses on Harner’s website is called “Wheel of the Year,” which is obviously a Wiccan reference.So, how did Harner actually come up with universal shamanism? Unfortunately, it was when he was conducting fieldwork. After a vision, he spoke first to the shaman, and then two missionaries “confirmed,” the similarity:“…The couple at the mission, Bob and Millie, were a cut above the average evangelists sent from the United States: hospitable, humorous, and compassionate. I told them my story. When I described the reptile with water gushing out of his mouth, they exchanged glances, reached for their Bible, and read to me the following line from Chapter 12 in the Book of Revelation: And the serpent cast out of his mouth water as a flood. They explained to me that the word “serpent” was synonymous in the Bible with the words “dragon” and “Satan.” I went on with my narrative. When I came to the part about the dragon-like creatures fleeing an enemy somewhere beyond the Earth and landing here to hide from their pursuers, Bob and Millie became excited and again read me more from the same passage in the Book of Revelation…” (pg. 6).Did the shaman and the evangelists see the same thing? No. One can find commonalities in anything if you try hard enough, and religious people whose primary goal is to convert someone will **always** find commonalities. In fact, we were told this as undergraduates.As I was reading this, it was clear to me that the shaman was pulling Harner’s leg. First, he handed over an expensive rifle AND the ammunition for the so-called shaman’s “training,” which included both Harner and the shaman taking ayahuasca together. Then, how inconvenient, after Harner drank it, the shaman backed out and,“…had decided not to participate after all…” (pg. 2).But the “shaman” wasn’t finished with Harner just yet. The shaman and his friend then led Harner on a wild goose chase through the jungle while,“…Sometimes the others would snack on the smoked boiled manioc or smoked meat that they carried in their monkey-skin pouches. I, however, was forbidden to eat solid food. “You must suffer,” Tsangu explained, “so that the grandfathers will take pity on you. Otherwise, the ancient specter will not come.”…” (pg. 11).Still unfinished, they then **purposefully** lost him in the forest and wouldn’t answer his cries. I could go on and on, but I’ll spare you all. I have watched **innumerable** people arrive at the reservation with pure hearts and intentions, and many of them were abused by unscrupulous people claiming to be medicine men. There **are** good and honorable medicine men, but you have to spend considerable time on the reservation to discover who they are; it’s a trust relationship, and it doesn’t happen overnight. And relationships matter. A lot, especially among tribes.Harner’s method is a shamanism devoid of cultural context. Are there some cross-cultural similarities? Sure! Anthropologists have known this for eons as you can see in HRAF (Human Relations Area Files). But even with all of those similarities, you still—no matter how hard you try—can’t fit a square inside a circle. For example, a Mormon, a Baptist, and a Christian Scientist might all believe in Jesus, but they are **not** the same religions—and they will tell you that.Naturally, after having handed over “payment” for his training as a shaman, Harner also decided to get into the business himself. After looking at the website, I **finally** found the costs that they were charging for **training** people to be shamans, and it ONLY costs between $160-$295.Does this mean that Harner’s instructions in this book can’t be followed and utilized? No, that is absolutely not what I’m saying. But what I AM saying is that you don’t have to pay people excessive money to find your path in life. There are, in fact, many paths from which to choose from, and they are ALL different, despite what Harner would have you believe.
