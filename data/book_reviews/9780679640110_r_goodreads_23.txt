I’ve read and re-read the parts of Varieties that concern the religion of “healthy-mindedness,” the “sick soul,” and the “divided self” many times. On this read, I focused on “mysticism,” “conversion,” and “saintliness.”

James famously redefined mysticism as a private, individual experience, claiming that it universally involves (1) ineffability; (2) noetic quality; (3) transience; and (4) passivity. Thus he helped to create a disciplinary fault line between interior, contemplative mystical states and performative, culturally embedded ritual. To me, this seems like a mistake. First, because mystics don’t have their experiences in vacuums, but often, if not always, within some sort of conceptual and interpretive frameworks. Blake saw visions of angels, not of Kali, because he was raised in a Christian, not a Hindu, culture. Second, because even persons performing functional rituals may be having profound ecstatic experiences. We don’t have medieval first-person phenomenological accounts of seiðr, but that doesn’t mean the Norse weren’t having private, internal experiences as intense as those spoken of by memoir-writing Christian mystics. But I’ll have to read more on the differences between mysticism and shamanism before I can argue that the category should be more inclusive. Maybe I am misunderstanding something – I totally welcome opinions on this.

James’ lectures on conversion cover both gradual and sudden conversion. He argues that those with a relatively narrow attentively conscious mental field, coupled with a highly developed subliminal mental field, are more prone to sudden conversions. If you have lots of unconscious material going on under the surface, its rising into consciousness can feel like it came out of nowhere, and you might think, “God talked to me all of a sudden!” I probably fit this narrow-conscious-field mental category, since my thoughts and feelings seem to surprise me an awful lot. Still waiting on my sudden conversion, though ;p

Finally, saintliness. James covers a lot of ground, but the most interesting to me was asceticism as a form of liberation. If you’re no longer beholden to physical comforts (or even survival), who can take anything away from you? Who can frighten you? No authority stands above you, not even a religious institution.

Apparently, was one of Wittgenstein’s favorite books—he even brought it with him to the front in WW1. Not something I would’ve expected from Ludwig! But then it’s difficult not to love William James. Even in disagreeing with him I still appreciated the talent of his writing (better than his brother’s – sorry not sorry) and the palpable kind-heartedness of his thinking.
