GREEK AND ROMAN SIEGE MACHINERY 399 BC - AD 363 is a great companion to another book by the author: GREEK AND ROMAN ARTILLERY 399 BC - AD 363. If you are going to try to take a fortified city in those ancient times and didn't have the time to sit around and starve them out, one would need the tools to get through the defensive walls. This book tells you what was available at the time.

Ancient cities were located in strategic spots or simply grew in desired locations along trade routes or around harbors. If the terrain favored the defense then the walls were built to take advantage of this. If not, attempts were made to alter the terrain such as the digging of ditches in front of the walls. So the first step in breaching the defenses would be filling in the ditches. Bare in mind that the defenders on the walls would not be sitting idly by while this was happening. No, they would be sending down a shower of arrows and rocks on the enemy. So what the ancient Greeks would do was build a tortoise.

A ditch filling tortoise was a huge wedge shaped mobile defensive work that they could roll up to the ditch. Those inside could then dump baskets of earth, rock, and logs into the ditch to fill it in. Those working underneath would be protected by the outer shell which was composed of layers of different material. The frame and protective shell was composed of wood. Over the wood would be bundles of rags or brush to soften the impact of rocks. Sometimes a layer of a hair and clay mixture was used as well. Covering this was wet hides for protection against fire.

All the while this was going on archers and men manning the catapults and ballistas in the siege towers would be taking the defenders on the walls under fire. Siege towers were huge towers taller than the city walls that were rolled up close enough for their weapons to be in range. They were built in a similar manner as the tortoise. Once the ditch was filled in a siege tower would be rolled forward and a drawbridge on the tower lowered to allow the men inside to go over the wall.

Sometimes, for whatever reason, a (battering) ram would be used. In this case the ram would be employed where it could make a hole in the wall maybe halfway up instead of at the base as the base was the thickest part of the wall. (Note: The gates were usually the strongest part of the defenses.)

Alexander and his father made extensive use of such machines to conquer cities.

The Romans, being marvelous engineers, also filled in ditches under cover. They had their own version of siege towers and rams, but they pretty much functioned in the same way.

There were plenty of drawings and even some great artwork in color to illustrate these machines.
