In 1963, the Bronx Zoo in New York held an exhibit called "The Most Dangerous Animal in the World."  The title was printed in red on the top of an empty cage, and behind its bars stood a mirror. Visitors of the exhibit who peered into the cage in the hope of seeing an exotic animal were surprised to find nothing but their own reflection staring back at them. The exhibit came to mind while I was reading 𝐓𝐡𝐞 𝐆𝐢𝐫𝐥 𝐍𝐞𝐱𝐭 𝐃𝐨𝐨𝐫.
If I had a shred of doubt left in me regarding who is the most dangerous animal on earth, this book threw it straight into the fire. The real monsters are among us. They can be your next-door neighbors, your coworkers, or even your classmates. Humans at sight, beasts of inimitable foulness at heart. It's a terrifying and bleak realization, one that I came to terms with many moons ago, or at least I thought so, until I read this book.


Based on a true story of the shocking abuse and murder of sixteen years old Sylvia Likens, 𝐓𝐡𝐞 𝐆𝐢𝐫𝐥 𝐍𝐞𝐱𝐭 𝐃𝐨𝐨𝐫 is one of the most excruciating, heartbreaking, and disturbing books I've ever read, and still, I found it difficult to put it down, even during moments that felt like a cacophonous aura of evil, pain and sheer anxiety. 


With exceptional skills, 𝘑𝘢𝘤𝘬 𝘒𝘦𝘵𝘤𝘩𝘶𝘮 has woven a study in violence and grief. His incredible, compelling writing  has swept me into a world where the once harmonious deceiving strings of a quiet suburb on hot summer days have been tuned to terrifying, painful melodies which have turned darker and darker with each page. A world where a grown-up adult who was supposed to guide and protect kids manipulated them to participate in despicable, horrifying acts. I found myself gazing into the ugliest, most horrific psyche through the cracks in soma of a 12-year-old narrator named David. I felt his moral conflicts while he watched and saw not only the  brutal violence and humiliation inflicted on Meg by Ruth, her sadistic caretaker and Satan's spawns, aka, the kids, but also, the death of his own childhood and  innocence. I truly don't remember when was the last time I felt such an urge to save a character in a book, and when was the last time I wanted to hurt one so badly.


There is something to be said about an author like 𝘒𝘦𝘵𝘤𝘩𝘶𝘮 who possessed a rare ability to provoke such intense reactions from a reader and make one feel almost obligated to sit and reflect for a moment, or at least that was how i felt, and now, I can say wholeheartedly that this brilliantly written, unforgettable novel has left me emotionally drained and haunted by the echo of many moral questions that most likely would never be answered.
