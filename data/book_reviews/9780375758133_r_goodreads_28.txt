You don’t have to look very far to see that blackness is still a very fraught topic. In March 2021 Candace Owens, an African American author and commentator, was talking about Meghan Markle and said “she isn’t black enough to be a victim of racism”. Prince Harry himself said this January that Meghan is “not visibly black”. On the Oprah Winfrey show Meghan herself said that one unnamed Royal person raised concerns about how dark her unborn baby’s skin was going to be. We see how the person of Meghan has freaked out so many people! She’s black – no, she isn’t black enough….!

Then there was the if-it-wasn’t-so-offensive-it-would-be-funny scene at the Buckingham Palace reception last November. For those who missed this little gem, the two main characters were Ngozi Fulani, a black charity leader, and Lady Susan Hussey, one of the late Queen’s “ladies-in-waiting”, who was 83 at the time, and who is a close friend of our current King, and whose late husband was chairman of the BBC.



Here is the conversation :

Lady Susan: Where are you from?

Ms Fulani: Sistah Space. [This was the name of her charity]

Lady Susan: No, where do you come from?

Ms Fulani: We’re based in Hackney.

Lady Susan: No, what part of Africa are YOU from?

Ms Fulani: I don’t know, they didn’t leave any records.

Lady Susan: Well, you must know where you’re from, I spent time in France. Where are you from?

Ms Fulani: Here, UK.

Lady Susan: NO, but what Nationality are you?

Ms Fulani: I am born here and am British.

Lady Susan: No, but where do you really come from, where do your people come from?

Ms Fulani: ‘My people’, lady, what is this?

Lady Susan: Oh I can see I am going to have a challenge getting you to say where you’re from. When did you first come here?

Ms Fulani: Lady! I am a British national, my parents came here in the 50s when …

Lady Susan: Oh, I knew we’d get there in the end, you’re Caribbean!

Ms Fulani: No Lady, I am of African heritage, Caribbean descent and British nationality.

In this ancient posh dame’s brain Ms Fulani looked so obviously black she must be African and couldn’t possibly be British, hence the amazingly rude and racist questions and the final Ah! So you’re Caribbean! (Not British! I knew it!).

This short novel Passing is about the cringe-inducing topic of black/mixed race people in America pretending to be white and getting away with it (or not). The reasons for doing this are too obvious to mention, but whole thing is like skating on thin ice your whole life, living with the knowledge that somebody who knows “who you really are” could pop up from the past any day of the week and detonate your pleasant white life.

Nella Larsen employs a melodramatic device in having her passing character Clare Kendry married to a vile racist



So this is a plunge into the conflicted psychological ramifications of Irene, the friend of Clare - how a black person who “pass” but chooses not to regards one who has decided to live as a white person – to say it’s complicated would be a more than considerable understatement. I thought it could have been more fleshed out, but certainly it’s pretty memorable.

Recommended.

3.5 stars
