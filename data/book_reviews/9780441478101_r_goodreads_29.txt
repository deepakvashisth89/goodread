The Left Hand of Darkness was first published in 1969. But I must have read it for the first time about three decades after its publication, and I would have never suspected it haven’t been written but a few months before.

Since that first reading, I have come back to this novel many times, and it always surprises me how something happening in the world at the moment I start reading feels deeply linked to Ursula Le Guin’s words here.

The premise of the book is as simple as its development is vast and complicated: what if there were no genders? What if humans only took on male or female characteristics when they went into heat once a month? What if by making sex unavoidable it was then kept separate from everything else? What would a society like this look like?

Well, that’s the reality in the icy planet of Gethen.

We are introduced to this planet by Genly Ai, a solitary envoy from the Ekumen —a sorts of League of All Worlds. Ai is tall, black and male, nothing like the small, plump, brown-skinned, hermaphroditic people from Gethen. He lands on Gethen—his mission is to evaluate the entrance of Gethen to the Ekumen—at a time when the two hostile nations that control the planet, Karhide and Orgoreyn, are getting ready for war. His arrival as a envoy of the Ekumen, and all it represents, only feeds the hostilities between the two states.

But Ai, his name almost a cry of pain, brings with him the standard prejudices of a gendered society. And his prejudices blind him to the possibility of war, as he concludes that though politicking, double-dealing and status anxiety plague Gethen’s societies, they have never mobilized into war. “They behaved like animals, in that respect; or like women,” Ai notes.

And here is where Le Guin’s genius permeates the pages to a degree we forget is there and we simply don’t see that perhaps it's not the lack of an aggressive and imposing male gender that keeps the peace, but the ice age that has Gethen (which literally means Winter) in its grasp.

The Left Hand of Darkness has some of the most chilling descriptions of freezing conditions you'll find in fiction—the howling gales too loud to shout over; the white-outs in which all sense of direction disappears; the rations painfully shrinking; breathing knives as the air freezes your nostrils and lungs. They are a superb example of Le Guin’s talent in pulling the reader in, letting us feel, more than imagine, how it is to live always in the cold, in a world that doesn’t understand warm.

Isolation, exile, betrayal are this novel's great subjects. The meaning of being a traitor is explored throughout to the book. With the relationship between Ai and his reluctant helper, Karhider Estraven, Le Guin sets this question against a background of state-fuelled anxieties about the loss of power and national identity and offers us answers from all possible angles. But solitude plays an significant role in their relationship. When politician Estraven takes up Genly Ai's cause, he’s exiled from his country, and both men are forced to survived the hostile terrain without help. For me, the most extraordinary section of the story is the grueling three-month trek the two of them make through arctic wastes to get back to Estraven's country, and contact Ai's spaceship. Out on the ice, with only sledge and tent, they become equals, then friends. Their survival is a miracle but also a feat of human ingenuity, planning, and determination.

This book marked me, it made me the huge Sci-Fi fan I am. It also introduced me to the incredible works of Ursula Le Guin, and for that I’ll always be grateful. But beyond my personal feelings on this book, it is as it has been recognized: a master piece of speculative fiction. The anthropological and even economic notions displayed by Le Guin’s in portraying such realistic world under such unrealistic conditions are hard to find in other books.
