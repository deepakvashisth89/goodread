Cormac McCarthy is a connoisseur of words and the rhythms of speech. I can't think of anyone who writes like McCarthy (who recently died). He needs an indulgent reader, someone who is willing to give him free reign. Here's a quote."He lived for another five hours and died sometime before daybreak unattended. They hadnt even taken off his shoes. Charlie had gone home and Suttree and the mother sat in the little waiting room. When the doctor came out and told them he was dead Billy Ray's mother began to cry very quietly. She sat there with her chin quivering and she shook her head slowly from side to side over her dead warrior. Suttree touched her shoulder but she waved him away and she did not look up."He walked out of the hospital and across the wet grass toward the road. Very slowly the lights of the city were going out, the billboards, the streetlamps. He crossed the river by the high iron bridge, past the orchards in the dark, lights in the water upstream and the sky paling and the night and its disciplines draining away leaving the barren trees as lack as iron and a paper city rising in the dawn. A great stillness had fallen. He walked through the dead gray streets. A newspedlar was opening his bale of papers at the corner. The streetsweepers had passed and in the black gutterwater the lights from the polelamps lay like pietins among the darker neon bleedings." (p. 377)Suttree's friend has just died. How does McCarthy show grief, as opposed to merely saying there was grief? Please reread the second quoted paragraph.There is no plot; the characters drive the story, poor people living on the river in Knoxville, Tennessee, in the nineteen-fifties. All speak with this strangely articulate low-brow vocabulary: ex-cons, drinkers of moonshine, small-time crooks, broken and living on pennies.There is a kindness among the abysmally poor river folk that is often moving. Suttree's nature is both generous and profligate. The book at times serves up outrageous dark humor, but this is offset by filth and decay and poor hygiene and death. The book is not, as some have written, a comedy. It is its own signature kind of pungent literary fiction.Scenes that will set your hair on fire include: "part-time-catamite" Leonard's implication of Suttree in a plan to dispose of his father, dead six-months, so his mother can keep receiving the old man's welfare; the lethal bar fight in which Suttree is brained with a floorbuffer; the meeting with the old black witch whose prophesy Suttree declines to hear and, it could be argued, bolts town to avoid; his aimless wandering in the hills above Gatlinburg, all macabre delusion; and, after heavy rain, a murderous mudslide.I rememberV.S. Naipaulin an interview extolling the virtues of the simpler word. Of course, he was right. It was McCarthy's wish however to break with that esthetic standard as often as he could — somehow it works, but I couldn't say how. My pleasure in the writing at times verges on the ecstatic.I gladly recommend this novel and — in no particular order —The Crossing,The Passenger,Cities of the Plain,All the Pretty Horses,The Road, andBlood Meridian.
