This first of two volumes concerning women's situation in the world they live in—and have lived in—reasonably exhaustively informs us of their position historically. Beginning with this foundational element, biology, De Beauvoir then tells us about what life has been like for women historically and mythologically, that is to say, in what ways she, as a woman, has crossed and marked consciences.This book is well done. It approaches the woman's condition by examining what determines her in the first place, namely, the biological fact. We thus learn how women, by menstruation and pregnancy, are placed very quickly (from puberty) in the face of immanence, while men, not having to live through such "traumatic" events physically, discover themselves somewhat as airy and transcendent. The woman is then regularly brought back to her immanence and gets stuck, believing that her life consists only of that. While the man not feeling the weight of these chains can more easily develop in transcendent spheres and believe, in turn, that he is not, or so little, subjected to the vagaries of the flesh. Then, continuing with historical considerations, De Beauvoir demonstrates how this physiological inequality, among others, has been used bitterly to enslave and subject women through the ages and times. Thus, men, more often than not, in seeking to assert their will to power, had no difficulty crushing women and relegating them to roles and positions of subordinates or even, in specific contexts, enslaved people.In the same way, the woman is seen as determined as otherness, and she is assigned the status of the Other, that is to say, of a different consciousness, separate but still very present and able to enter into conflict with the subject who meets it. The conscience of man, seeking to become sovereign, thus worked to reduce this Other, to dominate it, to be able to reign. She was also the ideal target for projecting everything we could not accept at home: fear, disgust, uncertainty, sentimentality, and so on. This work demonstrates the prevalence of various myths about women, which have existed and continue to exist. We encounter them in the forms of witches, enchantresses, sinners, beauties, angels, nymphs, and divine creations, among others. Unfortunately, these myths are placed along the path leading to the development of women, as well as the men who interact with them, and will always do so.This reading was very beneficial to me. It allowed me to consider this reality of the woman and her being in the world. The work comprises 400 pages. De Beauvoir takes the trouble to explain and detail her position with examples and historical facts. We could blame him, and this would not be inaccurate, for a certain tendency to trace in black pencil elements of biological or historical life that leave little room for nuance. Yet, exercise is necessary for those who seek to shed a little more light on the question, and, in the end, it is not so far from reality, as it is supported by research and sober reflection. In closing, I would say that this book is essential for anyone interested in feminism.
