“No Two Human Beings Understand Each Other. They Can Understand but Their Own Voices.”This bitter truth – optimists might please themselves by regarding it as an assumption merely –, as voiced by the protagonist’s daughter Nina, seems to be the upshot of Joseph Conrad’s first novel,Almayer’s Folly, published in 1895, and to me it is already redolent of the better-known quotation from Conrad’s masterpieceHeart of Darkness:”It is impossible to convey the life-sensation of any given epoch of one's existence — that which makes its truth, its meaning — its subtle and penetrating essence. It is impossible. We live, as we dream — alone.”If we accept this pessimistic outlook on human relations as a truth, then we will have to brace ourselves for far-reaching consequences not only with regard to daily life communication but also, to a much larger degree, to communication across cultural and ethnic borders. Last not least, literature itself will be affected because if Nina’s and Marlowe’s statements are true, how then can an author hope to put his ideas across to his readers, and this is probably where Conrad’s famous dictum of a writer writing only half a book, the other half being with the reader, comes into play.Definitely,Almayer’s Follyis one of those books where the reader is called on to do a lot of decoding work and where he never knows if he succeeded in cracking the code. Conrad tells the story of the Dutch trader Kaspar Almayer, who has never seen his native European country but idealizes it as a place of splendour and civilization, and who allows himself to be married to a Malaysian girl, by the redoubtable English captain Tom Lingard, and to be planted into a trading post on the Pantai river, in the jungle of Borneo. Lingard has great plans with his adopted son-in-law, whose mind he poisons with stories of a hidden gold mine in the depth of the Bornean jungle in quest of which he spends the major part of Almayer’s earnings before he finally disappears in Europe – where he tries to collect enough money for yet another expedition in order to find his El Dorado – and is never heard of anymore. Almayer, “the only white man on the east coast”, is left to his own devices, dreaming of a glorious future for himself and, especially his half-caste daughter Nina, whose mother he cannot understand and therefore despises, whereas his actual situation is becoming more and more hopeless. Not only is he driven out of business by the Arab merchant Abdullah, but the Dutch, who exercise colonial power in that region, are not interested in the least in the welfare of their feckless compatriot whom they regard as a harmless fool. His neighbour, the local Malay potentate Lakamba, connives in his downfall with the Arab traders but suddenly his fortune seems to take a turn for the better with the arrival of the Malay Dain Maroola, a dashing and enterprising youth from a noble family. Almayer regards Dain as a perfect ally in the quest for the legendary gold treasure that is to make his dreams come true, and in order to earn enough money for another expedition into the jungle, the Dutch trader helps Dain run illegal gunpowder. Dain’s gunpowder trafficking is found out by the Dutch, however, and the young man returns to Almayer’s place, ignoring all the danger, in order to convince Nina to flee with him. Almayer is rudely awoken from his dreams of grandeur when learning of his daughter’s being in love with Dain, but although he swears never to forgive Nina what he regards as her betrayal of him, he still agrees to help the two young people escape from the Dutch. With Nina being gone, nothing is left in Almayer’s life, and he dedicates the paltry remainder of his life to trying to forget her.Although barely 200 pages long, Conrad’s first novel is fraught with meaning and symbolism and already contains many of the themes that are going to play a role in Conrad’s later works. We can read the story simply as the tragedy of a plodding soldier of fortune, whose racial prejudices contribute to his final downfall. Being faced with Lingard’s idea of becoming his son-in-law and partner, Almayer reacts thus:”Startled by the unexpected proposal, Almayer hesitated, and remained silent for a minute. He was gifted with a strong and active imagination, and in that short space of time he saw, as in a flash of dazzling light, great piles of shining guilders, and realised all the possibilities of an opulent existence. The consideration, the indolent ease of life—for which he felt himself so well fitted—his ships, his warehouses, his merchandise (old Lingard would not live for ever), and, crowning all, in the far future gleamed like a fairy palace the big mansion in Amsterdam, that earthly paradise of his dreams, where, made king amongst men by old Lingard’s money, he would pass the evening of his days in inexpressible splendour. As to the other side of the picture—the companionship for life of a Malay girl, that legacy of a boatful of pirates—there was only within him a confused consciousness of shame that he a white man—Still, a convent education of four years!—and then she may mercifully die. He was always lucky, and money is powerful! Go through it. Why not? He had a vague idea of shutting her up somewhere, anywhere, out of his gorgeous future. Easy enough to dispose of a Malay woman, a slave, after all, to his Eastern mind, convent or no convent, ceremony or no ceremony.”Almayer may love his daughter but he sees the money he is seeking as a means of making his white fellow-men forget about the fact that Nina’s mother is of Malay origins, and he never even has the idea of asking Nina why, all of a sudden, she had decided to abandon her education amongst white people and to come back to the old place in the jungle, there being a terrible humiliation at the bottom of that story which Almayer does not want to know anything about. When he finds out that Nina plans to leave him for the sake of Dain Maroola, who, as a Malay, does not meet Almayer’s social standards as a potential husband for his daughter, the father reproaches Nina:”’[…] You have no heart, and you have no mind, or you would have understood that it was for you, for your happiness I was working. I wanted to be rich; I wanted to get away from here. I wanted to see white men bowing low before the power of your beauty and your wealth. Old as I am I wished to seek a strange land, a civilisation to which I am a stranger, so as to find a new life in the contemplation of your high fortunes, of your triumphs, of your happiness. For that I bore patiently the burden of work, of disappointment, of humiliation amongst these savages here, and I had it all nearly in my grasp.’”,whereupon Nina reveals to him the inner struggles she has fought between her love for him and her feelings for her mother:”’[…] No two human beings understand each other. They can understand but their own voices. You wanted me to dream your dreams, to see your own visions—the visions of life amongst the white faces of those who cast me out from their midst in angry contempt. But while you spoke I listened to the voice of my own self; then this man came, and all was still; there was only the murmur of his love. You call him a savage! What do you call my mother, your wife?’””’Then I saw that you could not understand me; for was I not part of that woman? Of her who was the regret and shame of your life? I had to choose—I hesitated. Why were you so blind? Did you not see me struggling before your eyes? But, when he came, all doubt disappeared, and I saw only the light of the blue and cloudless heaven—”However, Conrad’s world is not all cast in black and white but rather in black and different, though usually darker, shades of grey, and therefore Mrs. Almayer is not primarily the helpless victim she would have to be shown as according to modern-day politically correct tastes, but she, too, despises her husband for his weakness and she does her best to estrange her daughter from her father’s ways and love, and to further Nina’s falling in love with Dain, deriving her own financial profit from their union.Conrad very cleverly introduces Mrs. Almayer as a little girl, found by Lingard in a piratical prau, huddled under the corpses of the defeated. Lingard adopts the little child as his daughter and sends her to a convent to be educated there, and the reader is at first likewise inclined to unthinkingly take the view of the white captain that this innocent little girl must be relieved at having been saved from the pirates and at being brought up in the relative security of a nunnery. Later, however, the narrator lets out that”on the day when the interesting young convert had lost all her natural relations and found a white father, she had been fighting desperately like the rest of them on board the prau, and was only prevented from leaping overboard, like the few other survivors, by a severe wound in the leg. There, on the fore-deck of the prau, old Lingard found her under a heap of dead and dying pirates, and had her carried on the poop of theFlashbefore the Malay craft was set on fire and sent adrift. She was conscious, and in the great peace and stillness of the tropical evening succeeding the turmoil of the battle, she watched all she held dear on earth after her own savage manner, drift away into the gloom in a great roar of flame and smoke. She lay there unheeding the careful hands attending to her wound, silent and absorbed in gazing at the funeral pile of those brave men she had so much admired and so well helped in their contest with the redoubtable ‘Rajah-Laut.’”And the narrator goes on to enlarge on the future Mrs. Almayer’s interpretation of her new situation like this:”She realised that with this vanishing gleam her old life departed too. Thenceforth there was slavery in the far countries, amongst strangers, in unknown and perhaps terrible surroundings. Being fourteen years old, she realised her position and came to that conclusion, the only one possible to a Malay girl, soon ripened under a tropical sun, and not unaware of her personal charms, of which she heard many a young brave warrior of her father’s crew express an appreciative admiration. There was in her the dread of the unknown; otherwise she accepted her position calmly, after the manner of her people, and even considered it quite natural; for was she not a daughter of warriors, conquered in battle, and did she not belong rightfully to the victorious Rajah? Even the evident kindness of the terrible old man must spring, she thought, from admiration for his captive, and the flattered vanity eased for her the pangs of sorrow after such an awful calamity. Perhaps had she known of the high walls, the quiet gardens, and the silent nuns of the Samarang convent, where her destiny was leading her, she would have sought death in her dread and hate of such a restraint. But in imagination she pictured to herself the usual life of a Malay girl—the usual succession of heavy work and fierce love, of intrigues, gold ornaments, of domestic drudgery, and of that great but occult influence which is one of the few rights of half-savage womankind. But her destiny in the rough hands of the old sea-dog, acting under unreasoning impulses of the heart, took a strange and to her a terrible shape. She bore it all—the restraint and the teaching and the new faith—with calm submission, concealing her hate and contempt for all that new life. She learned the language very easily, yet understood but little of the new faith the good sisters taught her, assimilating quickly only the superstitious elements of the religion. She called Lingard father, gently and caressingly, at each of his short and noisy visits, under the clear impression that he was a great and dangerous power it was good to propitiate. Was he not now her master? And during those long four years she nourished a hope of finding favour in his eyes and ultimately becoming his wife, counsellor, and guide.”This passage carries Nina’s pessimistic words on to a new level, namely that of cross-cultural communication and understanding, or rather of its insurmountable difficulties, and probably gives more dignity to the world-view of the “non-white individual” than politically correct ravings on “noble savages” whose paradisiac life is destroyed by evil Europeans could ever do. It is Almayer’s tragic and cardinal sin that he never even stops to consider his daughter’s mixed roots and instead tries to make her adapt to the standards of a glorified culture and society he has never even really experienced first-hand. InAlmayer’s Folly, Europeans, Arabs and Malays share the same vices, follies and aspirations basically, which not only renders absurd any accusations of Conrad’s writings as racist but also underlines the author’s all-pervading pessimism, and, I’d like to add, knowledge of human nature. Near the end of the novel, the one-eyed Machiavellian Babalatchi, at first sight a through and through unlikeable character, puts the Malays’ dilemma like this:”’[…] Even I have sailed with Lanun men, and boarded in the night silent ships with white sails. That was before an English Rajah ruled in Kuching. Then we fought amongst ourselves and were happy. Now when we fight with you we can only die!’”Almayer’s Follyis probably not yet on a par with the grander novels Conrad was yet to produce but all the assets are already there, like his dark sense of humour – as when Almayer, lost in reveries about future banquets, forgets to do justice to the bowl of rice in front of him – and his use of symbolism, which is sometimes very obvious – as with the eponymous half-built new house which will be his only refuge when everything else is in ruins – but which is sometimes subtler and cast in the powerful prose that makes it so difficult to believe that English was actually Conrad’s third language:”In a moment the two little nutshells with their occupants floated quietly side by side, reflected by the black water in the dim light struggling through a high canopy of dense foliage; while above, away up in the broad day, flamed immense red blossoms sending down on their heads a shower of great dew-sparkling petals that descended rotating slowly in a continuous and perfumed stream; and over them, under them, in the sleeping water; all around them in a ring of luxuriant vegetation bathed in the warm air charged with strong and harsh perfumes, the intense work of tropical nature went on: plants shooting upward, entwined, interlaced in inextricable confusion, climbing madly and brutally over each other in the terrible silence of a desperate struggle towards the life-giving sunshine above—as if struck with sudden horror at the seething mass of corruption below, at the death and decay from which they sprang.”In my eyes, Conrad’s first novel deserves a full recommendation because of its brooding atmosphere, its complex characters – Conrad even manages to make me feel pity with Almayer although he is a victim of his own racial prejudices and his failure to face reality and to take his life into his own hands –, its intricate story and, above all, because it already introduces the major themes that will pervade most of Conrad’s later works, where – of course – they are going to be dealt with in even subtler ways. All in all,Almayer’s Follyis an outstanding first novel by one of the greatest writers ever.
