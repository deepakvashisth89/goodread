Did I expect to get drawn deeply into smell theory when I picked up this book? No. I figured I'd abandon it before I finished all 352 pages, but it turned out to be eye-opening in several ways.First of all, the accepted theory of how our sense of smell works is that molecule shape influences smell. However, it turns out that there are more than a few exceptions to the rule, which is why chemists for perfumers might make hundreds of fragrance molecules before they land on the one they're looking for. Luca Turin set out to try to find a better smell theory by imagining the nose as a machine and figuring out how it worked. He resurrected an old theory that the smell of a molecule is related to the vibrational frequency of that molecule. Combining what he knew of biology, chemistry, and physics, he set out to prove (or disprove it). Some of the most telling experiments he did were related to molecules that had the exact same shape but smelled completely different, which he found on multiple occasions. With this theory, he was hoping to accumulate enough information to create a software algorithm be able to predict smell based on vibration.What's disheartening about this book is how many times Turin's theory was throttled, despite having plenty of proof. You want to think of the scientific community as being open-minded, but when something challenges your life's work, you don't necessarily embrace it. For example, there are millions of dollars being pumped into the fragrance industry, and the chemists sitting around making all of these attempts at the perfect fragrance and taking weeks or months to hit upon the perfect one have the most to lose from a more exact way to predict smell. It makes me wonder what other scientific discoveries out out there, published in some tiny journal somewhere and not making the waves that they should because the right people didn't embrace them -- or the companies with the money to bury it to protect their livelihood buried it.Anyhow, despite being fairly chemistry heavy (and maybe because of it), this was an enjoyable book (probably made easier to understand by helping my teen with her chemistry homework these days). Turin certainly had the knowledge, personality, and drive to get this theory out there. If he couldn't do it, I'm not sure who could. But at least someone was fascinated enough by it to write a book about it.
