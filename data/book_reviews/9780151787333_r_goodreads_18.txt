“A woman must have money and a room of her own if she is to write fiction… a room with a lock on the door.”A literal truth that’s equally valid as a much broader analogy. Woolf even thinks her small inheritance more useful than gaining the vote.Image:Woolf's room of her own at Monk's House, Lewes, Sussex, which is surrounded by windows and doors to the beautiful garden (note boat and lighthouse painted on the fireplace).My photo; more info about the househere.For a woman to have the practical and financial independence to do what she wants, it also helps to have an education, and Woolf is acutely aware of her lack of formal education, and knows that her sex is the main reason. But the pages are filled with insight and erudition that sent me Googling in several instances.“Lock up your libraries if you like; but there is no gate, no lock, no bolt, that you can set upon the freedom of my mind.”OxbridgeImage:The Parker Library, Corpus Christi College, Cambridge(Source)Oxbridge refers to the shared characteristics of the UK’s two oldest universities, Oxford and Cambridge. The OED cites this work as the source: Woolf uses it to apply to the real universities when quoting Thackery’sThe History of Pendennis, in which Oxbridge is a single, fictional college, with echoes of the two greats.The six chapters of this seminal feminist text are an expanded version of two talks Woolf gave about women and fiction, at Newnham and Girton (women’s colleges at the University of Cambridge) in 1928. Hence, the sometimes conversational tone, and her very specific observations about the things she cannot do there because she’s a woman, such as walking on the grass, or going into the library without a Fellow (professor) or letter of introduction.Woolf notes the relatively plain food at women’s colleges, because they can’t afford such indulgences, and how that disadvantages her sex:“One cannot think well, love well, sleep well, if one has not dined well.”I fondly recall the thrill of all the times I wandered a Cambridge college when it was closed to the public, even walking on the grass, despite my being a woman, and not even through my own merits, but because my child was studying there, and later had their wedding reception there. Woolf and her ilk deserve such privileges far more than I do.British MuseumImage:The Round Reading Room at the British Library(Source)After her Cambridge trip, Woolf goes to investigate her observations. She finds lots of books by men about women, but realises “Women do not write books about men”. She skims newspaper headlines and bemoans the rule of the patriarchy.Woolf wonders why some women are poor and answers with traditional, misogynistic generalisations. She feels hampered by her lack of university research skills:“Truth had run through my fingers. Every drop had escaped.”ClassIntersectionality was not a term back then, but Woolf moves on to class, specifically the absence of novels by middle-class women, although she assumes that many “Anon” were women. She wants to know about the daily life of middle-class women, especially in the eighteenth century, but domestic tasks leave few footprints in history. She also wants to read about the psychology of women written by women, but finds little.Woolf imagines Shakespeare had a sister, Judith, and all the reasons she would not have been able to write his plays:“For genius like Shakespeare's is not born among labouring, uneducated, servile people… How, then, could it have been born among women whose work began, according to Professor Trevelyan, almost before they were out of the nursery?”Breaking throughImage:Paintings of women writing usually show them writing letters, rather than anything more significant. This affluent woman is by Albert Edelfelt in 1887(Source)Woolf acknowledges the more recent success of female novelists (but still being held back in painting and music) and how women’s writing has changed:“Reading and criticism may have given her a wider range, a greater subtlety. The impulse towards autobiography may be spent. She may be beginning to use writing as an art, not as a method of self-expression.”TheBechdel Testdid not exist in 1929, but Woolf celebrates how refreshing it is to have, and read about, relationships between women that are not also about men:“It was strange to think that all the great women of fiction were, until Jane Austen's day, not only seen by the other sex, but seen only in relation to the other sex.”This is in a section where she triumphantly declares that “sometimes women do like women”, but although she's clearly, but coyly, talking about lesbian relationships, I don't think she's solely talking about that.Turn it round, and the absurdity is plain:“Suppose, for instance, that men were only represented in literature as the lovers of women, and were never the friends of men, soldiers, thinkers, dreamers; how few parts in the plays of Shakespeare could be allotted to them; how literature would suffer!”Image:Books on the stairs at Woolf's country retreat, Monk's House, Lewes, Sussex.My photo; more info about the househere.Quotes•	“The willows wept in perpetual lamentation.”•	“I thought how unpleasant it is to be locked out; and I thought how it was worse perhaps to be locked in.”•	“Money dignifies what is frivolous if unpaid for.
