Having just completed the full run of John le Carré s Smiley books--some re-reads--I am here to say that Russia House (1989), also by John le Carré, is generally as good the others, a classic spy novel. Such a great writer. Again, most of the action is reported in interviews, recalled, so it's a bit less direct than your typical thriller, but the quality of the writing and the insights about tradecraft and moral dilemmas therein are superior. le Carré had been a "Joe," an agent in MI6, so he knows the inside as well as anyone writing.I first read this on a plane to Moscow in 1991, glasnost and perestroika in full bloom, Gorbachev in power, more democratic reforms possibly on the way, feelings of hope and questions about new directions abounding. I was going with some fellow English teachers and some high school students to meet some English teachers and students attending an English-speaking Lycée. We did a cross-cultural writing project together, in keeping with the new open-ness. And yes, we were there in Red Square when a million Russians gathered there to push back against the Gorbachev's stand against public demonstrations there, a possible coup? Very calm and respectful rally, and we were there! And later that year, in December 1991, the dissolution of the Soviet Union happened!But this novel is about the end of the Cold War, involving that faction of the British Intelligence Service devoted to spying on Russia, nicknamed The Russia House. The story focuses at least initially on a British book publisher conscripted to Moscow to help with the investigation of a manuscript. Also while on the plane we watched the 1990 film starring Sean Connery and Michelle Pfeiffer and adapted by Tom Stoppard. I gotta see that again! And, oh yes, I still have my set of nesting Gorby dolls I got there.In 1987, Bartholomew "Barley" Scott Blair, a British publisher, went to Moscow. Katya seeks Barley out at an audio fair, hoping to convince him to publish a manuscript for her friend Yakov which details Soviet nuclear capabilities and atomic secrets. The manuscript (of course) falls into the hands of MI6 (and others). Barley is at the outset like many of us in the world at the time hopeful about glasnost and the future of the planet. But this is 1987, not 2026. Vladimir Putin was at the time doing his 16 years stint for the KGB, Gorbachev was at the time leader of Russia, then Boris Yeltsin became head from late 1991 (after I had been there in the summer) and things headed eventually away from democratization, where we certainly still are far from today.This is a pretty straightforward plot, in some ways. We have a flawed party boy who has never spied before.. We have a send-up of Ugly Americans. We have a romance that seems credible, with some warmth (le Carre is not a great writer of romance, but he has romance in all his books).I think there is a nod to Graham Greene’s Our Man in Havana, that features a vacuum cleaner salesman, Wormold, to spy for Britain about the possibility of a Russian missile base in Cuba. Both Greene and le Carre served in MI6, so they knew what they were talking about. Greene’s book is a broad satire of the spy business, and in some ways so is Russia House, that makes some fun of the spy business, but Russia House features a book publisher as a spy, and so we get a satirical glimpse into the publishing industry, too. And lots of literary references. One character’s code name is Goethe. The literary folks are, including Barley, and us, naive about the political future. We see in this book how we might worry that a rosy future in Russia and with Russia-West relations may not be guaranteed. But as with Greene, le Carre ends the book by underscoring the need for people to do good in spite of the situation supposed amorality spycraft. I really liked the book.
