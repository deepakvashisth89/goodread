“To cease, to sleep, to replace this intermittent consciousness with better, more melancholy things uttered in secret to a stranger!… To cease, to flow, fluid as a river, as the ebb and flow of a vast sea along coasts seen in a night in which one could really sleep!… To cease, to be unknown and external, the stirring of branches in remote avenues, the tenuous falling of leaves that one senses without hearing them fall, the subtle sea of distant fountains, and the whole indistinct world of gardens at night, lost in endless complexities, the natural labyrinths of the dark!… To cease, to end once and for all, but yet to survive in another form, as the pages of a book, a loose lock of hair, a swaying creeper outside a half-open window, insignificant footsteps on the fine gravel on the curve of a path, the last twist of smoke high above a village as it falls asleep, the idle whip of the wagoner stopped by the road in the morning. Absurdity, confusion, extinction—anything but life.”

(This is more of a personal essay than a review.)

I picked this book up on a whim after another failed attempt at therapy and antidepressants, and I knew from the first few pages that this would become a favorite, but I didn’t know how much I needed it until I reached the later part of the book. This selection begins by placing us in the life of Bernardo Soares, a minor clerk whiling away his life in the office, his house, the streets. But as the non-novel continues, the fragments—like diary entries—become longer and more intense, and the office, his house, and the streets gradually fade away. Then we’re drifting free of everything, as if poised on the brink of all possibilities.

A favorite… More like my own soul than a favorite, dressed as a book, risen to meet me… Inside are all my innermost feelings, all the things that lay buried in me, burning and also desperately longing, coupled with descriptive powers I’ll never have. Like Soares, I don’t understand why I can’t translate my love for the flutter of gold and scarlet leaves down a long shaft of sunlight into the love of life that so many others seem to have. I analyze my feelings so closely I don’t know whether I can still feel as deeply as others do. I feel out of step with life—that I’m on a stage and don’t know the words that everyone else picks up instantly. I feel as if my life is failed, even though it’s ridiculous because I’m only nineteen. And I’m constantly sick of the world… of living… of having lived.



Like Soares, I constantly tell myself that nothing matters as long as I can keep reading and thinking about writing, that whatever waves break over me don’t matter as long as I can fly into myself. Without books, I don’t know what would happen to me. I don’t agree with everything Soares says, though. Some of these fragments are quite insane. I couldn’t be content with the life of perfect solitude that Soares looks up to. I actually wish I had an easier time connecting with others in a meaningful way. I don’t people my inner world with characters I create. I don’t think you can understand everyone else just by knowing yourself. I still want to travel, to fall in love. I’m not that disillusioned with life—at least, not yet.

I know that my thoughts are constantly solipsistic in the extreme—especially after seeing them laid out this way. Reading this book wasn’t always a pleasant experience. But it reminded me that there are others out there much like me, even across the distance of a century and more. (Pessoa described Soares as “a simple mutilation of my personality. It’s me minus reason and affectivity.”) I want to memorize all of this, to fix in my mind forever all these words that seem to confirm my existence… but they’re already fading… already gone. Soares would say I’ve added the essential to my dreams. Maybe that’s enough. Maybe.





Thanks for reading this ramble. I’m sure in a few years I’ll read this and cringe and have to force myself not to delete it. But right now I just want to make sure I don’t lose this feeling, this moment.
