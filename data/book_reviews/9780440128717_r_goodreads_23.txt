I didn't know that Agatha Christie had written anything other than murder mysteries, which I suppose is exactly the point of doing it under a pseudonym. Interesting!Anyway, this book is persistently, foully misogynistic and antisemitic. It's also a ridiculous, sensational and improbable plot with poorly-constructed characters. It's well-written, however, and despite its deep problems does touch upon some genuinely interesting themes about class, art and gender. Fundamentally, the book is about the Quest For Art and the ways in which Vernon's singleminded and often destructive pursuit of art is, like, totally a good thing, I swear.Before I get into the problems with this book, it's important to understand that it was first published in 1930. The book is set throughout Vernon's life, hitting important beats during the Boer War, pre-WWI, WWI and post-war. The First World War is a looming spectre throughout the novel, although we never actually see any frontline action; the narrative is constantly under strain from the reader's knowledge of the war, which in 1930 could be assumed to be firsthand, and the ways Christie skips around it heighten the sense of its gravitational pull. It's a clever choice and one in keeping with Vernon's sense that all life is merely a distraction from his art.As a child, Vernon has a group of friends who will remain the other important figures in the narrative: his cousin Josephine/Joe, his neighbour Sebastian and an annoying village child, Nell. The other important character is Jane, who he meets as an adult. Believe me, I have things to say about the women, but for now I have enough to be getting on with in Sebastian.You see, Sebastian is Jewish. Christie makes sure that you know this. She mentions it rather a lot. Warning: absolutely vile antisemitism coming up under the spoiler.On page TWO of the book we have our first antisemitic remark about Sebastian. "Who is this fellow Levinne? A dirty foreign Jew. That's all he is!" Obviously, I assumed that this was meant to reflect poorly upon the speaker, but then we get this:'"It won't catch on. A stunt, thath all." "Yet, I don't know - there's a feeling for this Cubist thtuff." "Levinne's shrewd." "Dropth money deliberately thometimes - but getth it back." "Cost...?" The voices dropped, hushed themselves mysteriously as sums of money were mentioned.Those were members of his own race. Sebastian Levinne smiled.'This is appalling. Writing out lisps, homogenising 'members of his own race' as a babble of voices obsessed not with the art but with the money it could generate and separating them out from previous paragraphs which discussed 'men' and 'women' (ah yes, the secret third gender, Jewish) - it's bad. Sebastian continues to be characterised by obsessive shrewdness with money and an unfortunate, racially-coded appearance throughout the whole novel.After the first chapter, we cut back to meeting Sebastian as a child. Here's that introduction:'First came Mr Levinne - very round and stout, tightly frock-coated - an enormous nose and a shining face. Then Mrs - an amazing sight. colossal sleeves! Hour glass figure! Chains of diamonds! [..] With them was a boy rather taller than Vernon with a long yellow face, and protruding ears.'And there's this wonderful gem Sebastian tells Vernon: '"You don't realize. Jews are frightfully powerful. My father says people can't do without them. That's why Sir Charles Alington had to sell us Deerfields."A sudden chill came over Vernon. He felt without putting the thought into words that he was talking to a member of an enemy race.'1930, everybody! This is just so, so insidious. It's not presented as the bragging of a little boy because Sebastian does in fact go on to be incredibly influential. Christie presents Jews as ugly, money-obsessed infiltrators who force themselves into English communities using a sinister kind of influence. This is where that context about 1930 and the WWI presence/absence becomes important. Christie is fuelling the stab-in-the-back myth and actually directly contributing to the attitudes that motivated the Holocaust. It's unconscionable.Sebastian is to some extent an interesting, nuanced character. It's just that this antisemitic rhetoric is inescapable whenever he's on the page and it drowns out anything else that Christie tries to do with him. Another example of how intolerance worsens art.Okay, there are a LOT more examples of antisemitism because it really is the defining feature of this novel, but we're moving on to the only people Christie is LESS tolerant of than Jews: WOMEN. Wow, she seems to hate women. Every single woman is a Madonna or a Whore, but with a fun twist: you can't win! Madonnas and Whores both suck!Let's look at the Whores first, since they are more interesting. The Whores are Joe, Jane and Joe's mum. Jane is the prime example. She is a singer who enters illicit, non-marital relationships with men. These are long-term relationships and she treats them seriously, so she's hardly what would be considered promiscuous today, but obviously she isn't married so she's a slag. She is consistently treated very poorly by men but remains utterly devoted to them. This is the template for Christie's Whores: their problem is that they have too large a heart and fall for hopeless men, who wound and abuse them and leave them worse off by far than before. Then they repeat the cycle. Christie doesn't condemn the Whore for their (relative) promiscuity, but she does depict them as foolish to allow their love for men to ruin their prospects.Jane ruins her singing career for Vernon, which is the wrong thing to do, because the book makes it clear that to produce Art is the only right choice. She ruins Art for him, making her a fool.Joe fades from the page as the book goes on but we learn that she has a series of similar love affairs which end very badly for her. (I'll discuss the Whores' endings in a moment). Joe's mother, very much in the background, runs away with a man and has a series of affairs which end very badly. See the theme?As to what their foolishness gets them:Joe's mum dies tragically off-page, Jane dies tragically (and a bit farcically) off-page, Joe gets tuberculosis and ends the book possibly about to die, but possibly about to be saved by Sebastian despite her foolish decisions, which is nice I suppose.Now for the Madonnas. Typically, you'd expect these to be model women. However, Christie's Madonnas are women whose ability to conform to societal expectations arises from a series of deep character flaws. These women are Vernon's mother, Nell's mother, Nell and literally every other woman in the book.Nell's mother is something of an antagonist to Vernon, standing in the way of a love-match. She prioritises appearances and finances over her daughter's happiness. She is, in my opinion, very sensible but this doesn't matter: she is BAD. Vernon's mother is a total nightmare. She projects the appearance of virtue but is presented as a nag to Vernon's father and a smothering force to Vernon. She seems to lack all empathy. She views people as props to her life and feels no deep, sincere emotions. At the same time, the text does acknowledge that her husband's many affairs have deeply wounded her, but her emotional reactions to this are shown to be so blatantly insincere and melodramatic that even the child Vernon can tell. She views herself as the heroine of a melodrama and enjoys tragedies likeher husband's death and, later, her son'sbecause she becomes the centre of attention. She's a total piece of work. The text introduces some sympathy for her at the start but it is clear before long that she's really taking it all too far, and the right thing to do would be to weather her husband's infidelity and coldness towards her like a champ - indeed, as the Whores would.Nell is more complicated. Nell wants so, so badly to throw it all over for love, but can't bring herself to. Christie presents us with genuinely insightful class analysis here, showing Nell's incapacity to exist in a lower-class environment due to the deep-seated incompetence and fear of poverty her upbringing has instilled in her. Poverty, for Nell - I am not joking here - means only having one servant, or perhaps two. She's not cold, like the older women, but strangely detached. She loves very deeply but has no follow-through. She vacillates between what is easy and what is right (in this case, "right" means "serves the men in her life without thought for her own comfort, as the Whores would") and never manages to settle on one side or another.That the Madonna and the Whore are in direct competition, and that they represent the tension between too-detached and the too-vulnerable ways of interacting with the world, is made clear by the patently ridiculous ending. You actually can't make up how stupid this is. I'm putting it in spoilers, but I beg you to read it because it is SO funny and you shouldn't read this book anyway.So, Nell and Jane have been in a Madonna-Vernon-Whore love triangle for the entire book. Nell is too concerned with money and appearances to fully give in to her love, whereas Jane gives her body and soul to Vernon but he is only interested in what he can't quite get. Eventually, Vernon leaves Jane and marries Nell, but then there's a whole amnesia plot and Nell remarries so Vernon gets with Jane. This is already ridiculous but it gets so much worse.Vernon and Jane, and Nell and her new husband, unknowingly board the same ocean liner from London to New York. It hits an iceberg and sinks (I know, how unoriginal). This happens off-page and Vernon narrates what happened:'they came drifting along the deck, those two - right by me - slipping - sliding - faster and faster - and the sea waiting for them below.I'd no idea Nell was on board till I saw her - drifting down to destruction - and crying out "Vernon!".There isn't time to think on these occasions, I tell you. One can just make an instinctive gesture. I could grab on to one or the other of them... Nell or Jane...I grabbed Nell and held her, held her like grim death.'Jane drowns.This is RIDICULOUS. You're telling me they came at him like a slip-and-slide down the deck and had no means of saving themselves, but conveniently were aimed right at him? What am I supposed to feel about this image? Jane's death is meant to be a betrayal, and it totally is, but it's also absurd. She looks so stupid for giving everything to Vernon only to die in this way. Then Nell is also made to look absurd, when she throws over her husband and comes running to Vernon in New York, only to be rejected because he has decided he actually loved the dead Jane more. He gets to feel scathing and superior and both women are humiliated and abandoned, despite neither having done the faintest thing wrong!The message of this work is that women have value only as muses in the production of great art, which is the realm of men. Men's only goal in life should be to produce that art and women should be prepared to be used and then discarded in the process. Abusive men are excused if their wives seem at all upset about their abuse, and doubly excused if the art they produce is very nice.I know that Christie is trying to complicate this idea, to force us to confront the fact that sometimes artists who produce great things have had to do unpleasant things to make it. She's engaging with the "death of the author" debate many years before Barthes (1967 btw). It's just that she bends over backwards to depict the women who are used in this way as victims, yes, but mostly as stupid for allowing themselves to be victimised. It's a man's world, Christie claims - specifically, Jewish men's world, in all the awful conspiratorial antisemitism that suggests - and everybody else had jolly well better stop complaining, or you'll deserve what's coming to you.I think she had better have stuck to the murder mysteries.
