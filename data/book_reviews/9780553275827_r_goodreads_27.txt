I have read this one multiple times. I was introduced to it as a young officer along with its spiritual and thematic predecessor, “Company Commander” by Charles MacDonald (published in the late 1940s). In “Platoon Leader,” James McDonough tells his story of personal and professional growth under fire in Vietnam.

McDonough’s combat memoir offers much of value, especially for junior (or aspiring) leaders. This book addresses most of the acute challenges that young military officers face, whether serving in a war zone or not. The crucible of combat heightens the stakes and, for the casual reader, raises the drama of the narrative. However, anyone who has accepted the responsibility of leading America’s sons and daughters will recognize that any “drama” woven in the anecdotes is simply the manifestation of the human challenges of leadership in extremis.

The caution I would offer with this book is that it is truly a snapshot in time. While many of the basic themes and lessons are indeed timelessly relevant, some of the specifics are unique to the American military experience in Vietnam. Having served in combat multiple times during our Nation’s more recent extended campaigns in Iraq and Afghanistan, I can attest that while the nature of the challenges of leading in combat was very similar, they often manifested themselves in very different ways. As with any work of history, especially a personal memoir, a prudent reader will resist the urge to translate details precisely to new circumstances. Instead, one must sensibly apply the generalities and adapt to the current day.
