Peel away the mask of the ego and you reveal not the face of the id, but an infinite array of further masks, none which is primordial, but all which are archetypal. There's a cynical nihilism to Carter's world, which resembles an intertextual smashing together of only texts, but it's a gleeful nihilism, intrusive and obscene, like Swift shot through de Sade. Everything here is smoke and mirrors, a reflected image of an image similarly reflected. Carter draws from Taoism one sentence, then Pataphysics the next, then Logical Positivism. A storm smashes apart a community. The next scene, a man cavorts around with an erection larger than his body. The world itself is slipstream. It may flow quickly, creating strange amalgamations, only to catch, like a neurotic fixation, and swirl in painstaking circles for what feels like an eternity. Other times, unwanted things intrude. There's a recurring theme of rape in this book, which, while distasteful, seems to serve as a metaphor for self-destruction, the tactless irruption of the unconscious that does nothing but betray and demean the conscious. There're also extended scenes where nameless figures transform into grotesque caricatures of various ethnicities. If rape is Carter's metaphor for self-betrayal, then racism is the content of such betrayal: inane shit we internalise far before we've developed critical thought. It's obscene, it's tactless, but it's us, even if it came from outside of us. And this is perhaps the solipsistic paradox and joke of Carter's world: that all that we are is what comes from outside of us—myths, novels, films, paintings, philosophical treatises, racist and sexist stereotypes—yet we, nonetheless, remain the sole bearers of this cultural baggage, which wages war inside and across us, sometimes generating sublime beauty, other times perverse filth.
