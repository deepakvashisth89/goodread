A well-developed, slow-reading and enchanting novel, with many interesting aspects, of which I can only touch on a few here. To begin with, Wallace Stegner (1909-1993) indirectly provides a clever sketch of the American frontier years, more specifically the 1860s-1890s, more or less the later phase of the migration to the West. He does this through a reconstruction of the lives of Susan Burlings and her husband Olliver Ward, whose job as a mining engineer takes them to the most remote, barren regions of the then-Far West. Stegner thus gives his novel an epic quality without strictly being an epic. That's because the main focus is the personal story of Susan and Olliver, the evolution of their relationship, initially characterized by attraction and trust, later by trial and estrangement. It is primarily Susan who speaks, as Olliver, as he himself admits, is not very adept at "talky talky".

And here lies the masterfulness of this novel: Stegner introduces us to Susan and Olliver through their grandson, Lyman Ward, a 58-year-old historian who has completely secluded himself to write the life story of his grandparents. Lyman is confined to a wheelchair, having had one of his legs amputated due to illness, has been left by his wife for another man, and has become embittered in every way, alienated even from the society of the time, the late 1960s, where flower power reigns (here personified by his young assistant, Shelley). Instead, Lyman flees into the past. Primarily through Susan's letters to a childhood friend, he attempts to reconstruct his grandparents' story; a laborious process, especially as Lyman tries to uncover his grandmother's true experiences and inner thoughts.

As a reader, you are taken on a captivating journey, through both Susan's and Lyman's eyes. Ultimately, Lyman's main goal is to discover how Susan and Ward managed to stay together as a couple for 60 years, despite the tragedy of their lives—how, in his words, they found that "angle of repose," a technical term with different meanings, mostly indicating the angle at which things stop moving and come to rest, but also an engineering term that indicates how forces moving in different directions at some point can balance each other out, like an arche in which the two parts balance each other. Both meanings are relevant here.
