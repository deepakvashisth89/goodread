This is wildly creative and action-packed, which carried me through the casual racism and misogyny of 1956 SF. But the last 100 pages became progressively weirder till I had little idea what was happening or why.EnticingIt opens echoing Dickens to describe a somewhat Victorian 25th century:“This was a Golden Age, a time of high adventure, rich living, and hard dying… but nobody thought so. This was a future of fortune and theft, pillage and rapine, culture and vice… but nobody admitted it. This was an age of extremes, a fascinating century of freaks… but nobody loved it.”Humans have learned to jaunte hundreds of miles: teleporting by visualisation, concentration, and belief (plus knowing the coordinates). Trade is transformed, war and crime rife, pandemics rage, people-smugglers take money and advantage of refugees, and although religion is outlawed, a new prudery reigns.Image:The word “Revenge” spelt out in blood, with a bloody handprint(Source)“Of all the brutes in the world he was among the least valuable alive and most likely to survive.”When Gulliver Foyle is the last man alive on SS Nomad, and SS Vorga sails past, ignoring the SOS, he determines not just to survive, but find out who gave the order, why they gave it, and then to wreak revenge. This takes him to extraordinary places, via extreme body-mods, with various unwilling women in tow, as the hunter is himself hunted.His initial character development is good. “The blood-red stigmata of his possession” force him to learn to control his emotions: “He took a deep breath and drew calm about him like an iron cloak”. He educates himself and finds other tricks to aid his mission.But really, it’s a series of ever more extreme jauntes, rather than a decent plot arc.AmusementPresteign of Presteign is the head of one of the wealthiest clans and subscribes to:“The principle of conspicuous waste, on which all society is based.”Hence fabulous, but redundant instruments playing a conspicuous part in his Wildean soirées: a golden organ played by a robot, and using elevators and phones rather than jaunting.“Presteign would have preferred human servants but androids and robots kept secrets.”There’s also Geoffrey Fourmyle of Ceres, a super-rich buffoon, who quickly ceased to amuse me:“Nobody ever suspects a clown.”Image:“Evil Boris Clown” cartoon by Martin Rowson(Source)YukThere’s violence (including rape) and torture, but they largely fit the plot, and this was penned in 1956. I felt more uncomfortable with the racism and misogyny - although some of the female characters actively fight against their submission.Māori-style face tattoos are repeatedly described as “hideous” (they’re a key plot point, but could be hideousorMāori, rather than both). A “lovely Negro” woman is exoticised (a one-way telepath, filling the magical Negro trope) - and abused. “A glorious albino” is blind except for infrared. And although the Chinese are famously “discreet”, it’s an advantage if they do “not look Chinese”. A Japanese man is merely “a small Japanese”.Image:A beautiful Māori facial tattoo(Source)Finale- no spoilersIt came to feel more magical than sci-fi, specifically one of the powers a character acquires plus the psychokinetically controlled substance, PyrE.In the last 50 pages, we’re introduced to a Skoptsky “without senses, without pleasure, without pain. The ultimate in Stoic escape”, a seventy-year-old baby, synaesthetic overload (complete with weird fonts and sketches), and a personality transformation, none of which made any sense to me.
