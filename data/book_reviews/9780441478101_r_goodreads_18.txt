Set on the planet Winter, also known as Gethen, we follow human Genly Ai on his mission to the alien world. In his role as an envoy, he is trying to persuade the inhabitants of Winter to join an alliance with that of Ekumen, an intergalactic group of planets.

The other main protagonist is Estraven; at the beginning of the story, they are the King of Winter's right hand or prime minister, but it isn't long before they are accused of treason and banished. Their relationship with Genly is fraught to start but evolves as the plot progresses.

Gethians for most of any given month are genderless, and there is only a short window within said month where, for mostly reproductive purposes, they become female or male. It could be either, depending on hormonal secretions. This temporary state is referred to as 'kemmer'. The Gethians look at Genly in his fixed gender as 'perverse'.

The Author creates a complex civilisation, heavy on politics, bureaucracy and divisions—reflecting our own in many ways; the metaphors are apparent throughout. Whilst these parts were creative, the characterisation was not; both main protagonists sounded too alike. I had to keep flicking back and forth to double-check whose narrative it was, and neither had much in the way of depth.

The book does excel in its stellar world-building and the topics it introduces. The most interesting aspect of the story was the exploration and impact of a genderless society and the 'kemmer'; it was thought-provoking and, for its time, groundbreaking. Nevertheless, that was tainted by the constant references to the masculine, which left me confused; everyone was referred to as 'he', 'him', 'man', 'men', 'gentleman', etc.

I could understand it to a degree if it was solely from Genly's perspective, as his thoughts on gender were linear to the societal constructs of his experiences of life on Earth, but it was used the whole way through even by the genderless Gethians. This only reinforced patriarchal stereotypes and was a huge and glaring contradiction to the message the book was trying to convey. Given it was written in the 1960s, maybe it can simply be put down to being a product of its time; nonetheless, the inconsistent messaging remains.

I'm going to be honest, I was completely bamboozled by this book for the first 15-20%, and that doesn't happen often. It was hard to read—the prose was clunky, the plot confusing, and I was struggling to get into it. I was desperately trying to piece together what was happening because the premise was so intriguing.

Although I did finish it, I found it slow-paced, dull and tedious in parts, and the main characters are underdeveloped. My biggest gripe was how emotionless it was; there were no heartfelt moments that made you feel... something. It's not badly written by any means; it's just presented in a really monotonously flat way.

I think it's one of those books which fell foul to expectation versus reality. I had high hopes going in but was left somewhat underwhelmed. Whilst the plot isn't one I can say I enjoyed, some of its themes—the yin-yang and balance of all things, how friendship endures despite initial prejudices, and the impact of gender on society—were compelling and as significant today as they were when the book was first published in 1969; in that aspect, the Author was ahead of their time.

I'll go down the middle and rate it a 2.5/5 but will round up.
