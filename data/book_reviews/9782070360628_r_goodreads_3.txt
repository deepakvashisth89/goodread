I'm genuinely surprised I didn't like this more, given that I thoughtPère Goriotwas brilliant andLost Illusionsis considered by many to be Balzac's greatest work. But, while I am interested in the themes Balzac explores— idealism vs realism, ambition vs integrity, the corruption of wealth, the pretensions of the bourgeoisie —there were times when reading this book was a chore.This La Comédie humaine novel, far more so than the other two I have read, felt very much a novel of themes, and the characters themselves were not especially interesting. Most of the major characters are not personalities but instead represent worldviews, social forces, and ideas about art, ambition, and society. Everything is symbolic. Lucien symbolizes ambition and ego, where David symbolizes integrity and honest work. Eve symbolizes morality and wholesome love, where Madame de Bargeton symbolizes cultural pretension and superficial romance. Angouleme is the humble and sincere provincial counterpart to the fake glitz of the capitalist machine that is Paris.I do appreciate this and, in many ways, I do think it's quite clever. But as someone who loves to get lost in a story, to feel the emotional pull of characters who I won't forget, this was not my ideal way of spending over 700 pages. InPère Goriot, I felt such intense heartache for the eponymous old guy that my review reads like a bit of an emotional breakdown... I never came close to that level of feeling withLost Illusions.Perhaps it is partly because you can see how the characters' stories will go and so they do not feel organically developed. It is obvious from very early in the novel what Balzac is selling you— Lucien as ambitious and weak-willed; David as virtuous and steadfast —so that I was just waiting for the events of the novel to unfold, prewritten in archetypal destiny as they were.I did not actually mean to write such a negative-sounding review. I'm giving it three stars, which still means I "liked it", but I expected to like it a lot more.
