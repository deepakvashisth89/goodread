I was late coming to the Elvis Costello 'party,' hearing his brilliant second shot at country when I was in high school and dismissing him outright but then picking up Spike unheard when it came out based on the interesting reviews it got. As a late-comer, I was eager to find out as much as I could about the man and his music; part of that curiosity put this book in my hands years ago, and I'm only now getting around to reading it.Two important things to know about Tony Clayton-Lea's biography of Elvis Costello: this is a biography of 'Elvis Costello' and not the Declan MacManus that came before him; and it's not so much a biography as a fluidly written encapsulation/review of past interviews with the enigmatic Costello. With those considerations in mind, Clayton-Lea has managed an easily-read, enjoyable overview of Costello's career with no real revelations but an awful lot of (what appears to be) conjecture on the fellow's inner workings. Though quotes are thoroughly credited in the back (very little of the interview work was done by Clayton-Lea himself, and all of the interviews are major press so available to the general public), there are intuitions provided frequently on motivations and reactions that only Costello himself would know...and he appears to not be talking about them. So, what we get is a big piece of the authour's assessment of why he thinks certain things happened in Costello's career, which is a major weakness to the narrative as it stands.The other weakness is merely a stylistic one, in that Clayton-Lea occasionally steps in with a nudge-and-wink sort of persona to give off-the-cuff one-liners at the reader. These are out of place in an otherwise straight-forward work of non-fiction, and I certainly found it off-putting (the final line of the book is 'Oh, Mr. Costello...really!'). The book would have been considerably easier to read without these interjections, but they're never anything that genuinely harms the authour's credibility.This edition features a hefty (if not exhaustive) discography of Costello's recorded output and some live-only work he's produced, a supportive bibliography of larger works and the aforementioned sources listing, as well as a prefatory acknowledgements section. Also included is an eight-page black-and-white photo section, chiefly taken from promotional and published images, mostly captioned with quotes from the book.While this was a brisk and entertaining read, I don't feel that I've come away from it with any more insight into Elvis Costello than I had from reading years' worth of interviews before it. For a new fan of the man, this would be a great place to get a quick look at his career (as well as a listing of things to find and listen to), but I think I'm better off letting the music speak for the man.
