The Twilight-Colored Smell of HoneysuckleIf you grew up in the rural South, Faulkner’s line about “the twilight-colored smell of honeysuckle” can hit you like a sudden chill. It pulls up porch-light memories: summer dusk, older relatives talking low, faces half-lost to time. I can still see my great-grandfather Giovanni, who came through Ellis Island in 1910 as an 11-year-old from Bologna, Italy and by the time I recall visiting him in the early 1970s, he was a jovial, bald man with a thick accent and thicker glasses, and he would slip into Italian just for me. That smell of honeysuckle is not just pretty writing. It is how the past sneaks back in.I first bought The Sound and the Fury in college because I thought an educated Mississippian was supposed to. I made it ten pages and decided Faulkner was messing with me on purpose. I tried again a few years later, same result. The sentences felt scrambled, time jumped without warning, and I could not get my footing.Years later, after my mother died, I drove through Natchez, past the old mansions and live oaks draped in Spanish moss, and it finally clicked. The past is not over. Not down here, not anywhere. And Faulkner is not asking you to read comfortably. He is asking you to enter a mind, then a family, then a whole region where time does not behave.So I went back, older, a father, less interested in proving anything to anybody. I used a companion guide, and I do not apologize for it. Once I understood what Faulkner is doing, especially putting you inside Benjy’s consciousness without handrails, the book opened up. It is difficult on purpose, but the difficulty is the point. When you crack it, the rewards are real: the weight of time, the ache of loss, the way memory works, the way decay hides inside beauty, even inside honeysuckle.This is the hardest novel I have ever read, and one of the most rewarding. Any Southerner who has not read it should try. Then try again.
