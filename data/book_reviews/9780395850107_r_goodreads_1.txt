Engaging study of American women activists in the 19th and 20th Century, showing how many were gay or bisexual and why they matter. Faderman, author of numerous books on LGBTQ history, focuses largely on the pre-modern, pre-Stonewall feminists - women's suffrage groups of the 19th and early 20th Century, social reformers and political activists in the Progressive Era, New Deal bureaucrats and brain trusters and progressives in postwar America - who were forced to hide their sexual orientation. She argues that their sexual orientations were far from incidental; given the time period they operated in, with highly restrictive gender roles discouraging women from education, independence or taking roles in the public sphere, gay and bisexual women were often more able to act on reformist impulses than heterosexual women. Thus Jane Addams, the founder of Hull House, worked in conjunction with partner Sarah Weber; suffragist Carrie Chapman Catt leaned heavily on her secretary and companion Alda Heaton Wilson; Frances Perkins, FDR's Secretary of Labor, who had an affair with Mary Harriman Rumsey while both were married; and many other women chronicled, affected sweeping changes in American society that traditional femininity, theoretically at least, denied. Such activism proved a double-edged sword, however, as it became easy to dismiss such women as "spinsters" and "inverts" whose personal predilections concealed radical ideologies. Faderman's analysis is acute and sympathetic, interweaving biographic profiles with the passionate correspondence between these women; the book does occasionally feel limited, however, in the relatively few women of color chronicled and the absence of transgender individuals (which may be the result of her selected time frame). But the work stands as a valuable examination of how feminism and lesbian identity informed, promoted and reinforced each other throughout American history.
