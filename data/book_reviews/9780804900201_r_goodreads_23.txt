On the face of it The Black Arrow is juvenile Errol Flynn swashbuckling country. The outlaw's real name may be Ellis Duckworth but he is The Black Arrow or, rather, the user of black arrows, and he is a vengeful man not a righteous taker from the rich, giver to the poor. That is perhaps the first hint that Stevenson had something more than a story of high adventure, archery and swordplay bubbling in his mind.

The story was first serialized in the magazine Young Folks using Stevenson's pen-name Captain George North. It seems to have been a response to the critical attacks made on his earlier serial , now known as . That story was not adventurous enough apparently and young Jim Hawkins too passive to be a hero. has peril and battles coming out of its ears. Dick Shelton is our hero, 16 years old and as handy with a crossbow, dirk and sword as a seasoned warrior three times his age. To level the character development, he is also impetuous, too trusting and forgiving, and prone to falling in love with a pretty face.

Young Dick has been wronged, seriously wronged. His father had been murdered on the orders of the evil, traitorous Sir Daniel Brackley, though no one knows he was behind it. He had taken Dick as his ward in order to plunder the Shelton lands and Dick's estate. Dick suspects what has happened yet has no proof, so he is off into the greenwood where he meets another boy, John Matcham, to whom he is strangely attracted. Thus begins our journey into struggle, misfortune, retaliation, and so many hints toward Shakespeare that one feels beneath all of the pseudo-medieval "tushery" (a term used by Stevenson: that sort of dialogue forms tushery) there lies some literary fiction.

There are so many people pretending to be what they are not. A girl dressed as a boy, an outlaw and Dick disguised as monks, a loyal Lancastrian lord who will sell himself to the Yorkists for a price, another noble wearing the cowl and carrying the bell of a leper. So much action set in the forest. The appearance of Richard of Gloucester - Richard Crookback - who will become Richard III. But this is not Shakespeare's Richard - or Olivier's - this is a ferocious war leader, a man who can fight off seven adversaries and come out grinning, a man who demands loyalty and often receives it. He is also a man with a vicious ambition who, Stevenson hints, will come to a sticky end. Dick is almost drawn into his mesmerizing gaze, yet not quite. Which is where the most un-juvenile aspect of the novel comes in.

To begin with, is full of bravado and bravery. It is easy to decide who is good and who is bad and the bad guys can be killed. Dick does his share of the killing. Wrongs can be committed of they are meant to facilitate achieving a right. The theft of Captain Arblaster's ship is the chief example. Then, after Dick meets Richard Crookback and takes part in the savage battle of Shoreby, a strange pacifism descends on the young man and the sword-wielding adventurer becomes almost penitent. Dick looks at the slaughter around him and understands that he can never share the cruelty of men such as Crookback. He meets Arblaster again, who is now a drunken, penniless pauper whose ship and cargo were stolen and then wrecked in a storm, and realizes what his ultimately useless theft had caused. It seems that in the space of a chapter or two the story has become a novel and is no longer for adventure-seeking children.

However, as it was published in a paper called , there must be a happy ending, though not quite the straight forward one of boy reunited with girl, they kiss and get married. There is the matter of the villain's death and the arrival of a seductress called Alicia. The flirtatious Alicia shoves children to one side while she has a go at snogging the now innocent Dick in the greensward. But all ends well, as well it must for young folks, and Dick and Joanna are set for a life of bliss while around them the Wars of the Roses will continue from one bloody battlefield to the next until Crookback, briefly a king, is slaughtered himself and the execution-loving Tudors take over.

A far better book than it is often considered to be.
