When I set out to read Virgil'sAeneida few months ago, I decided on the Alan Mandelbaum translation from 1970, and ordered a paper copy. The paper copy took a long time to arrive so in the meantime, I started a free ebook edition translated by John Dryden, c1710. I also downloaded a parallel text with Virgil's original Latin on one side of the page and a translation by J.W. Mackail on the other (I learned Latin at school).In spite of having the ebooks, I was impatient to get my paper version. When it finally arrived, the packaging bruised and battered, it turned out that the book gods had played a trick on me. The book I received was not the Mandelbaum translation but a 1981 edition translated by Robert Fitzgerald. It was a lovely Everyman hardback so I wasn't unhappy. But I didn't give up on my original choice and downloaded an ebook of Mandelbaum'sAeneid.So now I hadThe Aeneidin Latin plus in four translations! This profusion of enthusiasm is because I've long wanted to read the story of Trojan Aeneas's troubled voyage from Troy to Italian shores after the defeat by the Greeks in the battle made famous in Homer'sIliadand hisOdyssey.In the course ofThe AeneidVirgil describes every twist and turn of Aeneas's journey in stylish detail but the opening lines sum up the whole work beautifully. Whether you're someone who's read the book already or someone who will never get to it, this opening verse is worth spending a moment with :"Arma virumque cano, Troiae qui primus ab orisItaliam, fato profugus, Laviniaque venitlitora, multum ille et terris iactatus et altovi superum saevae memorem Iunonis ob iram;multa quoque et bello passus, dum conderet urbem,inferretque deos Latio, genus unde Latinum,Albanique patres, atque altae moenia Romae."Here is the J. W. Mackail prose translation from the parallel text:"I sing of arms and the man who of old from the coasts of Troy came, an exile of fate, to Italy and the shore of Lavinium; hard driven on land and on the deep by the violence of heaven, for cruel Juno's unforgetfulanger, and hard bestead in war also, ere he might found a city and carry his gods into Latium; from whom is the Latin race, the lords of Alba, and the stately city Rome."Alan Mandelbaum's translation:"I sing of arms and of a man: his fatehad made him fugitive; he was the firstto journey from the coasts of Troy as faras Italy and the Lavinian shores.Across the lands and waters he was batteredbeneath the violence of High Ones, forthe savage Juno’s unforgetting anger;and many sufferings were his in war—until he brought a city into beingand carried in his gods to Latium;from this have come the Latin race, the lordsof Alba, and the ramparts of high Rome."Robert Fitzgerald translates it like this:"I sing of warfare and a man at warFrom the sea-coast of Troy in early daysHe came to Italy by destiny,To our Lavinia western shore,A fugitive, this captain, buffettedCruelly on land and on seaBy blows from powers of the air—behind themBaleful Juno in her sleepless rage.And cruel losses were his lot in war,Till he could found a city and bring homeHis gods to Latium, land of the Latin race,The Alban lords, and the high walls of Rome."Finally, here's the Dryden version:"Arms, and the man I sing, who, forc'd by fate,And haughty Juno's unrelenting hate,Expell'd and exil'd, left the Trojan shore.Long labors, both by sea and land, he bore,And in the doubtful war, before he wonThe Latian realm, and built the destin'd town;His banish'd gods restor'd to rites divine,And settled sure succession in his line,From whence the race of Alban fathers come,And the long glories of majestic Rome."Did I read all four translations, you might wonder?Well, I mainly read the Mandelbaum, but when I came on passages I particularly liked, I checked how they read in the other versions.One such passage concerned a fierce storm by means of which Aeneas's powerful enemy Juno, sister and wife of Jupiter, tried to destroy Aeneas's fleet of ships as they neared Sicily. Here's how the storm dealt with one unfortunate ship:"Three times at that same spot the waters twistand wheel the ship around until a swiftwhirlpool has swallowed it beneath the swell."(Alan Mandelbaum)Fitzgerald's version:"Three times the eddying seaCarried the ship around in the same placeUntil the rapid whirlpool gulped it down."Here's Dryden's version:"……thrice round the ship was toss'd,Then bulg'd at once, and in the deep was lost"I wondered if Herman Melville had this passage in mind when he was writing the end of Moby Dick.If you're wondering how the rest of Aeneas's fleet survived Juno's fierce storm, well, he needed the help of his goddess mother, Venus (his father Anchises was human). Like the Greek heroes, Achilles and Odysseus, Trojan Aeneas has some superhuman powers but he is mortal and needs help from the Gods from time to time. So instead of continuing on to Italian shores after Juno's storm attacked his fleet off Sicily, Aeneas turns southwards with Venus's help, and his bruised and battered ships find safe harbor at Carthage on the North African coast, then ruled by Queen Dido. Dido falls in love with Aeneas but he knows he cannot stay with her since the fates have decreed that he must take his people to Italy and found the city of Rome (Virgil was writing this saga during the time of Augustus Caesar, centuries after the time in which Aeneas's odyssey is set, so he emphasizes the glory of the future city of Rome, and by naming Aeneas 'father Aeneas' at times, he associates him with Augustus in his readers's minds. Dryden even uses the word 'August' in his translation so we can't miss the parallel: "The Trojan chief appear'd in open sight, August in visage, and serenely bright."(my other translations didn't use the word 'August' but the parallels are still clear).But back to Carthage and Dido (who may prefigure Cleopatra with whom another Ceaser had a dalliance) here's a beautiful verse Aeneas speaks when he's thinking of leaving Carthage and Dido's arms:"In freta dum fluvii current, dum montibus umbraelustrabunt convexa, polus dum sidera pascet,semper honos nomenque tuum laudesque manebunt,quae me cumque vocant terrae.""While rivers run into the sea and shadowsstill sweep the mountain slopes and stars still pastureupon the sky, your name and praise and honorshall last, whatever be the lands that call me.”(Mandelbaum)"So long as brooks flow seaward, and the shadowsPlay over mountain slopes, and highest heavenFeed the stars, your name and your distinctionGo with me, whatever lands may call me." (Fitzgerald)"While rolling rivers into seas shall run,And round the space of heav'n the radiant sun;While trees the mountain tops with shades supply,Your honor, name, and praise shall never die."(Dryden)Before they leave Carthage, there's a fine personification of rumor:'Rumor,whose life is speed, whose going gives her force.Timid and small at first, she soon lifts upher body in the air. She stalks the ground;…………………fast-footedand lithe of wing, she is a terrifyingenormous monster with as many feathersas she has sleepless eyes beneath each feather(amazingly), as many sounding tonguesand mouths, and raises up as many ears.Between the earth and skies she flies by night,screeching across the darkness, and she nevercloses her eyes in gentle sleep. By dayshe sits as sentinel on some steep roofor on high towers, frightening vast cities;for she holds fast to falsehood and distortionas often as to messages of truth..."(AM)Alas for lovesick Dido, the rumor she hears concerning Aeneas's fleet finally leaving her shores is true, and she is destroyed by it.But Virgil moves on fast from Dido's destiny and the reader's attention is soon focused on the fierce descriptions of the ships facing into the waves:"—the skywas everywhere and everywhere the sea—""They cleave the furrows with their equal thrusts;the whole sea gapes, torn by the oars, the ships’three-pointed beaks."(AM)"Abreast they cleft their furrows, all the seaTorn up by oar strokes and the biting prows."(RF)"With shouts the sailors rend the starry skies;Lash'd with their oars, the smoky billows rise;Sparkles the briny main, and the vex'd ocean fries.Exact in time, with equal strokes they row:At once the brushing oars and brazen prowDash up the sandy waves, and ope the depths below."(JD)An interesting line I noted in this section was spoken by Aeneas's elderly father, the mortal man, Anchises:"Iam iam nulla mora est; sequor et qua ducitis adsum.""'Now, now delay is done with: I follow, and where you lead, I come'"(J. W. Mackail)It's a somehow familiar line (New Testament?) so it's interesting to see how the others translate it:"'Now my delay is done; I follow; whereyou lead, I am.'"(Mandelbaum)"'Now, now, no more delay. I'll follow you.Where you conduct me, there I'll be.'"(Fitzgerald)Dryden's version is more elaborate:"'Now, now,' said he, 'my son, no more delay!I yield, I follow where Heav'n shews the way.'"There was another familiar notion expressed in this section too:"they have the power because they feel they have it"(AM)"Who felt that they could do it, and so could."(RF)"For they can conquer, who believe they can."(JD)And so Aeneas reaches the coast of Italy but has to take a detour into the underworld just as Odysseus did—and Dante will do in Virgil's company.I enjoyed the colour descriptions of Charon's boat on the river Styx:"blue-black waters", "blue-black keel","blue-gray sedge"(AM)"dead-black water", "dusky craft", "grey-green sedge" (RF)"pressing water", "leaky vessel","muddy weeds" (JD:-)When Aeneas returns from the underworld, and continues travelling up the coast of Italy, there's a fine description of the rising sun which recalls Homer's famous 'rosy-fingered dawn':"And now the sea was red with sunrays, saffronAurora shone in her rose chariot;the winds fell off, and from the high air everyharsh blast was ended suddenly, the oarsbeat down against the waters’ sluggish marble."(AM)"Then soon to eastwardSea began to redden with dawn raysAnd saffron-robed Aurora in high heavenShone on her rosy car. Now suddenlyThe wind dropped, every breath of wind sank down,And oar-blades dipped and toiled in the sparkling calm."(RF)"Now, when the rosy morn began to rise,And wav'd her saffron streamer thro' the skies;When Thetis blush'd in purple not her own,And from her face the breathing winds were blown,A sudden silence sate upon the sea,And sweeping oars, with struggling, urge their way."(JD)I kept being drawn back to Dryden's rhyming couplets even though one of the introductions to the other translations claims he took liberties with the text. When I'd return to Mandelbaum afterwards, his more literal lines jarred a little with me. Fitzgerald was a happy medium. His lines seemed to flow better than Mandelbaum's and are thought to be more faithful than Dryden's. But still I read Mandelbaum and Dryden the most—the Fitzgerald hardback is not nearly so portable as my phone.Once Aeneas goes ashore in Italy, the story becomes a long series of battles because the people who live there are mostly not welcoming towards Aeneas and his people, or accepting of the fact that it is his destiny to colonise their country (and some have been manipulated by Juno too). Dryden translates Virgil's intention in this section very neatly:"For I shall sing of battles, blood, and rage,Which princes and their people did engage;And haughty souls, that, mov'd with mutual hate,In fighting fields pursued and found their fate;That rous'd the Tyrrhene realm with loud alarms,And peaceful Italy involv'd in arms."Though I'm not a lover of battle scenes, I was in awe at the visual quality of Virgil's composition. I thought I could see the hordes of diverse fighting men as they poured in from all sides. It reminded me of scenes from the film version ofLord of the Rings. And so many sons of gods were among them. So many plowmen too, their tools transformed to weapons.And even in the midst of describing fierce fighting, Virgil gives the reader a break with passages like this one:"As, when thick hail comes rattling in the wind,The plowman, passenger, and lab'ring hindFor shelter to the neighb'ring covert fly,Or hous'd, or safe in hollow caverns lie;But, that o'erblown, when heav'n above 'em smiles,Return to travel, and renew their toils:Aeneas thus, o'erwhelmed on ev'ry side,The storm of darts, undaunted, did abide;"(JD)Aeneas's main enemy is another mortal demigod called Turnus (they both seek a marriage with a local princess called Lavinia, daughter of the king of Latium). The drama in this final section of the book is intense, and Virgil leaves the reader divided as to who to root for. Sometimes I was cheering Aeneas and sometimes Turnus. Here's Turnus rousing his men: "Audentis Fortuna iuvat"Dryden translates it as: "Fortune befriends the bold."Mandelbaum as: "for fortune helps those who dare."The goddesses interfere in the conflict all the time, and on one occasion a simulacra of Aeneas is created to draw Turnus away from the battlefield towards safety. So we have an interesting scene with Turnus chasing after the vanishing Aeneas while the real Aeneas tries to chase after the vanishing Turnus. And Turnus being removed to safety makes his earlier words about fortune favoring the brave seem two-pronged. When he reaches safety, he is ashamed because fortune has favoured him and sent him away from the battlefield. But never fear, Turnus will get a chance to fight a last battle when Aeneas eventually tracks him down—as you might have guessed.I know this review is way too long so thanks for reading this far. I'll finish with a verse that reminded me of our current east/west divisions :"The neighb'ing nations of th' Ausonian shoreShall hear the dreadful rumor, from afar,Of arm'd invasion, and embrace the war"(JD)
