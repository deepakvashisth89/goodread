In the first of his two-volume memoirs, Loitering with Intent shows an adorably charming side of Peter O’Toole. If you’re looking for Hollywood stories, you won’t find them here. This book is focused strictly on his childhood and young adult years. When it ends, he hasn’t made a single film yet. In a way, though, that’s the best way to get to know him. As Barbra Streisand wrote, rather than changing you, success makes you more of who you already are. Getting to know young Peter is a treasure and a treat, and I’m looking forward to reading his second volume.​In movies, we’ve seen kids either getting sent to the countryside to escape London bombing or having to live through air raids in the city. They are portrayed as resilient, and when the war concludes, they’re never shown to have residual emotional problems because of those life-and-death situations. Peter wanted his readers to understand how much the children of war were affected, and it was a noble cause. He experienced both “movie scenarios”. He was sent to the country for years, and while in the city, he hid, terrified, in air raid shelters and practiced wearing a gas mask in school. It took its toll, and he effectively described to his readers how death and destruction were constantly on his mind. The only odd part of this book was the huge focus on Hitler. I think his readers could understand that Hitler dominated his thoughts, without giving him almost as much biographical information as Peter himself. It was a thin book anyway, and probably a third of it was taken up with stories or interpretations about Hitler.Besides that, Peter’s writing was surprisingly charming. His vocabulary was extensive, and choosing “toddled” instead of “walked”, for example, effected the right tone and immediately immersed the reader into his setting. We saw his rose-colored childhood, got to know his friends, and felt for the little boy who would have been perfect if he’d grown up during peacetime. Indeed, Peter admits, “there may have been another boy who [had a happier childhood], but if so, show me his photograph.” Usually, people go into the acting profession because they weren’t loved enough as a child, but that certainly wasn’t Peter’s case. He went to his first audition on a lark with a buddy, and then he was told he had promising talent. Perhaps that’s why he didn’t make very many films throughout his career, if he didn’t have the emotional lack of approval stemming from childhood.
