I read this at the behest of my student, the loud and too often proud, Jackson McPherson, who described it as "his favorite book ever." I must say that, unfortunately, I did not feel the same way.The book gets its title from the Dylan Thomas poem, "A Refusal to Mourn the Death, by Fire, of a Child in London," which is itself a call to remember that every death in major disaster is the death of a unique person, who deserves to be mourned as a person not a number. Cormier seeks to build up the personhood of each of his characters through changes in perspective throughout the book, but the gravity of each death is lost due to a number of reasons.Perhaps the chief stumbling block is the trephination of the plot; there were simply far too many gaps in the railroad trellis to give the suspense the weight Cormier desired. Why did they steal that bus? Why was the military so unbelievably incompetent? Why did the snipers not shoot everyone when Kate tried to escape? Was the Inner Delta shadow government actually important at all? Why was this omnipresent international terrorist group leader caught in some Boston apartment with no trouble? What are the terrorists trying to accomplish? The entire story just felt like amateur hour at the hostage situation.The two other main gripes I have would be the sexualization of the main female protagonist, Kate, and the Shutter Island ending of the story. The weirdly descriptive scenes of Kate urinating in just about every chapter are wholly unnecessary. The ending felt contrived and unsatisfying, like an M. Knight Shyamalan movie.There were some moments of decent tension, and the story was not so bad that it was unreadable. A lack of coherent motivations and world building, however, dropped the rating significantly.
