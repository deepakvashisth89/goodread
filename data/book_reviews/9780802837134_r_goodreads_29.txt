It is not uncommon to hear the idea that “truth is subjective,” and Wells’ 'No Place for Truth' traces the history of evangelicalism to understand how we got here. Much of Wells argument centers around the psychologizing of our society and the substitution for the knowledge of God/truth for an experiential knowledge of oneself, and that being evangelical has come to mean simply that “one has had a certain kind of religious experience” (7, 126, 130, 141,143). Much of this has less to do with the evangelical movement itself, and more with the sociological phenomena associated with modernity, secularization, and globalization (12, 77-78, 81). These problems create a society centered on commerce, detached from any religious foundation, and marked by anonymity through the loss of place and belonging. One example is the change in the role of clergy. Where clergy used to be “brokers of truth,” they now function more for psychological help or managing people (233). To become a “place for truth,” Wells believes that the primary solution centers around a recovery of objectivity in the Bible (279) and in the holiness of God (296).

Where 'No Place for Truth' excels is in its sociological history. Wells is incredible at mapping out the social factors that led us to a world that denies objective truth, embraces globalized commerce, and removed many of the traditional identity shapers we used to rely upon. Although I appreciate his diagnosis of our social environment, his proposed remedies remain largely theological and abstract and do not sufficiently explore how they can be lived out in real life. Where Wells does well at identifying the marks of a globalized society (impersonal, fast-paced, relative), he spends comparatively little time developing the ordinary practices of the local church that can address the social fragmentation he so effectively diagnoses. God has situated churches to be local: in a place and community. There, people can find belonging, ancient truth, and a spiritual family based on the real event of the resurrection of Jesus Christ.

It would be helpful for pastors and laymen to understand our climate. Although 'No Place for Truth' was written over 30 years ago, it reads almost prophetic in retrospect. We are more globalized than ever – with access to global opinions in a matter of seconds. As such, the opportunity to offend others has exploded exponentially. Pastors should recover not only objective doctrine but also the ordinary means of grace – faithful preaching, baptism, communion, church membership, discipline, prayer, and genuine Christian fellowship. In so doing, our churches can flourish on the foundation and confidence of truth as the Word is preached, as Christian love and charity are given freely, and the truest sense of belonging can be experienced.
