“I had a farm in Africa at the foot of the Ngong Hills.”

From its very first sentence, "Out of Africa" captivated me. It is enchanting, old-fashioned, poignant, wistful, and insightful. Karen Blixen’s account of her life in Africa, told through a series of reminiscences from 1914 to 1931, portrays her deep love for that country — its people, its landscapes, and its animals. At times, it has the quality of a fairy tale, filled with beauty and melancholy.

Blixen is a master storyteller. Her ability to transform memories into vivid narratives makes it easy to understand why Denys Finch Hatton loved listening to her recount her stories. She does not merely describe Africa; she recreates the feeling of a place that shaped her life forever.

The book, however, is not without its difficulties for modern readers. As a work of its time, some passages may challenge contemporary sensibilities, particularly those concerning race, colonial society, and her descriptions of hunting. Blixen was undeniably a product of her aristocratic background and historical period. Nevertheless, I did not find in her writing a sense of ethnic superiority; instead, what stands out is her profound attachment to Africa and her genuine admiration for the land and the people who became part of her world.

Yet the Africa she describes no longer exists. Her coffee farm eventually fails despite her determination and efforts to save it, and her African adventure slowly comes to an end. The loss is beautifully expressed in one of the most moving passages of the book:

“It was not I who was going away, I did not have it in my power to leave Africa, but it was the country that was slowly and gravely withdrawing from me, like the sea in ebb-tide.”

Out of Africa is more than a memoir of a place; it is a meditation on memory, belonging, and loss. Through Blixen’s extraordinary storytelling, a vanished world remains alive in the imagination.

Highly recommended.
