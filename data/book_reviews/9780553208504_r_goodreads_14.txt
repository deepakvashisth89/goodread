What could possibly entice me to read a 635-page novel? It wasn’t the Nobel Prize for Literature awarded in 2007. Nor was it a desire to tackle a feminist classic. It was something much simpler: late author Nora Ephron once spoke about how deeply this novel affected her, and that stayed with me. I wanted to understand why.I ordered “The Golden Notebook” from my local library and read it in-between other books.Through my interest in this book and the author, I learned that she insisted she was “completely unconscious of writing a feminist book,” despite her evident frustration with the feminist movement. In a 1994 New York Times interview, she remarked that while feminism unleashed an “explosion of energy,” that energy was ultimately talked away. Those conflicted feelings seem to live inside Anna Wulf, the novel’s central figure, who becomes a vessel for expressing the realities of women of that time—their memories, ambitions, disappointments, and emotional complexity.The New York Times Book Review said:“The Golden Notebook is Doris Lessing’s most important work and has left its mark upon the ideas and feelings of a whole generation of women.”So how did it feel reading it in 2025/2026?Writer Anna Wulf, attempts to work through a period of writer’s block by documenting her life in four separate, color-coded notebooks. Each represents a distinct phase or aspect of her experiences, with the fifth—the golden notebook—serving as her attempt at wholeness and self-realization. (Hence, the title of the book.)Through these notebooks, Lessing explores themes of feminism, political ideology, war, and mental health. Some passages feel rooted firmly in their time; others feel unexpectedly current, especially in their examination of inner conflict and identity.There is an extensive cast of characters who move in and out of Anna’s life, reflecting her evolving perspectives and growth over time.Reading The Golden Notebook felt less like finishing a story and more like sitting with someone else’s life for a while—its contradictions, frustrations, and questions.Although not an easy read, it still was thought-provoking, insightful, and a fascinating social commentary of its era.For readers willing to go along on this journey, The Golden Notebook offers a deeply rewarding—and sometimes illuminating—experience.
