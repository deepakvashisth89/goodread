This review only refers to the titular novella - in fact I had the Agberg e-edition that contained just the novella itself, but could not find that one on GoodReads.Sailing to Byzantiumis another take at human immortality in a post-scarcity society - in the year 50000 there are only 5 cities, but all are spectacular examples from Earth past (with some liberties to make them even more stunning). Once in a while (maybe every few years, but time is not really something the people are concerned about) one of them is demolished and replaced by a different one. Asgard, Venice, Rome, "New Chicago" (from ~2500), Alexandria, Chang-An, an endless sequence of beautiful places for the relatively small number of "citizens" to enjoy. Most of the population consists of "temporaries", apparently non-sentient quasi-people who keep the cities working and provide background characters and services for the citizen tourists. Charles Phillips, the protagonist, is a "visitor", someone apparently translated from 1984 New York (the time and place the book was written) to this magical wonderland. UnlikeArthur C. ClarkeinThe City and the Stars, Silverberg seems to be content with this state of Earth in the future, and is only concerned with his main characters finding their (joint) individual happiness in it - in the end they are, of course, sailing from Alexandria to the newly opened city of Byzantium.The first thing I noticed was how incredibly well-written the story is. Silverberg's prose is impeccable, and he effortlessly integrates parts ofW.B. Yeats's original poem into the story. Also, the individual journey of the protagonist is interesting, plausible, and in the end quite satisfactory. What leaves me a bit unsatisfied is the shallowness and ultimate pointlessness of the society. We hear nothing about the "planners" who build new cities (and demolish old ones), and while happy immortality seems great for our heroes, where is it going? Still, an easy 5 stars, and not just because I just came of a binge of very bad third-rate 1960s German pulp SF.
