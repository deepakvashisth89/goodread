First published in 1995, this biography about the British royal family is, not too surprisingly, still valid today. Starting with the dreaded Georges of the 18th century and then continuing through the dreaded 1990s, author Donald Spoto provides a full background to the family everyone loves to discuss. Because of its publication date, it does not include the death of Princess Diana, but the characterizations of the leading players feel as though not much has changed through the years.

Spoto begins with the history of how the current Windsors came to be. When Queen Anne died, the true next-in-line heir was passed over because of his Catholic religion. Paying the price for their ridiculousness, the British crown then went to George I of Hanover, who couldn’t even speak English. Then came George II, George III (who lost the American colonies), and finally George IV (who couldn’t fit through a door). When William IV took over the throne as the next brother, his one goal was to stay alive long enough to ensure the ascendancy of the teenage Victoria. At this point, the public were weary of the Hanoverians, so even though Queen Victoria was as Teutonic as her predecessors, she came off as being more in line with the British spirit and so ushered in the Victorian Age. Her son, Edward VII, had to wait a long time until mama died, but he charmed his way through life and prepared the way for the first Windsor, his son George V.

George V was the first Windsor monarch because the British royal family realized that with WWI causing such despair across the United Kingdom, a name change was in order. So, in 1917, the official name of the royals changed from the German House of Saxe-Coburg and Gotha to the House of Windsor. It sounded better. The new name also fit the king’s style, which was austere and traditional, breaking away from all the nutbucket Georges who had preceded him. While the new family name sounded very British, it would mean something else when the future Edward VIII thankfully abdicated, became the Duke of Windsor, and dragged that name around for the rest of his life. George VI and Elizabeth II would put the wackiness behind permanently, as both strove to serve as professional monarchs, seeing their jobs as a duty as opposed to frolicsome privilege.



By the late twentieth century, however, the knots were fraying. Divorce, which caused Edward VIII to abdicate, was now an everyday thing in the royal family. Princess Margaret. Princess Anne. Prince Charles. Prince Andrew. It just went on and on. Even Windsor Castle caught fire, causing one to wonder if the Queen would remain Queen before the new century arrived. It seemed the royals were on their way out, but Elizabeth held firm, even if the other family members forgot their sense of duty.

The book takes a hard look at each member of Queen Elizabeth’s family, from her parents to her sister to her youngest son. We see the traditional and serious Charles, who has since carried that same sense of duty to the throne, even though he’s already in his last years. Much as Edward VII had to endure a long wait and a short reign just to prepare his own son, so it appears King Charles III is using his time in charge to make way for the future William V. Princess Anne, who I think would have been the best choice for a monarch, comes across just as serious and professional. Alas, the same cannot be said for the other brethren, especially Prince Andrew (or now known as Andrew Mountbatten-Windsor thanks to big brother putting his royal foot down). Even in the 1990s, Andrew was a pain-in-the-arse, thank you very much, using his “favorite son” privilege to wreak havoc everywhere he went. Spoiled, entitled, smug…even his active combat role in the Falklands War couldn’t save him from the public’s loathing (and continued loathing) of him.

This was a very interesting read, as one can see where Spoto was going with this from the get-go. It’s clear that he doesn’t really admire the Windsors, which is understandable. But I think that even in the 1990s, he underrated the Queen’s ability to pull everything together, thanks to that strength of will she had. The author also surprised me with his take on the Duchess of Windsor, who almost helped destroy the British monarchy. It’s a new way of looking at her, basically promoting a view that she really didn’t want to marry Edward VIII. I’m not sure about that, but he provides some research to support this take. Still, I ended up admiring some of the monarchs, even if I think the entire line could have been much improved if the Stuart line had been allowed to resume their royal-ness.
