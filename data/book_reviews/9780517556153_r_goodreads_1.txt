"War, HOO! What is it good for?!"Yeah, not much actually, particularly in the modern nuclear-armed world.That's the inescapable conclusion you'll likely reach after reading Gwynne Dyer'sWar, a fascinating exploration of one of humanity's greatest curses, and the source of both misery and death for countless hundreds of millions throughout history.War has seemingly been with us forever, and is largely accepted as something we will never be without. We dedicate massive sums to preparation for it (my home, Australia, has budgeted 42.7 billion dollars for defense in 2020-21) and spend vast intellectual resources on designing gear for it.Defense is an almost unquestionable section of most national budgets, beyond criticism and audit, ensconced in an impenetrable aura of nationalism and realpolitic. To question it is to render yourself unelectable in many nations.But was it always so, and more importantly, will it always be that way?The answers, according to Dyer, are "sort of" and "no, unless we all want to die in nuclear fire".While it seems inter tribal conflict has been around a long time (and was quite deadly in pre-modern societies) war as an organized activity is more recent, and as a professionalized activity even more so.This is a fascinating work, filled with mind boggling facts and figures, and insights into behaviors an practices in previous eras of warfare that seem utterly insane to modern eyes, such as:Some 19th century armies had so many horses that they needed 4000 acres of grassPER DAYjust to keep them fed.Many armies in history have had issues with soldiers refusing to kill their opponents. Thousands of muskets retrieved from Gettysburg were double or even triple loaded with shot - their owners were only pretending to fire on the enemy, presumably to avoid breaking societal prohibitions regarding murder. To counteract such humanitarian impulses modern Western armies now explicitly indoctrinate soldiers with the drive to kill their enemies.People marched and stood in the crazy 17th & 18th century musket formations - you know, where they got mowed down like grass in front of the opposition's rifles - because that was the best tactic for victory with their crappy guns that only worked at short range and en masse. They may look nuts to us, but then so does the phalanx, where the men at the front are pretty much sure to get speared (or crushed between their fellows and the enemy) when they encounter a rival unit. The phalanx too, was the best way to win in its day, and winning was the game.Beyond all these fascinating facts, Dyer also shows that war isn't what it once was. War between rival prehistoric low-tech tribes was grinding, but cheap. War between European professional European armies with muskets was expensive, but fairly quick and largely confined losses to the soldiery. War between industrial powers was cripplingly expensive, grindingly long, and resulted in the destruction of cities, industry and millions of civilians.Now, with modern weaponry and nuclear weapons war between global powers is so costly. and potentially so devastating, that it has ceased to be an effective tool for settling disputes between nation states of that level. Even if kept to a low level, the cost of weapons (a fighter jet now costs approximately 175 times what a WW2 spitfire did, adjusted for inflation) and their complexity means that a state can't replace the weapons it loses in war fast enough - in a sense they are too valuable to be risked in war. Even the US, a major military power, ran low on cruise missiles during its campaign in Kosovo.It is likely that it wouldn't take long before a state incurred enough material losses to begin looking at a nuclear strike as an option to prevent defeat.If we are to avoid the nuclear fire, it is likely that we will need to strengthen international institutions such as the UN, and work globally to end violent interstate conflict. A failure to do so runs the risk that any given small conflict could escalate into one where one side or the other is tempted to initiate a a nuclear strike that could spark off the apocalypse.This won't please the "UN AGENDA 21 IS TRYING TO CONTROL US" nutters who will view any extension of international cooperation as some sort of wacky plot, but I'd rather deal with a few irate tinfoil-hatters than the end of human civilization.The answer to the iconic question I posed at the start of this review is simple. What war is good for is heralding the nuclear apocalypse, and making Cormac McCarthy'sThe Roadinto a work of nonfiction. We had best cure ourselves of the habit of warfare, and do it quickly.Four swords beaten into plowshares out of five.
