A poetically brilliant and complex literary exploration of capitalist and patriarchal domination over land, nature, animals, and women. Margaret Atwood, you're a fucking genius. I would give you my kidney, bro.

This was my first real introduction to eco-feminism; I took a Nature and Culture class in college and the teacher half-ass explained the concept, saying "a lot of people find eco feminism to be sexist because it essentially equates the female body to animals. So, let's move on."
On the contrary, Atwood seamlessly blends immersive nature writing with scathing displays of male domination, demonstrating how a sense of ownership and a lack of empathy in extractive settler cultures extends beyond acquisition over land, murder of animals for sport or pleasure, and the flattening of plants (considering them lifeless beings, objects to use, destroy, and discard) to the realm of the domestic, envisioning women as identical conquests: food, sport, mother thing, sex object.

Atwood doesn't shy away from saying what she means here, referencing the "American" as a virus that takes hold of the spirit and creates gluttonous, soulless monsters of consumerism, who will lie, charm, and kill to acquire that which will temporarily satisfy their hunger. I loved how radical and scathing her narrator was in her condemnation of consumerism and settler ideology/westward expansion, as well as the hypocrisy of her views at times (claiming that people only see a flag or trust others based on a homogenized identity, but then implicitly trusting a group of men fishing once she realized they were Canadian... before quickly reassigning them as Americans, due to their gluttony with the fish and their loud energy.)



The first fifty pages or so were hard to get into, but once I began to understand the narrator and the cadence of her thought patterns, which involved abstract and disjointed reflections that moved through time, I began to love the book. She's purposefully difficult to understand as she slips through her memories, attempting to understand herself and the formation of her identity.

The disappearance of her father came to symbolize the death of a good man's conception of his place in the world and the realization that one's good intentions will always shrink in favor of their actions. In this vein, Atwood examines different facets of the patriarchy through each of her characters, artfully tying in the ways in which women abandon one another in favor of survival under such conditions. The narrator exists as a sort of genderless being, playing witness to the forces that shape her into a woman and a human against her will.
Ultimately, she battles with her surroundings and place in the world, resorting to extreme measures to gain an understanding of it all and potentially usher in a fresh beginning for a new type of humanity.

Almost every time I've dipped my toes into psychedelics, I have grieved heavily that I am a human and that I am playing a role that I never chose; that the violence inherent the human condition is inescapable and that the pain I have caused, as part of this "humanity" and as an American, to other living beings and the earth, is so palpable and far-reaching. I've tried, on more than one occasion, to lay on the ground and shrink my person until I am one with the dirt, the grasses, the bugs. I have also watched birds flying overhead and willed, until my head ached, that I could somehow transfigure into a being that causes less harm through her existence. I have begged the sun to take me and make me anew.

Grappling with these existential themes on every page and consistently rejecting human exceptionalism, this novel resonated so deeply with me.

If you enjoy ambiguity, poetry, conceptual literature, and are an environmentalist to your core, a feminist, and a lover of nature - you might have just found your next favorite book.
