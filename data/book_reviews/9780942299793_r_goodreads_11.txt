The book about how the spectacle consumes us, and about what technology under capital does to attention. Excellent alongside "Manufacturing Consent". Though Chomsky wouldn't recommend it, and I know that pairing is mine rather than received.

Most of us know Chomsky and Herman's propaganda model and how the media manufactures consent. What's understudied is Debord's wider claim: that the spectacle isn't confined to media at all, that a consumer society is one where we consume spectacles more or less everywhere, and that lived experience gets steadily displaced by its representation. That idea is less discussed and more relevant than the media critique it sits beside.

What he doesn't do is take the easy exit. He ties it all to technology without collapsing into primitivism — the problem isn't the machine, it's what capital does with it. That distinction is why the book has aged as well as it has.

He was prescient, and I don't use that word loosely. Mobile phones. The internet. Facebook, Twitter, clicktivism. Google. If it still sounds far-fetched, look at how the attention business actually works. Nicholas Carr, writing four decades after Debord, lays out the mechanism: the intellectual technologies Google pioneered promote fast, shallow skimming and discourage sustained engagement with any single argument or narrative, because the company's profits are tied to the velocity of our information intake. The faster we move across the surface of the web, the more links we click, the more pages we view, the more Google learns about us and the more advertisements it serves. The advertising system is explicitly built to work out which messages will grab our attention and then put them in front of us. Every click is a break in concentration, a bottom-up disruption — and it is in Google's economic interest that we click as often as possible. Irene Au, then head of user experience there, put it without any decoration: get users in and out really quickly. All their design decisions were built on that.

In plain terms, the distraction is the product.

Which brings me to the main problem with the book, and it's a real one: it is most definitely not written in plain terms. Most people cannot read this, which rather defeats the purpose. A theory of how attention is destroyed, written in a form that demands more sustained attention than almost anything else on the shelf, has a difficulty it can't solve from the inside. That's why the writing gets a four and not a five — not obscurity for its own sake, but a style that excludes the people it's describing.

Still, the book is important, and what it understands is the thing that matters: how spectacle, once technology is in the hands of capital, alienates people, marginalises them, and dissolves commonality and community. Google isn't trying to empower anyone. What that business model needs is a consumer-oriented, historically amnesiac, distracted, mobility-addicted population locked in a permanent cycle of spectacle — and it has one.

We need to be concerned. We need to reclaim the cyber-commons. And we need to read slowly and carefully, which is the one instruction this book gives by being what it is.

Five. Read alongside "The Revolution of Everyday Life".
