It's a good, comprehensive view of events but not much insight into Diana's actual personality. There's a lot of what happened but not much of why it happened or how Diana felt about the events. We're told Diana is this clever woman, a forceful personality, but though we're told what she is we're never shown. Where is her wit? Her knowledge of art or music? In what way was her personality so strong? Everything she had or could have been seems to be consumed by her devotion to her husband. She remains a cool, calm, blank slate except when it comes to an almost psychotic devotion to Mosley. She dedicates her life to him body and soul. Turning viciously on anyone she perceives as not entirely for him.
The story of her internment is told sympathetically. The author seems to see the actions of those who raised concerns, about Diana's contacts and activities, as almost underhand and petty. But she WAS in contact with the highest ranking Nazis, HAD been involved in transferring foreign money to British fascist causes and DID want Britain to lose the war. She was so much of an influence and well thought of in fascist circles that her fellow fascist internees were falling over themselves to clean for her. The widely held opinion that she would be dangerous if not in prison during the war never faces any examination. She seemed to have way too much information about German military manoeuvres, but there's never any real probing into what this says about her involvement and contacts. Instead we get pages and pages about the filth and dangers of prison and how terrible it was for her to be seperated from her husband.
It feels very relevant that she, even while publicly known as being an enemy of the country in a time of war, had family and friends in Downing Street campaigning for her interests. The general public were 77% against the Mosleys, the press was against them - but the people in power were more in their favour? What did this mean for the wider war effort? What did this kind of opinion split and it's power balance mean? What can we take away from any of this in terms of dealing with fascism? Dealing with people in power or with close ties to power? There's no exploration of any of these kind of questions. When the war ends the narrative just moves on to them being completely free - there's nothing about the reaction of either of the Mosleys to the defeat of Nazism or the fates of Hitler and the rest of their German friends.

Oswald Mosley's own personality comes across in narcissistic, sociopathic clarity however. He ends up dominating much of the book and Diana's story. He was absolutely foul, not just because of his politics but his actions as a husband and father. The way he treats women is extraordinarily disgusting. Not just the wife he was cruel to, the affair partners he seems to use as he wishes (though I do wish we had more examination of their actions - because what on earth were any of them thinking?) but also his own daughter who he seems to disown and discard while being a much closer active father figure to his sons and stepsons.
I was really surprised this was based on interviews with Diana herself as I did not get that sense at all during reading. There's very little in the way of actual quotes from Diana outside of short extracts from letters she wrote and, like I said, there is so little actual insight into the events or her feelings about them. This book could have been assembled from documentary sources entirely and I don't see what would have been different.
