LENA: "Seem like God didn't see fit to give the black man nothing but dreams" (I.I.46)I read this play in high school almost 30 years ago. I have seen this on stage twice. I have seen three of its movie adaptations. I have taught this play time and time again."A Raisin in the Sun" is one of the most powerful plays about the American Dream, and a black family's fight to be seen and heard. I read this play in high school, and now as a teacher, teach it every now and then to my high school kids who are often moved by Ms. Hansberry's moody, biting dialogue.I recently had to give this a reread: It still remains as humorous and biting, as unsentimental and wonderful, and as big-hearted as the first time I read it.I must say that I can't understand anyone ever disliking this play, or even rating it less than 5 stars because it truly is a masterpiece of American literature.Written during the height of the Black Arts Movement, it remains revolutionary since when Hansberry wrote A Raisin in the Sun. It remains a cornerstone of one of the greatest works by an African American woman.I am baffled that there are opinions out there on Goodreads land that actually demean this play, and do it without any true nuance.In terms of how impactful this play was, it belongs with A Streetcar Named Desire, Death of a Salesman, Long Day’s Journey into Night, and Angels in America as the big five.I take it personally when negative things are written about this play. I think it is so searing, and such an indictment of how black Americans felt during the 1950s- that in many ways, the play is a lot bleaker than it is uplifting.I feel as if some of the negative comments stem from readers own insecurity, discomfort with race, and even aggression that the story might seem too cliched or even too triggering from those who don’t want to see life through the black experience.The 1961 legendary filmed version starring original cast members: Sidney Poitier (as Walter Lee); Claudia McNeil (as the towering Lena) and Ruby Dee (as Ruth, the most complicated member of the Younger family) was also part of my formative years as a reader and began my love of theater and film.The Younger family's hunger for a piece of the American Dream, with all of their dreams hanging by a thread is all too relatable.For those who do not know the story, Ms. Hansberry's play centers on a social security check that Lena Younger has inherited from her deceased husband.They live in a dilapidated apartment in Chicago where it is too cramped, and there is too much tension. It's one of the ultimate masterpieces of the kitchen sink drama.The check that Lena has inherited represents a dream for each of the family: Lena wants to buy a house at Clybourne Park- a white neighborhood. The house has a room for each of the family members, and a yard for her grandson Travis to play in.Walter wants to use the money as part of a business venture with his friends Willy and Bobo- they want to open a liquor store. They want to be their own boss, and not work for anyone else anymore.Walter's sister Beneatha wants to be a doctor. She is in college and has two suitors to choose from: Nigerian, Asagi, or rich and wealthy George.Finally, noble Ruth just wants a better life for everyone as she knows both her husband and mother in law have suffered the indignities of both the Great Migration, and systemic racism that has worn them down.When an inevitable tragedy threatens to tear the family apart, Lena declares, "sometimes you just got to know when to give up some things" (III.I.140) countering Walter's earlier declaration of "I want so many things that they are kind of driving me crazy" (I.II.73).It's an epic, and intimate masterpiece of American Drama that really peels back the desire for the good life, and in a way that could provide some reprieve from the political upheavals that were happening during the 1950's and 1960's during the Civil Rights Movement.Finally it’s all about the love that a household run by women have for Walter and Travis.Lena loves her children with all her might, and she is a mighty force to be reckoned with. Ruth loves Walter more than he will ever know.All women love Travis and both want to show him the way to what it means to be a man and to have a stake in something.Beneatha wants to learn and blossom and is filled with a curiosity that’s bigger than what the rest of her family desires. She still loves her family too.I have been lucky to have read the sequel "Clybourne Park" by Bruce Norris in which the Younger house is now for sale in the 2000s, where a black family now is trying to decide whether to sell it to a white couple. This riff establishes the gentrification and the opposite of the white flight many cities experienced with black homeowners moving into white neighorhoods.I have seen the play twice live: A 2018 Off Broadway production in Harlem that was a gorgeous, understated memory play.I saw a misguided, but still compelling production at the Public Theater in 2023 starring legendary actress Tonya Pinkins as Lena; Francois Battiste as Walter, Paige Gilbert as Bennie, and Mandi Madsen as Ruth.There are two other solid filmed adaptations besides the definitive one starring Mr. Poitier, Ms. McNeil, and Ms. Dee.There's a 1989 Broadway production recorded for PBS starring Danny Glover and Esther Rolle, and a 2004 adaptation based on the Kenny Leon Broadway revival starring Sean Combs as Walter; Phylicia Rashad as Lena and Broadway’s GOAT: Audra McDonald as Ruth.However, read the play first- then watch the 1961 film with Mr. Poitier and Ms. McNeil. You'll never forget them.
