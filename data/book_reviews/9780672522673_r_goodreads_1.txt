50 years ago, Edwin Newman wrote this book about his experience with the English language of his time as an extension to his first publication, Strictly Speaking. For Newman, practicing language, especially writing, should be a precise affair when possible, although some rule bending is to be encouraged from time to time. Sloppiness seems to be the cardinal sin that must be rebutted by the modern practitioner; after all, the point of this technology is to communicate, to share ideas, and half-hearted attempts at this are not only a disservice to the members of the conversation, they are a crime against the medium.Newman addresses the flak he got from the pedantry that escapes between the lines of his first book, and which I was planning on calling out in this review, but his self-awareness has pardoned him by my reckoning. He asks readers to interpret his repeated exercises in mock corrupted prose as a form of doting affection for his American English. I can grant him this, but he is of the old guard and I think it is out of pity for them that I make the excuse. The old guard is dead and American English has mobilized and globalized.A Civil Tongue predates the Internet and what it did to language, though the writing was on the wall and Newman was reading the signs in the subtext. Having passed away in 2010, I’m now curious if the author wrote on the topic later in his life, or if his retirement from NBC in 1984 and the scattered projects rounding out the decade prompted an increasing disengagement from televised dueling of pens and swords. He does not seem to be the type of fellow who would keep his opinions on these things to himself, so I will keep an eye out.The journalism that Newman champions seems to be in short supply now, or it is simply too sophisticated and becomes buried under the avalanche of partisan detritus that clogs up the pipes of our modern news machines. I think the most important phrase from the entire book was tucked into an unassuming paragraph on page 107, and if nothing else is to be recalled this proverb stands firmly on its own: “We are all safer when language is specific. It improves our chances at knowing what is going on”
