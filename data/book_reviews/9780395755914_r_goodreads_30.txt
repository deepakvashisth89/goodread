4.5 stars!this collection is obsessed with pain that thinks, and so am i!!what drew me in most was Atwood's insistence that sadness is not just emotional, but inherited, chemical, psychic. in "A Sad Child", grief feels like something passed down, something that settles early and quietly, long before it can be named – a kind of origin wound.in "Waiting", the horror never arrives as spectacle. instead, it turns out to be familiar. domestic. like home. the dark thing you've prepared yourself for isn't monstrous at all – it's memory, recognition, december evenings. the fear isn't the attack; it's the return.i was especially drawn to Atwood's mythic women – figures who are done being symbols.Cressida, disgusted by pityHelen, erotic and annihilatingSekhmet, (many flies are alive / while he is not — one of the coldest lines I’ve ever read)and Half-Hanged Mary, feral, holy, furious, alive. that poem feels like bodily theology: i hurt, therefore i am. pain not as weakness, but as proof of existence.again and again, the body appears as an archive – holding what language can't. in "Cell" survival forgets how to die. in “Down”, there’s the unbearable line about making a cut “for the pain to get in” in “Girl Without Hands” and “Shapechangers in Winter”, loss is written directly onto flesh, cells, touch. grief isn’t abstract here; it’s anatomical.there is also a powerful pull toward water, death, return – toward the womb, and toward a refusal to finish grieving in “Two Dreams, 2,” “The Ottawa River by Night,” and “Statuary”finally, “A Fire Place” reframes destruction itself. the earth wounds itself constantly, without regret. only humans call it damage. only we mourn the burned place.i love poems where pain is intelligent, where bodies are sacred in unclean ways, and where women are most powerful when they're angry, unfinished, or impossible to redeem. and this collection DELIVERS
