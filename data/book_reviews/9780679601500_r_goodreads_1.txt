Finishing Edward Gibbon's DECLINE AND FALL OF THE ROMAN EMPIRE, over 3,000 pages, I feel the way the great historian did when he finally put down his pen in 1787: exhausted, elated, enraged at the subject, and proud of him naturally. Though, unlike Gibbon, I did not take a walk in my acacia garden after completing my task. This third and concluding volume of the "great and awful" history of the empire at sunset defies the mind to reach higher in understanding politics, religion, warfare, and the fate of nations. Ironically, Gibbon had no wish to write this book. His initial aim was to finish with the fall of the Roman Empire in the West after the Vandals sacked Rome in A.D. 476. Why did he change his mind? The ghastly spectacle of the one-thousand-year slow death of the Eastern empire at Constantinople aroused his all-pervasive curiosity. How could such a monster linger on for a millennium until 1453 and leave not a trace behind? How could the Muslims, heirs to a religion born in the mind of an illiterate Arab merchant, capture the Second Rome? The answer, and story, is not pretty. This third volume is filled with gloom, historical pessimism, and constant bloodshed. The book contains no heroes comparable to Marcus Aurelius, Justinian, or Heraclius from the previous two volumes. Statecraft and the arts both fell with Rome. In what was left of the West, the Franks forged and then lost their kingdom, and venal popes fought with savage Germanic emperors. Gibbon gives them a wink now and then, but this is primarily the sanguinary chronicle of Byzantium and the House of Islam in constant war for supremacy in Asia, Africa, and Europe. These pages belong to Genghis Khan and Timurlane, idiot families reigning in the East, and the awful glory of the Caliph Omar, Saladin, and Mehmet II, conqueror of Constantinople.Gibbon begins his history of these lugubrious times by looking at what he calls the weakest link of European civilization, the kingdom of the Franks and the Empire of Charlemagne. To be sure, he finds little civilization in Western Europe. He still calls the Franks, Goths, and Vandals "barbarians". His fondness for the British Empire no doubt prejudices his view, accurate though it may be. Gibbon thinks an empire should pass on both stable political institutions and advances in the arts and science. Charlemagne, the first monarch since the fall of Rome to claim dominance over Christendom, did neither. The illiterate king waged war on Saxony; Himmler later dubbed him "slayer of the Saxons", in the name of a religion he barely understood and had no use for. His one contribution to European history was a pilgrimage to Rome to be crowned Emperor of the Romans, a spurious title, by Pope Leon. Leo thought this act cemented the alliance of the papacy with earthly power. Charlemagne understood this gesture in reverse: "no pope can survive without me", kindling the first fire of freedom from Rome. After his death, laments Gibbon, the Carolinian Empire split into petty kingdoms presided over by his sons, then subdivided by countless barons and lords, marking the beginning of feudalism in the West. His Holy Roman Empire was, to quote Voltaire, neither holy, Roman, nor an empire. Europe was back to where it was when Rome fell 350 years earlier, without a central political authority and only rudimentary agriculture. No science, inventions or literary achievements to boast of. The Latin language went into decline as well, used only by the Pope and his entourage at the Vatican, monastic orders scattered over Europe, and ironically, the court of the Emperor of the East, lording over a Greek-speaking people. Gibbon thanks Charles Martel for saving Europe from Islam at the Battle of Poitiers, described in lavish detail, for otherwise we'd have mosques in London and Paris, and in the opposite direction, Otto the Great, King of Germany, for marching on Rome and showing the pope who was in charge of Europe. Yet, popes were not powerless. They held in their hands the one thing that united Europe, Christianity, and the growing divisions between Germans, Italians, and Franks allowed them to pit these powers against each other, with Rome the prize and a papal blessing to boot. Gibbon cannot resist, though, in offering a scathing portrait of the ideal pope: over sixty years old, celibate, with no appreciation of either the fair sex or family, and educated entirely in a hermetic world of dogma and superstition. Readers may draw whatever relevance they may find for today.Gibbon finds himself obliged in this volume to take a global view of history. Arabia, Russia, China, and India all played a part in shaping the European saga. Europe in the Dark Ages found itself surrounded and besieged on all sides. To the north, the Vikings unleashed their fleets from Normandy to Sicily, birthing new kingdoms along the way. Readers can lose count of how many times Sicily changed hands as the centuries passed, though Gibbon does admire Princes Robert and Roger. Through the Rus, the Vikings brought order to Russia, creating the first political state apparatus. North Africa and Spain represented the threat of the Saracenes, though a full discussion must await Gibbon's analysis of the origins and spread of Islam. But the menace no one saw coming lay in deepest and darkest Asia, with Genghis Khan and his Mongol hordes. Timujin of Mongolia invented a terrifying new form of cavalry warfare that, in just a few decades, made him and his sons masters of northern China, India to the gates of Delhi, and Russia, from the Black Sea region to Moscow and Novgorod. Strange stories of Mongol horrors reached Constantinople and Rome, and for the first time since the might of Augustus conquered the known world, Europe feared disappearing from the map. Gibbon finds in Genghis a virtue unique for this baleful era, religious toleration. The Great Khan espoused a vague monotheism and respected the Muslims, Jews, Christians, and Hindus who fell before his horses. If his empire disappeared, it was due to the usual suspects---inept sons who fought for the spoils among themselves. Had he framed a working code of laws and political institutions to go with them, who knows but we might all be speaking Mongolian today. The successor to Genghis and his Mongols, Timurlane, or Timur the Lame, nominally a Muslim from Samarkand, overthrew the whole ancient world. He and his line swept into China and completed the Mongol conquest, erecting a new dynasty, plowed into India, abolished the Delhi sultanate, and established the Mughal Empire, which lasted until the coming of the British under Clive, decapitated the Abbasid dynasty in Persia, the last to trace its lineage back to the Prophet Mohammed, moved into Syria and Anatolia, looked with avarice upon Constantinople, and proclaimed he would reach Gibraltar and the Atlantic. Only a rebellion in Damascus, forcing him to abandon his planned conquest of Byzantium, kept him from fulfilling his promise. Gibbon is amazed by this revolutionary, but laments that he is remembered only for piling up skulls in every city he conquered.Only after he has chronicled the Muslim wars of conquest in Asia, Africa, and Iberia is Gibbon ready to take a step back and delineate the birth, growth, and world-shaking strength of this new faith, which in less than one hundred years after the death of Mohammed in A.D. 632 could boast of adherents and kingdoms from the Himalayas to the Pyrenees. He apologizes to his readers for not knowing Arabic, and asks we take English and French sources with a grain of salt. Unusual for Gibbon, he begins with a sweeping look at the physical, environmental, religious, and political landscape of Arabia in A.D. 570, the birth year of Mohammed and the site of a future civilization and superpower. The Arabs of the sixth century found themselves trapped between two giants engaged in a cold war, Persia and Byzantium, "Rome" the Arabs called it, who both coveted Arabia Felix or Yemen. Ironically, this helped the Arabs to maintain their liberty. Neither power could move without igniting a conflagration. Freedom from both the Christians and the Magians allowed the denizens of the Arab Peninsula to forge their own faith. It also protected the Jews of Arabia, from whom Mohammed doubtless imbibed monotheism. Like Max Weber, after him, Gibbon dissects the crystalline features of desert religions: an all-powerful and often vindictive God; intolerance of other faiths, asceticism, although not monasticism, required of the faithful, and, with the notable exception of Judaism, the drive to convert the whole world, if possible. Gibbon never forgets the man Mohammed. He was "a genius visionary" who began preaching his new monotheism in Mecca, was driven out by his enemies in the Quraish tribe, found ready adoptees among the Arab nomads, established the center of the religion in Medina, and promised the faithful the spoils of pillage in this world and a sensual paradise in the next. Here lay the key to victory over Mecca, then Persia, and finally the Second Rome at Constantinople. Gibbon insists the aboriginal teachings of Islam and code of conduct were never lost. Muslim warriors from Spain to India sacrificed earthly pleasure and, if necessary, their lives in wars of conquest for an empire of faith. While Christians East and West were torn by schisms, the Albigensians in Provencal, the Paulicians in North Africa, Muslims stayed in step with their religion. Omar, the second Caliph in succession to Mohammed, was the first and still the greatest military genius the Arab nation produced, Gibbon writes admiringly. He moved the Prophet's army into Syria, smashed the mighty Persian empire, converted its people to Islam, and by directive, oversaw the addition of North Africa to the faith, bringing him to war with the Byzantines. Yet the Muslim camp could not escape the fate of all empires, overreach, and the emergence of rival centers of power. The Sunni-Shia split took Persia out of the orbit of Mecca, the Umayyad dynasty arose in Damascus, the Abbasids in Baghdad, who Gibbon proclaims, ruled over more territory and people than any civilization since Rome, the Shia Fatimid throne in Egypt broke from both Persia and Arabia, and Muslim Spain sported its own kingdom at Cordoba. What amazes Gibbon, and us, is how this diverse world remained loyal to the Koran, Hadith (sayings and acts) of Mohammed, the Arabic language, and the duty of jihad, not "holy war", Christians and Jews were protected as "Peoples of the Book", but the duty to proselytize the earth from China to France, and beyond. Gibbon writes with unholy glee that Jews and even some Christians, such as the monophysites Egypt, welcomed the Muslim conquests of their lands. Better the House of Islam than the Byzantine zealots.While the Arabs and Persians exhausted themselves fighting for rival kingdoms, a new Muslim power arose in the East, one destined to destroy Byzantium. The ethnic origins of the Seljuk Turks were murky to Gibbon, and to us, but this nomadic people learned the art of cavalry warfare from their Scythian blood cousins and Mongol overlords, turning their attention to the Anatolian Peninsula, located at crossroads of the Eastern Empire, Persia and Arabia. By taking control of that jugular vein the Seljuks threatened all three. Their successors, the Ottoman Turks (Turkish peoples are named for their chief) adopted for a political model the court of the Sultan of Persia, with its intricate bureaucracy, and after reaching the Aegean Sea quickly mastered the art of naval warfare. But, their stupendous rise to top power in the Mediterranean world, Gibbon thinks, was due to the invention of the Janissarie Corps, Christian youth taken from the conquered provinces of Albania, Thrace, and forays into Byzantium, converted to Islam, and trained from childhood to do only one thing---fight for the Ottomans and die, if need be. By this elite the Ottomans subdued the Mamelukes of Egypt, Albanian slaves who had overthrown the monarchy and proclaimed their own dynasty, captured Jerusalem from the Byzantines, and then turned their wrath and desire on Constantinople. The Emperor of the East quaked in his purple robes, and called on his hated Catholic brethren of the West to save Christendom.Gibbon cannot find enough animadversions to express his contempt for the crusades and Crusaders who rushed to the banner of Christ when Pope Urban II proclaimed a holy war to liberate Jerusalem from the Turks in 1095. These "ignorant fanatics", "peasant priests", and assorted numbskulls obeyed the head Roman Druid in seven expeditions from France or Germany to the Holy Land, with most of them getting killed along the way by brigands, confidence men, the Turks and fellow Christians. Barbarossa, the Holy Roman Emperor, sank into a swamp. Bad enough these sunshine soldiers kept drowning, or dying of malaria and smallpox, they dragged along their entourage of women, children and servants, who perished by the thousands. Next to each band of Crusaders went the pilgrims to Jerusalem, the putative raison d'etre of the enterprise, who were supposed to pray "at the tombstone of their dead savior", the Church of the Holy Sepulchre, but God took them way prematurely. Gibbon does find two heroes in this wicked game. Geoffrey of Bouillon, leader of the First Crusade, was motivated by genuine pity for the pilgrims, and once he took Jerusalem, and was proclaimed the first Latin ruler of the Holy Land by the pope, he drew up the Assizes of Jerusalem, a code of laws establishing justice through the courts and the rights of commoners that the English later emulated. Saladin, the Kurdish general who overthrew the Fatimids and retook Jerusalem for Islam, is Gibbon's other noble man. This military genius followed faithfully the strictures of Islam on war and peace. He sought no booty, aspired not to the throne of any state and, unlike Geoffrey, protected the defeated, the Jews and Christians of Jerusalem, after victory. Richard the Lionhearted, Saladin's opponent in battle, Gibbon jokes, was better ruling in the Holy Land than back in England. He had a lion's heart alright, but no gift for politics or leadership.The crusades accomplished nothing, of course, and Gibbon makes a strong case that they weakened Europe, West and East. In the West whole towns were depopulated to provide recruits for these mad escapades, the town economy was disrupted, and the hand of the baronial class strengthened over the peasantry. In the Empire of the East they lit the long fuse that brought down Byzantium. The emperors foolishly thought the monarchies of Europe would come to their assistance against the Turks in any future conflict. Wrong. The French, English and German kings and princes let Byzantium go hang. An attempted reconciliation between the Orthodox and Catholic churches only enraged the civil population of Constantinople, who accused their sovereigns of heresy. The emperors assumed these expeditions had weakened the Ottomans. Wrong again. The Sultans learned the art of siege warfare, perfected the gunpowder imported from China, developed stronger cannons, coordinated naval war with infantry tactics, bought off traitors inside the court at the Constantinople, and patiently plotted the downfall of that city until the final siege in 1453 led by Mehmet II, something of a scoundrel, per Gibbon, but a warrior wizard. Yet, Gibbon is right to write that "Byzantium destroyed itself". The monarchy was absolutist and supreme over the church, stifling all dissent. A decadent court filled with intrigues and assassinations, endless debates over theology, one of the hottest was whether the Holy Ghost proceeded from only the Son or the Son and Father, complete disregard for the army, and failure to cultivate allies, all brought the East to its knees. At the end of this sad tale Constantinople and Rome, prostrate before the popes and Italian warring aristocratic families, lay in ruin.Gibbon does find signs of hope at the end. Dedicated scholars like Petrarch and Boccaccio revived Latin literature after centuries of neglect and paved the way for the Renaissance. The crusades and wars with the Arabs and Turks discredited the holy edifice of European civilization, since all rulers, including the pope, valued politics and riches above faith, and made way for the Protestant Reformation. Muslim rule protected the Jewish and Christian communities of the Mediterranean from Spain to Palestine. In style, Gibbon has not lost his comic tough and sardonic humor. He calls the religion of the first generation of the followers of Jesus "the Christianity of circumcision", and gleefully recounts how in one town where the Byzantines emasculated the men to make eunuchs for the court, the women ran into the streets shouting, "Give us back our most sacred possession!". His dry comments looking back from from 1787 are praiseworthy. He sheds tears for the Egyptians, "five hundred years under a foreign race and religion" and compares his Muslim and Byzantine warrior-kings favorably with Cromwell. Gibbon is not without his critics. Perry Anderson in PASSAGES FROM ANTIQUITY finds plenty of evidence for scientific advances and laws protecting private property in the West during the Dark Ages, both of which provided an on-ramp for capitalism. Edward Luttwark in THE GRAND STRATEGY OF THE BYZANTINE EMPIRE thinks Gibbon wrong in seeing no military science or winning tactics in the armies of the East. No empire would last that long simply by chance and superstition. A child of the Enlightenment, Gibbon has few kind words for the dedicated monks who preserved literacy and art works in their cloisters and maintained the West's only education system. Coleridge wrote that "Gibbon's writing style is atrocious, and that is not the worse thing about Gibbon". These are grave deficiencies, but pinpricks on the body of the great historian who prophesied THE DECLINE AND FALL would earn him immortality. It has.
