People don't read Thomas Carlyle anymore. But we should.Carlyle is a thinker you probably disagree with in fundamental ways. I certainly do. He was a reactionary monarchist who totally rejected the enlightenment liberalism that is the foundation for the United States. He was terrified and disgusted by the French Revolution. While he was greatly sympathetic to its causes (the suffering of the French peasantry; a generation of aristocracy almost unique in its debauched uselessness), he had no doubt that the revolution's outcome would be blood, chaos and eventually the death of civilization.HisFrench Revolutionis monumental. Six volumes of sustained brilliance on a level you have to experience to believe. Carlyle is able to produce remarkable insights and fascinating presentation consistently over many hundreds of pages.The French Revolutionis a different model of history writing. Carlyle does not offer the reader a nice little framing device to make fluid and complex events relatable. Nor does he, like a modern historian, limit himself to a slick package calculated to efficiently transmit "the facts." This is history as a rushing torrent of force. Like reading a painting in a highly stylized literary voice.Whatsoever is cruel in the panic frenzy of Twenty-five million men, whatsoever is great in the simultaneous death-defiance of Twenty-five million men, stand here in abrupt contrast, near by one another. As indeed is usual when a man, how much more when a Nation of men, is hurled suddenly beyond the limits.For Nature, as green as she looks, rests everywhere on dread foundations, were we farther down; and Pan, to whose music the Nymphs dance, has a cry in him that can drive all men distracted.See?Carlyle shifts tenses and tones event by event so that the reader experiences the revolution as it occurred, rather than in perfect hindsight. Events are presented as difficult, frightening and often confusing. I've heard it said that Carlyle inspired Joyce's experimental style. I think that's right.The French Revolutionis also refreshing in that it makes no pretense of objectivity. Carlyle candidly offers himself as political/social philosopher and uses the revolution as a framework to discuss larger ideas about society and human interaction in general.I've touched very little on the actual content because it is so vast and so interesting it feels beyond the scope of a review like this. Suffice it to say that this is not the book to read if you're looking for a succinct account of the French Revolution, or if you're starting from scratch. But if you want to explore the heights of human ability to assimilate and analyze reality, look no further.Finally, it's worth noting that Carlyle's deep cynicism about democracy and humanity is probably corrosive and even poisonous if you're in a certain mindset. Don't read this as a manual on how to hate modern society. If you find yourself agreeing with Carlyle too much, it's probably time to take a break. Read some Herman Melville and enjoy the light of the world.
