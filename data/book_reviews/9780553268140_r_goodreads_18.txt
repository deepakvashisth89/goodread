BRANDEN BROADENS HIS DISCUSSION OF THE SELF, SELF-ESTEEM, AND MORENathaniel Branden (1930-2014) was a Canadian psychotherapist and writer who was Ayn Rand's chief spokesman, until their break in 1968.He wrote in the Introduction to this 1983 book, “This book will be concerned… with the ultimate human encounter: the relationship of the ‘I’ to the ‘me,’ of the ego to the self. The issues entailed by this encounter … affect virtually every significant human experience---from the level of our self-esteem, to the kind of person we are likely to fall in love with, to the ambitions and life goals we are like to set ourselves, to our most intimately personal sense of what it means to be a human being.” (Pg. xii)He continues, “In a world in which selflessness is commonly regarded as a synonym of virtue and selfishness as a synonym of evil---and in which the presumed goal of spiritual evolution is self-transcendence---a book titled ‘Honoring the Self’ may sound strange, even a bit disconcerting. This volume is grounded in the recognition that self-esteem and personal autonomy are indispensable conditions of human well-being. Its focus is the exploration and illumination of the meaning of these states and of the conditions of their realization; that goal is the guiding principle of all the chapters that follow… I propose to demonstrate that not selfishness but absence of self is the root of most of our evils, that selflessness is our greatest personal, interpersonal, and social danger and has been so throughout most of our history. In short, I shall invite the reader to reexamine some of our most prevalent beliefs about ego, selfishness, goodness---and human survival.” (Pg. xii-xiii)He adds, “The first act of honoring the self is the assertion of consciousness: the choice to think, to be aware… to honor the self is to be willing to think independently, to live by our own mind, and to have the courage of our own perceptions and judgments. To honor the self is to be willing to know not only what we think but also what we feel… and to accept our right to experience such feelings. To honor the self is to preserve an attitude of self-acceptance---which means to accept what we are, without self-oppression or self-castigation… To honor the self is to live authentically, to speak and act from our innermost convictions and feelings. To honor the self is to refuse to accept unearned guilt… To honor the self is to be committed to our right to exist… To honor the self is to be in love with our own life… Thus we can begin to see that to honor the self is to practice SELFISHNESS in the highest, noblest, and least understood sense of the word. And this, I shall argue, requires enormous independence, courage, and integrity.” (Pg. xiii-xiv)He summarizes, “My goals in this work… are as follows: to demonstrate, specifically and concretely, what honoring the self means and to show the overwhelming importance of this issue to human life and well-being; to examine the kinds of behaviors by which self is honored or betrayed; to develop a deeper understanding of the meaning of self-esteem and its potency as a force in determining the course of our existence; to show on what the attainment of positive self-esteem depends; to explore the meanings of autonomy and individuation and the path to their realization; to point the way out of the widespread problem of self-alienation; to illuminate the morality of honoring the self and to integrate the psychology of self-esteem with an ethics of rational or enlightened self-interest; and, finally, to show that ego, properly understood, is not an obstacle to spiritual fulfillment but rather that without which no fulfillment is possible.” (Pg. xvii)He explains, “Self-esteem is a concept pertaining to a fundamental sense of efficacy and a fundamental sense of worth, to competence and worthiness in principle… High self-esteem can best be understood as the integrated sum of self-confidence and self-respect… In sum, self-esteem is an evaluation of my mind, my consciousness, and, in a profound sense, my person. It is not an evaluation of particular successes or failures… Thus, I can be very confident of myself at the fundamental level and yet … inwardly be self-doubting and insecure.” (Pg. 4) He asks rhetorically, “Is it possible to possess too high a level of self-esteem? Not if we understand that we are speaking of … a genuine, organic experience, and not some overinflated pretense at self-value aimed at concealing a deficiency. No one would ask, ‘It is possible to enjoy too high a level of physical health?” (Pg. 7)He states, “An analogy may prove helpful. When human beings developed the notion of a God who is omniscient and omnipotent, they quickly added the attribute of all-good. It would be too terrifying to imagine a capricious or sadistic God. Therefore, if disaster befalls, the fault must be ours… So if I suffer, let me believe I have committed some offense, even though I don’t understand that it is… only if I can discover the actions that will please my parents, THEN will I receive the kindness and nurturing I crave.” (Pg. 37-38)He observes, “To speak of ‘thinking independently’ is useful… in terms of emphasis. Often what people call ‘thinking’ is merely recycling the opinions of others. So we can say that thinking independently---about our work, our relationships, the values that will guide our life, the goals we will set for ourselves---enhances self-esteem. And Healthy self-esteem results in a natural inclination to think independently.” (Pg. 49)He states, “Sometimes … unearned guilt follows a divorce or trouble with one’s children… This is why the protection of self-esteem requires a clear understanding of the limits of volitional responsibility… The concept of Original Sin---of guilt where there is no possibility of innocence, no freedom of choice, no alternatives available---is anti-self-esteem by its very nature. It is, therefore, antihuman.” (Pg. 61-62)He asserts, “At birth, the self does not exist. What exists is… the raw material from which a self can develop… To evolve into selfhood is the primary human task… To a tragic extent, most people are stranded along the path of development. Nonetheless, the central goal of the maturational process is EVOLUTION TOWARD AUTONOMY. This is the essence of the separation/individuation process.” (Pg. 109)He suggests, “We can better appreciate [that] the relationship of death anxiety to the challenges of individuation … is the ultimate defense against death: the refusal to live. If I do not come into existence, I cannot go out of existence. If I do not emerge as a separate entity, there is no one for death to touch.” (Pg. 194) He adds, “It is no greater comfort to be told: ‘Your consciousness… merely becomes reintegrated into the great cosmic pool of consciousness from which it came.’… if my consciousness becomes reintegrated into the great cosmic pool of consciousness… it [would] no longer be MY consciousness.” (Pg. 196)He reports, “To rethink the whole issue of egoism versus altruism, of selfishness versus selflessness, we need… [to begin] with the question of why we NEED a code of values… I do not know of any philosopher who has addressed the issue with the skill… and profundity of Ayn Rand. While I differ with her on a number of philosophical points… I think the foundation of her ethics is an unassailable contribution. Indeed, there is no way I can discuss my own views of egoism, selfishness, and ethics in general without reference to Rand’s work.” (Pg. 205)He argues, “Traditional religionists have declared that if God did not exist, morality would be unnecessary---everything would be ‘permissible.’ Secular moralists have declared that if Society did not exist, morality would be unnecessary… Life as the standard has been the underlying premise of every word I have written in this book. What we are concerned with here is the justification of that standard… Contrary to the prevalent belief that morality is needed to curb the natural human inclination toward self-interest, it is self-interest that GENERATES the need for a moral code.” (Pg. 209-210)In his chapter on ‘Self-Sacrifice,’ he states, “Let us begin with the symbol of the cross… Let us accept Christian terms, for the moment, and look at the symbol in its own context. On the cross, the ideal man was sacrificed to human depravity… The morally superior chose to be immolated for the sake of the morally inferior. The notion that such a sacrifice is in any sense right, that ignoble men and women… [are] living at the price of the perfect man’s torture, living on the blood of the ideal---is, I submit, as monstrous an injustice, as profound a perversion of morality as the human mind can conceive… And yet this is the symbol hanging, as moral inspiration, over our lives in the Western world.” (Pg. 220-221)He asserts, “The only proper and justifiable purpose of government is to protect … us from physical violence. It is the fact that our rights CAN be violated by others that necessitates the institution of government… the sole function of a government is to protect us from criminals, to protect us from foreign invaders, to provide a system of courts for the protection of property and contracts against breach or fraud---and otherwise to LEAVE US ALONE.” (Pg. 229)He is quite critical of Transpersonal Psychology (notwithstanding that in the ‘Acknowledgments’ he thanked Ken Wilber “for our illuminating discussions concerning the perspective of transpersonal psychology”). “I believe that an anti-individualist, antiself ideology is so deeply ingrained in our thinking, the notion of virtue so mired in association with selflessness, that many writers in this field see a seductive glamour in notions of self-transcendence and emancipation from the ego that ‘transcend’ logic.” (Pg. 241-242)This book expands Branden’s notion of egoism and the ‘self’ from his earlier books.Show more
