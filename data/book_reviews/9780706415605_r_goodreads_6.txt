“A vast silence reigned over the land. The land itself was a desolation, lifeless, without movement, so lone and cold that the spirit of it was not even that of sadness.”Jack London’s stories never fail to set me right in the middle of his settings. The chill seeps into my bones and the emptiness itself sends a shiver down my spine. To live in or traipse through a land so frigid and empty would plummet my spirits indeed. Yet, it also gives me a sense of awe to read about such things. I’m grateful to be writing this in a small patch of sunlight by the window while the birds are singing their springtime songs.I’ve readCall of the Wildtwice now, once as a child and once as an adult. It’s a favorite. Yet, for some reason, I’d never gotten around to this one. It follows White Fang, part wolf and part dog, through puppyhood into adulthood. This is not an easy one to read, particularly if you are an animal lover. There is violence done to animals by men and vice versa. There is violence amongst the animals themselves. It’s true to the spirit of men and dogs and wolves and other creatures of the wild, however.“The law was: EAT OR BE EATEN. He did not formulate the law in clear, set terms and moralise about it. He did not even think the law; he merely lived the law without thinking about it at all.”White Fang, like any other wild animal, is wired to survive. Along the way, he learns a thing or two about the power of man. He’s a loner, not one to seek out affection – until he learns there is such a thing that can be given to him by kind people. How sad that he had to wait so long to experience this.“White Fang experienced an unaccountable sensation of pleasure as the hand rubbed back and forth.”I really enjoyed this, even if not quite as much asCall of the Wild. Jack London is dependable. I can always count on excellent storytelling and vivid descriptions. I think the next time I reach for him, however, I’ll held a little further south. His Hawaii stories are beckoning.
