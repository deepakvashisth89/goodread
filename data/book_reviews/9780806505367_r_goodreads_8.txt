Spinoza dares—even in the deeply religious 17th century—to offer his own definition of God. His God is Natura naturans—the self-causing action of nature; nature in the active sense, rather than the passive backdrop found in most representations. As a result of these views, this 24-year-old descendant of Portuguese Sephardic Jews (who had escaped the Inquisition), fluent in seven languages plus Latin and trained to be a rabbi, was excommunicated from the Amsterdam Jewish community. A few years later, he was declared dangerous to his fellow believers. Spinoza spent the rest of his life as a secluded lens grinder, slowly grinding his mind into the heights of philosophy.Spinoza’s attempt to reconcile and combine religion, theology, scholasticism, logic, and mathematics is astonishing in its polyphony. Personally—from my comfortable 21st-century secular vantage point—I don’t believe he succeeded: there are too many contradictions and convenient adjustments in his orderly exposition. And plenty of mystery.Spinoza is not only erudite but deeply structured: every chapter of this work follows an algorithm: "Topic – Definitions – Axioms – Propositions – Proofs – Corollaries."Regarding the question of God, two points stand out amidst his complex conceptual apparatus:✔️ There is no proof that God does not exist, therefore He exists (this is a simplification, but his arguments can be flipped in the exact opposite direction).✔️ There is no harm in God existing; in fact, it brings a host of benefits—meaning it’s better for Him to exist than not. God is necessary. (A superb argument, with which I completely disagree, but it is brilliantly formulated!). Let’s not forget, however, that the rabbis had good reason to excommunicate the young, free-thinking philosopher…Overall, the detailed terminology introduced by Spinoza is quite painful to internalize because, after so many centuries, the concepts he defines have acquired different meanings, far removed from his original intent. But his goal was not to be accessible; it was to be exhaustive and precise—by his own criteria.According to Spinoza—the rationalist, the determinist, the stoic—free will and chance do not exist. People, Spinoza says, only think they act out of free will, but in reality, they do not know the true causes of their actions and are blindly and chaotically influenced by so-called "affects." Here, some of his formulations are precursors to modern psychology. Interestingly, according to Spinoza, positive emotions can somewhat compensate for a lack of exercised dispassionate reason; thus, religions purposefully preach love for one's neighbor and fear of God. The goal is precisely to produce acceptable behavior in the absence of sufficient reason.In the section on affects, the understandable formulations increase. In addition to the propositions with proofs and corollaries, Spinoza prepared a full list of 48 definitions (quite different in places from the commonly accepted ones), complete with their Latin names!After exhausting the affects, Spinoza reaches the climax—a praise of Reason as the sole guide to the good, as the tamer and commander of chaotic and distracting affects. One of his main arguments for why a person should be guided entirely by reason (and thus cannot err) rather than by affect, is that affects/emotions are an undergoing of an influence (passion), while the mind is the exerting of an influence (action).Ultimately, amidst all the complex draping, Spinoza preaches a harmonious human cohabitation of rational beings, in harmony with Nature/God—free from fear and hatred. He is a fierce opponent of the religious cult of suffering and self-denial as sources of bliss and holiness, mercilessly mocking them as harmful and vicious superstitions. Like the ancient Stoics, he preaches moderation without extremes, and a disciplined, unceasing pursuit of understanding and knowledge.Spinoza still has much to say today.
