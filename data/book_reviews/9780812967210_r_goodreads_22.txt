Quick Recommendation

Christian theology and mythology mixed together as one. Dante Alighieri's Inferno is both a subjective interpretation of Christian theology and a re-telling of mythology. If one is interested to dive deeper in either of these without any strong accuracy to the actual source materials or wants to read a morally dark tragedy this might be the right choice.

Dante Alighieri shows his own viewpoints and offers commentary to politics in Inferno. While it's mainly dark it offers a hopeful glimpse at the end of the story.



Dante Alighieri makes Inferno sound both frightfully dreadful and yet beautifully charming. From modern re-tellings and own interpretations of the Inferno I noticed quickly that I feel drawn to it. Mostly for the symbolic punishments and the psychology behind each crime. I wanted to read this for a while so I was naturally excited to finally dive into it.








The writing style is really beautiful. For one part it's beautiful for it's unique rhyme sheme (aba, bcb, cdc, ded, efe). Unfortunately most English speaking people aren't able to experience this unless they read Mandelbaum's translation.


Another reason for the writing style to be beautiful is Dante Alighieri's imagination of what Inferno might look like. He included lots of similes and symbols. Also metaphors were used to represent the punishments. My personal favourite part of his writing style is how he shaped the internal conflicts of Dante.







The Italian text offers both the rhyme sheme and it's original wording. One can understand Dante Alighieri the best not just by his viewpoints but also lyrical language the best by reading the original.




Darkness, human corruption, moral/spiritual errors and horror, divine justice and the nature of sin and evil.


Redemption, salvation and political corruption.



This time I won't talk about different characters. I want to keep it more general 'cause even though each of the souls has their own story to tell they share the same fate in Inferno.


What revealed character was their sins and each story to it. It was hard to forgive and sometimes hard not to show hatred. Some stories were more sad, some more dark and twisted and some others full of passion and love.








The stories that shocked me the most were the ones of treachery (especially the benefactor one with Babel and the like). The saddest story for me was the one being found in the seventh circle; violence against self. I pitied the avarice and prodigality the most. Showed hatred towards the ones that showed violence against others. Felt injustice by the unbaptised and violence against God ones. Was disgusted by the malebolge and felt familiarity with the wrath and heresy sinners.


Each punishment exposed inner turmoil. The symbolism and metaphor of each of them also revealed psychology behind each punishment. The reactions of the sinners to their own punishments revealed their thoughts, emotions and desires.











I liked how differently each sinner argued or sometimes even accepted their fate (like the lustful ones). They tried to justify themselves or deceive (mostly in the malebolge). Some even showed rage (wrath sinners), sadness or forgiveness (violence against self).



Originally I wanted to read Purgatorio and Paradio too but I don't like how it starts from darkness and ends in the light. It's obviously a Comedy 'cause of this structure but not something that I would enjoy reading. Inferno is more interesting to me than Paradiso 'cause of the struggles, pains and sorrows. Inferno doesn't give closure or resolves contractions and most importantly it doesn't excuse anything. It's much more honest and human than Paradiso could ever be.

I reduced 1 star of my final rating 'cause I didn't always understood what Dante Alighieri meant and the circles sometimes didn't make sense for me. I would have switched the eight and the seventh circle 'cause I personally think that violence is worse than malebolge. That's also why I didn't like the Bolgias that much while reading. It's all very subjective 'cause one doesn't know for certain how Inferno looks like, so I don't think that Dante Alighieri was ‘wrong’ in his viewpoints. I just have a different viewpoint than him and that's totally fine.
