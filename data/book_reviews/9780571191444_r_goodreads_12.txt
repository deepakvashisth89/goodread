’…he did mutter it was thought among the common people that a parson in a ship was like a woman in a fishing boat - a kind of natural bringer of bad luck’.A somewhat tricky book to read,Rites of Passageis set in the early nineteenth century, and filled with seafaring dialogue and ye olde worlde ways, so it took me some considerable effort to get through it.I had to read many paragraphs aloud in order to understand what was actually being said. At times it was hard to comprehend, but I persevered, because within the complex language and structure of the story was a fascinating tale of maritime woe and wit, which I was determined to finish, and I’m delighted that I did.I feel it was quite an accomplishment to get through this Booker Prize winner. I can understand why it won this award, because one word - characters - all of them were fantastic! So individual, so alive,(obviously not the dead one)so disdainful, and at times, extremely funny.The descriptions of being out at sea on the most grimiest of warships were superb. I could almost smell the stench of bodily fluids and filthy humans oozing from the pages!This is the first book of three from Golding’s ‘sea trilogy’, and I’ll definitely be continuing the series. But for now, I’m glad I’m off this boat, because! - my sealegs! - Good God, man! - are starting to fail me and one must go for a dram of rum to save oneself frommal de mer.Overall I thoroughly enjoyed my second Golding (the first being Lord of the Flies) and would recommend to historical fiction fans who don’t mind doing a bit of brainwork to get through it.4 ⭐ - Very good. I liked it a lot.
