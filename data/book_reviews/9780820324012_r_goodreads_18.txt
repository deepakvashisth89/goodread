A masterpiece of American satire, The Devil’s Dictionary is a monument to Ambrose Bierce’s cynical wit. Bierce began printing these devastating definitions in his newspaper column in 1867. His satirical dictionary was produced piecemeal in this fashion over the period of three decades, often quoted, imitated, and plagiarized before finally compiled in its complete form in 1911.

The book is more satisfying in its parts than in its whole, as certain definitions are necessarily more powerful than others. As a rule, the pithier definitions are superior to the wordier ones. Bierce spared nothing — neither religion, philosophy, country, family, nor self — all are assaulted by his vivisecting wit.

The edition that I read has a biographical introduction on Bierce the man, written by Angus Calder, which gives some insight into the mind and temperament of the man behind the dictionary. It is also illustrated by Ralph Steadman, famous for his collaborations with Hunter S. Thompson. In theory, the pairing of Bierce caustic wit with Steadman’s fiendishly chaotic illustrations should have been perfectly fitted, but I actually found the illustrations a distraction rather than a complement to the work, and was disappointed.
