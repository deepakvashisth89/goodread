As I mentioned at the end of my review of “Mysterious Tales of Old St. Paul,” I enjoyed rediscovering Shadwell Rafferty so much that I decided to go back and reread the other works by Larry Millett that feature the likeable St. Paul detective and his sidekick Thomas. In some of those works, Holmes and Watson are featured as well – making them true pastiches of the Arthur Conan Doyle canon.

Obviously, I'm a huge fan of Sherlock Holmes - both the original canon and the numerous pastiches that have been written by other authors over the years. Some of the pastiches I read are so good they easily could pass as one of Conan Doyle's creations. Others, however, just don't capture the voice, feel, plot, setting, etc. of the original canon works.

Typically, Millett does a terrific job of capturing ACD's voice. Unlike some of the pastiches I've reviewed over the years, his works typically sound like they could be part of the original canon in terms of the dialogue between Holmes and Watson.

Unfortunately, this one falls short in that area. It's really a Shadwell Rafferty mystery - not a pure Holmes pastiche. Yes, Watson and Holmes are in it. But they are not the true protagonists. And the book suffered because of it.

Also, the plot was my least favorite of Millett's novels. As you know, I don't go deeply into plots in my reviews to avoid spoilers. But here's the jacket description:

"The numerous followers of Larry Millett's extraordinary Sherlockian series will be thrilled to find Holmes's American consort, Irish saloonkeeper Shadwell Rafferty, taking his first crack at a case that looks like it might send a whole city government tumbling. Rafferty, large hearted and massively built, has a talent for logic, dissimulation, bribery, and action that is surpassed only by his love of 'the detectin' game.' On the eve of President McKinley's visit in 1899 to Minneapolis, a gruesome crime draws Rafferty from St. Paul to the booming city across the river.

A young union activist has been found, strung naked from a tree outside a ruined mansion. A placard around his neck bears the ominous words the alliance has spoken. Rafferty plunges into the case with his usual gusto, but finds more questions than answers. Was the murder the work of the union-busting Citizens Alliance or wild-eyed anarchists? Was the victim linked to an empire of corruption controlled by the city's slippery mayor? And how did the young man come to posses a photograph of a prominent citizen engaged in sexual congress? As the puzzle deepens and the dangers mount, Rafferty is joined by his old friends Sherlock Holmes and Dr. John Watson."

While the above plot had potential, it was incredibly complex at times. This is probably the reason it dragged at times. Also, the constant switching back and forth between Rafferty's account of the case and Watson's typical narration of Sherlock's approach made the narrative choppy and disjointed.

Ultimately, I feel that three stars is an accurate rating. I didn't hate it - but I didn't love it, either.
