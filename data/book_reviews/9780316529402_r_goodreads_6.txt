Wenn Deutschland bluht, bluht Krupp
(When Germany blooms, Krupp blooms)

Few families in history are as closely linked to their country's ups and downs as the Krupp family is to Germany. For me, the name Krupp brings to mind images of factories, weapons, guns and tanks that supported both Wilhelm II's ambitions and Hitler's war efforts. And the city of Essen, a heart of the Ruhr which I visited a few times and where the Krupp family became part of their identity.

In , historian William Manchester tells the story of the Krupp family over 400 years, from their humble beginnings in 1587 to their dramatic end in the mid-20th century. It starts with Arndt Krupp, a simple trader who made money by buying abandoned properties in Essen. Over the centuries, his descendants turned the Krupp name into a symbol of industrial power, with their steel factories and weapons shaping Germany and its role in world history. The story ends with Arndt Krupp (von Bohlen und Halbach), the last family heir, who gave up his inheritance in 1966, marking the end of the dynasty.

This book is nearly a thousand pages long, or, in my case, a 48-hour audiobook. Reading such a long story was not easy—it took me more than a month to finish. The Krupps and their story were with me on many walks and daily trips to work. What struck me most was how closely the family's fate was tied to Germany’s history. Through their successes and failures, the book gave a clear picture of Germany’s industrial revolution, its imperial ambitions, and the terrible effects of its two world wars.



One of the best things about the book is how Manchester keeps the reader interested in such a long and complex story. Just when the details of industrial processes or historical facts started to get too much, Manchester adds a new story or unexpected twist that made me curious again. His way of telling the story made the dense history easy to follow and even exciting.

However, as the story went on, my admiration for the Krupp family’s early business spirit turned into discomfort, especially with Alfried Krupp, the last heir of the family. Alfried was a key player in the Nazi war effort, using slave labor from concentration camps to run his factories. The personal stories of those who where forced to work in Essen’s factories are disturbing.

After the war, he was found guilty of crimes against humanity and sentenced to twelve years in prison, but his sentence was controversially reduced to only three years. This sharp difference between the early family’s hard work and Alfried’s involvement in terrible acts shows the moral decay that often comes with unchecked power and how a industry magnate loses his grip with everyday reality.



What makes The Arms of Krupp more than just a family biography is its wider historical meaning. The book shows how the Krupp family mirrored Germany's journey—from its early fragmented state to its industrial power, through its devastating wars and eventual post-war recovery. It also highlights the tricky balance between industrial progress and moral responsibility. Humanity’s ability to innovate is wonderful, but the risk is that you loose sight of ethical boundaries.
