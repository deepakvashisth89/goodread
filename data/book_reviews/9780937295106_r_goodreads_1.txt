'I'm not really worried,' she said softly. But she was dizzy, the way she'd felt on the ship just before she was seasick.

Kirsten, along with her family, voyaged over the ocean, took the train across America, and walked the final distance to Uncle Olav's farm...but now she has her greatest challenge yet: School.

"Kirsten sighed. 'But I can't do what you do. I only speak a little bit of English, and I can't read a word of it. I won't fit in at all.' "

But when she does go to school, it's so much worse than she imagined. She can't understand half of what the teacher is saying and her words come out all jumbled.





It's humiliating. But at least she has her cousins to keep her company and her secret friend, Singing Bird.

Singing Bird and Kirsten bumped into each by chance and while neither of them could speak English, they found a way to communicate.



But soon even Kirsten's secret friend isn't enough to keep her worries at bay. The schoolmistress, Miss Winston, is coming to stay at the farm, Kirsten must memorize a poem and recite it in front of the class, and one of her few friends might be leaving.

How could Kirsten ever feel at home in this new country when everything is going wrong?

This one was a really solid follow up to Meet Kirsten - I feel like we were able to see a lot of character development for Kirsten as she is faced with challenge after challenge.

My heart ached for her when she spoke of having to memorize the poem and then the anxiety she felt over speaking English in front of the other school children.

I think Singing Bird's presence in this book worked fairly well. As with any early pioneering book, the author must strike a balance between portraying the thoughts and feelings of the pioneers and our modern understanding of culture. Pioneers didn't have our same resources, and thus relied upon word-of-mouth for forming many of their opinions.

That can be seen in Kirsten's cousins, her teacher and even her Mama's lack of understanding regarding their native neighbors. It is unfortunate, but realistic.

I appreciated how Kirsten relied on her own experience with Singing Bird to form an opinion and how this book didn't shy away from the negative effects of the settlers on Singing Bird's family.

One thing that did confuse me about this book was the lack of grief over Marta. She passed in a rather traumatic way in book 1. While Kirsten grieved in that first book, I would expect that sadness to carry more over into this book but Marta's death isn't really mentioned in a significant way this book or subsequent ones.

Overall, I feel like this was a satisfying continuation of Kirsten's story and I cannot wait for the next one.



Like all of the main and mini Kirsten Larson books, this one has a mini historical section at the back called a Peek into the Past.

In this Peek into the Past, we learn about school in the pioneering days. Farm children worked in the spring and fall, so school was strictly in the summer and winter - but even then, students would frequently skip school to help their families. All the students had a singular teacher who used different books based on how advanced the children were.
