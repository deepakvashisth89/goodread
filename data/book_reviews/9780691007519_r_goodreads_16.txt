George Lefebvre's 1947 breakdown of the events and forces leading up to the French Revolution of 1789 is an excellent work of historical analysis. This book does not outline all the details of the individuals who were involved, or the complex events happening across France, but rather seeks to explain how those events unfolded. A general familiarity with the events of the French Revolution, and perhaps also of pre-Revolutionary France, would be of help to readers of this book.Overall, Lefebvre argues that the events of 1789 can best be understood as a series of "Acts," where different social classes in turn drove political events: the aristocracy, the bourgeoisie, the urban lower class, and finally the rural lower class. Each component of French society had long had a place in the traditional hierarchy, and the primary conflicts, according to Lefebvre, fall along these class lines. The privileged classes (aristocracy and clergy) were desperate to maintain their special rights and privileges in property and the management of the country. The lower classes strained under unequal taxes and perennial food shortages. The bourgeoisie (along with a few aristocratic allies and lower ranking clergy) sought reform and a constitutional monarchy. Louis XVI appears as a rather passive figure, determined not to surrender his power, but almost congenitally incapable of reading the room and realizing that some political reforms were essential. Lefebvre spends quite a lot of time on theDeclaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen, which he regards as revolutionary in its principles and central to understanding the debates of July through September 1789. He also skillfully explores how events large and small, rumors and panics, plots and mistakes, contributed to the Revolution's rapid escalation from a question of taxes, to a dismantling of feudalism; finally, low-level riots and would quickly burn out of control, ultimately leading to Louis becoming a prisoner of the National Assembly and the reactionaries plotting civil war. The stage for The Terror had been set.
