Ethical behaviour, for Baruch Spinoza, was a serious matter – and one to be approached with virtually mathematical exactitude. His dedication, and the rationalist precision with which he approached matters of both logic and ethics, can be seen on every page of his treatiseEthics(1675).Spinoza embodied the Enlightenment’s emphasis on the unfettered search for reason - in his life, as well as in his philosophical inquiry. His refusal to put religious faith above his search for the truth led to his formal expulsion from the Amsterdam Jewish community in which he had been raised. Unbowed by this rejection, Spinoza continued with his philosophical quest. TheEthics, published after Spinoza’s death, may be the ultimate expression of his philosophy.Spinoza’sEthics, notwithstanding his expulsion from a formal religious community, is strongly God-centered. He states categorically that “God, or substance, consisting of infinite attributes, of which each expresses eternal and infinite essentiality, necessarily exists” (p. 6). In the process, he offers his own version of an ontological proof of the existence of God, asserting that “If…no cause or reason can be given that prevents the existence of God, or which destroys His existence, then we must certainly conclude that He necessarily does exist” (p. 6).To those who would say that there cannot be, in the universe, anything higher than human beings, Spinoza responds quite sternly: “If, then, that which necessarily exists is nothing but finite beings, such finite beings are more powerful than a being absolutely infinite, which is obviously absurd; therefore, either nothing exists, or else a being absolutely infinite necessarily exists also” (p. 7). Spinoza concludes this part of the discussion by claiming that “Whatsoever is, is in God, and without God nothing can be, or can be conceived” (p. 9).Early in theEthics, Spinoza suggests that many people are fundamentally confused about the nature and power of God, as when he states that “the power that is commonly attributed to God is not only human…but involves a negation of power”, as “God is conceived by the multitude as a man, or in the likeness of a man”. This distinction is important to Spinoza, who insists to the reader that “No one will be able to follow my meaning, unless he is scrupulously careful not to confound the power of God with the human power and right of kings” (p. 26). There is, after all, the problem of “those who confuse the two natures, divine and human, [and] readily attribute human passions to the deity, especially so long as they do not know how passions originate in the mind” (p. 5).In other words, Spinoza wants his readers to put from his minds any images of God as an old, white-bearded man sitting on a cloud somewhere, ready to toss lightning bolts at the impious. God should not be expected to respond to human actions with emotions like anger or approval. God is pure mind, pure reason. Spinoza has neither time nor patience for any other concept of deity.Part III of theEthics, “On the Origin and Nature of the Emotions,” was particularly interesting to me. Spinoza sees the emotions as irrational and anti-reasonable, both literally and figuratively. Yet, like the Stoics of classical times, Spinoza feels that if one can understand the emotions better, through the use of reason, one can better control how one responds to emotional impulses.Those who deride the emotions, Spinoza says, “will doubtless think it strange that I should attempt to treat of human vice and folly geometrically”, but Spinoza insists that “I shall consider human actions and desires in exactly the same manner, as though I were concerned with lines, planes, and solids” (pp. 50-51). And indeed, things get quite mathematical from here.What follows is a careful, precise set of definitions for all sorts of emotions, many of which are paired. Spinoza explains at one point that “Hope is an inconstant pleasure, arising from the idea of something past or future, whereof we to a certain extent doubt the issue.” Similarly, he states that “Fear is an inconstant pain, arising from the idea of something past or future, whereof we to a certain extent doubt the issue.”Having set forth how hope and fear are quite similar – distinguished only by the difference that one involves a possible pleasure, while the other involves a possible pain – Spinoza concludes this part of his discussion by stating that “From these definitions it follows that there is no hope unmingled with fear, and no fear unmingled with hope” (p. 79). As all of us have felt both hope and fear countless times, I think most readers would agree with Spinoza’s remarks concerning these two emotions.All of this time, Spinoza is encouraging his readers to understand the emotions in order not to be led by them. Learning how to do so can lead men to behave honourably, and Spinoza hastens to provide another definition, writing that “by honourable I mean that which is praised by men living according to reason” (p. 100). Living according to reason brings a human being closer to God, to pure reason: “He who clearly and distinctly understands himself and his emotions loves God, and so much the more in proportion as he better understands himself and his emotions” (p. 125).Before taking up Spinoza’sEthics, I had a hunch that it would, in one way or another, reflect theNicomachean Ethicsof Aristotle. And my hunch, in a number of ways, was verified; I thought I heard, in Spinoza’sEthics, echoes of Aristotle’s ideas about virtue as a mean between two extremes of vice. But I wasn’t expecting Euclid of Alexandria, the great geometer and mathematician of the classical world, to be such an important influence here. The typical way in which Spinoza sets forth his ideas in theEthicsis by presenting a proposition and then furnishing a proof for that proposition, down to and including the Q.E.D. (Latin,Quod erat demonstrandum, meaning “that which was to be demonstrated”) that one always sees at the end of a freshly solved Euclidean math problem.But we’re not done with the math (or maths) yet. Along with propositions and proofs, Spinoza provides definitions, postulates, axioms, corollaries, explanations, notes, appendices, and even a couple of lemmas (a lemma is an intermediate theorem that supports a proof).It can all make for rather heavy going. Nonetheless, I found Spinoza’sEthicsinspiring – both as a guide to ethical behaviour, and as the last great philosophical testament of a fearless thinker who would not be turned from his search for the truth, no matter what the cost might have been to him personally.
