Man’s inhumanity to man

There are many books about the Nazi concentration camps. In this one we follow the lives of about a dozen inmates day after day for several months. All manner of ways they employ to stay alive we are privy to. How they struggle to maintain their humanity and their dignity. And this then becomes the reader’s challenge - will they dwell solely on the events that showcase man’s inhumanity to man or will they look to the ways some inmates unselfishly sought to help and encourage those who suffered alongside them? Can a heartfelt humanity prove itself ascendant even in the worst and most debasing hells created by human hands?

Remarque is best known for All Quiet on the Western Front, a novel of WW1 burned by the Nazis because it was too honest in its portrayal of the cruelty and horrors of war. This volume, A Spark of Life, is likely one of his least known. Remarque himself fled Germany or he would have been incarcerated in one of the death camps as a political dissident and suffered as his characters in Spark of Life suffered.

Powerfully and painfully written, the tragedy herein did not disappear in 1945. Such travesties remain with us today. And some there are who will tell you, “Oh but it never happened. Dachau, Auschwitz, Treblinka, they never happened. It’s all a gross exaggeration.”

Remarque knew better. That’s why he added his novel to the other cries for mercy and justice that rose from the dead and tortured of the Nazi camps, and from the survivors too, as a testament to the reality of the camps and their horrors. The fight to declare the validity of that testament continues today.
