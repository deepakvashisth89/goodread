My third reading of this book - with the first one when I was 19-20. This book changed me a lot. First, it convinced me that reading secondary literature about any significant thinker (instead of just making the effort to confront the original) is a waste of time and even worse. Second, it showed me for the first time how a fundamental thinker builds radically and vertically, and in opposition to a flat way like all other philosophers. Far more interesting than the content here is to witness how Kant was able to build something coherent out of thin air that sustains and makes everything else intelligible. In this respect, the things that still stand up to this day from this book are Kant’s struggles and hesitations, along with all those areas and concepts that he acknowledged as still partially opaque to him: the fundamental role of imagination, the unknown but necessarily common ground of both intuition and reason, the schematism, the origins of ideas and their prescriptive character for reason, the abyssal and fundamental aspect of time, and so on. It feels like this entire project was driven by Kant’s struggle with God - the conviction of the impossibility to prove God’s existence due to the limits of reason vs. Kant’s strong belief in the existence and necessity of God. It was also very interesting for me to read this at the same time as Nietzsche; as Nietzsche turned more and more aggressively against Kant and saw him as one of his main adversaries that needed to be taken down.


2020 original review:
Kant's insights are brilliant, the exposition of his transcendental idealism is extremely elaborate and systematic, but re-reading this book was quite a boring experience.
Working within the framework of subject-object (i.e. the two types of substances a la Descartes), Kant declares that the objects are things-in-themselves (i.e. noumena) and unknown as such. The experience is reduced to phenomena that we humans perceive. All that is important resides inside the human mind and is represented by the a prior structures of intuition (space and time), understanding, and reason. The a prior structures, as necessary and universal, are responsible for any possible, valid, and objective knowledge. The experience is the content of knowledge, while the a prior structures of the mind are the forms of knowledge; consequently cannot have any valid knowledge without one of the two.
Knowledge is analytic or synthetic. The first is obtained by analyzing a concept and is not very useful. The second is obtained by attaching a new predicate to a subject in a proposition. The predicate, given by experience, is attached as necessarily to the subject within the a prior structures of our mind. This synthetic a prior knowledge is universal, objective, new, and the only important and relevant knowledge.
Space and time are forms of pure a prior intuitions. They both exist only in our minds and make any experience possible. Everything exterior is given in space and everything interior is given in time. We cannot perceive something as out of space or out of time; that is any phenomena is possible only because of our innate space and time intuitions and consequently appear to us necessarily in space and time.
Mathematics is a special type of knowledge because its object of study - space and time - are pure a prior intuitions. Consequently we can do mathematics without reference to any concrete and external experience. However, we need to be wary in expecting the same success and path from other sciences – including philosophy/metaphysics.
There are a prior principles of reason in our mind; and unless we investigate them, acknowledge their tendencies, and limit their proper sphere of action – they can run wild and discredit the legit uses of reason and of any speculative knowledge. Most important, we need to stay within the bounds of experience. When we step outside the bounds imposed by our experience, we fall within the antinomies of pure reason (for example, that the space and time are either finite or infinite) and we are stuck there without any valid resolution. The questions and proofs concerning the existence of God, soul, free will, causality and so on are also outside these bounds of reason, not legit questions, and without any definitive answers in affirmative or negative. However, ideas like God and soul provide us with an a prior structure of pure reason – that of the unity of our representations and because of this are extremely useful. As such, speculative theology is nothing else but pure reason running wild. On the other hand, there is the practical reason and practical theology, and there the existence of God, soul, free will, causality, and so on are necessary and as such they all exist.
Kant's transcendental idealism, by collapsing most of what is important inside the human mind, was quite a revolution at that time and probably still is today. It allowed him to successfully bridge the gap between empiricists and rationalists. It explained the success of mathematics and postulated the a prior systemic judgments as the only valid and objective knowledge (due to their necessity and universality). He delimited the sphere of valid application of reason and denounced any application beyond these limits.
On the other hand, Kant firmly stayed within the framework initiated by Descartes of subjects and objects, did not question and consequently took for granted the existence of substances (res extensa and res cogitans). He postulated the existence of things-in-themselves even if we cannot say anything about them (except as phenomena of our experiences). As such, everything in knowledge is about the representations in our minds. His theory is unhistorical - as if it applies to all cultures and all times. He demoted God from existence in the sphere of pure reason, re-introduced God as a necessary and important principle in unifying our representations, and then postulated God's existence in the sphere of practical reason.
The categorical imperative looms all over the book. He continuously asks for moderation and restraint. The extensive exposition of the transcendental idealism is extremely elaborate and impersonal. He is quite a conservative, and as such, reassures any respectable bourgeois that things are as they should be and make sure they are arranged in a neat order. There is no smile or joy in reading this book.
