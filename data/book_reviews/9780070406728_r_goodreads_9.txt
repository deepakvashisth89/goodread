This book, as the title implies, is a selection of Marx's sociological and social philosophical writings. Typically in anthologies of this type, one would expect that the selected writings are full writings. That's not the case here, Marx never wrote a singular text on sociology or social philosophy. This book, instead, takes segments of various texts when they relate to a particular theme. Bottomore and Rubel do an excellent job explaining the context of these writings and of sociology in general in the preface.The book is separated into 5 parts: Methodological foundations, pre-capitalist societies, sociology of capitalism, sociology of politics, and future society.I am a fan of Marx's prose. They always have a nice flow even when examining less than glourious topics. Due to the nature of the book, selected paragraphs, the greater context for the points made or further elaborations (if not provided later in the book) are absent even when they were desired during reading. This makes the book better as a resource for an easily accessible library for quotes or notes of Karl Marx's writings.I like when Marx rebukes another philosopher of his day (especially Proudhon).Here's a quote pulled from the book:"The economic categories are only the theoretical expressions, the abstractions, of the social relations of production. M. Proudhon, as a true philosopher, seeing things upside down, sees in the real relations only the incarnation of these principles, of these categories which slumbered (as M. Proudhon the philosopher once again informs us) in the bosom of the 'impersonal reason of humanity'. M. Proudhon the economist has clearly understood that men make cloth, linen, silk-stuffs, in certain determinate relations of production. What he has not understood is that these determinate social relations are just as much produced by men as are cloth, linen, etc. Social relations are intimately connected with the forces of production..." (p. 108).
