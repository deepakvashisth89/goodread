Eric Newby is one of my favorite authors. He has a wonderful turn of phrase, and manages to get into hilarious circumstances. He also does his research, so the reader receives a thorough grounding in whatever Newby is writing about. A Small Place in Italy is an account of I Castagni, which I believe means the chestnuts, that Newby and his wife Wanda bought in 1967 as a vacation house.
The Newbys met in Tuscany during the war. Wanda was a teenage partisan, Eric was an escaped British soldier. They fell in love and married after the war. They went to live in London, but both of them dreamed of returning to Italy, to the tiny agricultural villages where Eric spent a few months hiding from the Germans.
When they found I Castagni, it was in deplorable shape. There were holes in the roof, wood rot in the beams, an extremely dangerous situation with the electricity, and piped in water coming in at such force that it blew a hole in the side of the water heater. It also came with a man named Attilo, who lived in the house, and it was implied, would continue t0 do so, despite a change in ownership.
I Castagni came with a small vineyard, which Eric set about putting into shape. The bulk of the book is about the grape harvest, in which the Newbys took part every October, picking their grapes and the grapes of all the farmers around them. The reader learns of the patterns of the harvest, the equipment, the meals fed to the workers, the various tasks involved in turning the grapes into wine.
According to Newby, the late 60s allowed Eric and Wanda to participate in the tail end of traditional agriculture in Italy. The farmers used mules and oxen to till their land, although they were beginning to own tractors. The farmers' wives had a couple of cows that they milked morning and evening, by hand. Chickens ran around in the farmyards. Few people owned cars, although the Newbys did, and walked along the footpaths that ran through the fields. On Saturday night, the whole neighborhood gathered for huge and rowdy meals during which the guests passed on gossip at the top of their lungs.
By the time the Newbys sold I Castagni in 1992, that life had vanished. No one had mules, horses or oxen anymore, the land was being sold for vacation houses, and instead of the rowdy gossip that enlivened neighborhood dinners, people ate "with one eye on their plates and one eye on the TV (screen). This is a lovely book full of affection for the mountain people of northern Italy. Read it.
Atilo did continue to live in their house, and was not only a true friend, but extremely useful since he knew how to manufacture any number of useful items in his workshop.
