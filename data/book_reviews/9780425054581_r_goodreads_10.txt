Unlike Triplanetary, First Lensman spends only its first brief chapter on setting up the status quo through an exchange between Arisian and Eddorian representatives engaged in their endless struggles along proxy lines involving untold numbers of planets and civilizations. Two diametrically opposed space-faring peoples, one adherent of order and other of chaos, have their designs set on how future should be shaped where humanity is but a small fish about to make big waves.Virgil Samms, formerly of Triplanetary League, realizes their current Solar Patrol is simply not enough to protect the Civilization after a valuable lesson in their recent moon situation had been learned. Yes, they must look beyond just humanity as there are people aplenty such as Rigellians and Velantians as willing allies. Samms, as well as his closest friend Roderick "Rod the Rock" Kinnison, set into motion a plan of a far wider-reaching organization that would protect integrity and justice for all. A Galactic Patrol, if you will. Putting aside all other issues from such an entity coming into existence, they also must resolve the very matter of logistics: who and how would actually qualify to serve, how to facilitate safe and reliable communication across the universe, and perhaps most importantly how to prevent someone from falsely representing the Galactic Patrol? It's pure luck, or perhaps not, that they get contacted by Mentor of the Arisian people who has a solution - the Lens. He will make Virgil Samms the very eponymous First Lensman, a unique individual no one will measure up to. Needless to say, there are obstacles aplenty as wherever be Arisians, Eddorians are sure to follow with their own counter plans...We're looking at a novel roughly divided in two halves. Firstly, we're dealing with a trifecta of obstacles our Lensmen have to contend with to even clear the path for the organization to exist. You might visualize it as three story arcs: political pushback, pirate factor and rise of a certain drug. Needless to say they all come together to one degree or another, but it lets more character have a spotlight and breathe. Second half is simultaneously both believable and sky high on optimistic altruism I couldn't help cracking a smile. How would the people of North America, Tellus actually vote when push came to shove to actually BIRTH this galactic organization into being? Kind of surreal when you think about the following: yeah, we're an organization with means to read minds, inability to lie when telepathically linked and an industrial complex on a hidden planet aiming to invalidate every government out there as supreme authority... did I mention we're genuine, absolute good guys with the best intentions? VOTE COSMOCRATS!As far as characters go you're still looking at the hyper competent sort I brought in my review of Triplanetary ages ago, but since there's more of them now they get to differentiate themselves a bit. A Lensman has to be of such moral character, strength of mind and conviction that they all inevitably fit the same mold with different specializations. "Lensman grade" becomes the single most sought after characteristic a person could possess. Virgil and Roderick get the most attention, one more of a thinker and other a military man, but both sharing a goal. I did like how younger characters actually get to do more of the action stuff, though. I especially liked the returning Conway Costigan, but seeing Jill Samms do the social subterfuge that guys couldn't almost makes up for the mandatory rescue mission she gets herself stuck in. And that's without having the Lens herself since, I don't know, Arisians thinks girls are yucky? Seriously, only one woman in the entire future history of Lensmen will carry the Lens, and Jill debates "how much of a woman will she really even be?" which got some eyebrow action on my end. Keep your secrets, Mentor.Now we reach the point that might get fellow readers to drop First Lensman. Writing itself. How do I put this into words? I would say Doc Smith likes to focus on things I don't particularly care for and you can tell this is an older novel purely from certain, errr, technological jargon purple prose. There are only so many "beams" and "screens" I can take when describing space battles, or how what was once "hyper beams" is now just "beams" because advancements here and there focused energy consumption enough to go past "prototype beams". He loves getting into that minutia in the face of conjured up fleets from two secret planets, where people of one are ALL without fail zealously loyal to Galactic Patrol, but a black ops mission where a team of mentally connected Lensmen get to break into a building and put a Lewiston round straight through the sadist's head? That gets half a page. I love enthusiastically debating how Lensmen must be better, but I also need some carnage from time to time. Characters conversing also tends to be very dry on the factual side and I'm not just talking about aliens. You could have a great time reading the entire novel in that 1950s news anchor voice, though.Would I recommend First Lensman? Yes, but I also see how someone would bounce off very easily. Reading it was at times a struggle, one arc was not AS interesting as others, and the second half is a whole lot of wishful thinking with a big space battle in the middle. Those sure were a lot more hopeful times than what we live in now.
