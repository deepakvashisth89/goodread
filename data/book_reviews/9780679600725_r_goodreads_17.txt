There are some things for which three words are three too many and three thousand words that many words too less.

Despite his above quote, William Faulkner always erred on the side of using the three thousand words rather than the three when describing anything. His dense, protracted verbiage, magnolia scented and Spanish moss bedraped, is sultry, stifling, sweltering. It’s simultaneously a major impediment to reading Faulkner, and the principle reason why you should. Faulkner’s baroque verbosity is oppressively brilliant.

In Faulkner spun a tale of a world haunted by its own demise. Again and again he used ghost imagery to convey the ultimate stupefaction of a Southern culture moribund to its own mythology:







The story Faulkner tells to illustrate his theme is that of the rise and fall of Thomas Sutpen. It is told and retold, repeatedly, by several different narrators, layering the tale with biased first hand accounts, second hand observations, as well as hearsay and conjecture, illustrating both how myths are created and the lie behind them. Sutpen came to Jefferson, Mississippi as a mysterious and wild young man — no family, no past, no connections — and proceeded to wrench a doomed dynasty out of nothing. His legend and furious ambition dominated the town both during his life and after his demise, and the story agonizingly peels away layers and slowly reveals the corrupt secrets that ambition and energy concealed.
