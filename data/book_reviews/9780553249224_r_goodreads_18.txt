Found this at the local antiques mall, a beat-up paperback with a beautiful painted cover (not this edition, I don't like changing editions on this site). I'm aware of L'Amour status as one of, if not the most, celebrated authors of westerns, but can't remember if I've heard one of his books before. Now was a good as time as any, especially seeing thatRadiganis only 150 pages. A lesson to any author wanting to get read.Knowing how many books L'Amour wrote, and the nature of the paperback publishing business back in the day, I expected a rather blunt, punchy, and simple narrative that hewed closely to conventional western storytelling tropes, but made for easy and enjoyable reading. That's exactly what I got, but it's worth noting that as plain as the language is here, there is a beauty to his portrait of the part of the frontier. Much time is spent detailing the frigid weather, the sense of isolation, the danger of life out in the wilderness, and though you could never call L'Amour a prose stylist, he does succeed in nailing this particular romantic image of the West without sanitizing the hardship. At a time where I feel more than a little ashamed and disgusted by American culture, and the myths Americans like to tell themselves, I must admit that this reminds me that there is something enticing about this image of hard-won American fortitude and industriousness, even if it is a lie.The narrative itself is more procedural than thriller, following multiple parties in a cattle war in the mountains of New Mexico, but seemingly unconcerned about generating any tension whatsoever. The omniscient narration does not attempt to obfuscate the trials of the enemy, and its hero Radigan and his two compatriots are so on top of things that there's never any doubt that they'll be victorious. Someone who doesn't care for the western milieu may struggle with this, because most of the book ends up being a recounting of the process of this small-scale frontier conflict rather than about the characters or the sense of danger. Fortunately for me, I like a barebones western, so this remained a pleasant read throughout.Even so, the cavalier structure did throw me a bit; there's only six chapters in the book, and the sudden and frequent perspective shifts combined with the lack of any attempt to generate real drama meant I never felt any sense of momentum. Nothing in particular ever felt particularly important. If I hadn't spent so much time reading old pulps, I might have been more bewildered, but I guess that's just how people wrote when they were being paid by the word and had an incentive to pump these out.
