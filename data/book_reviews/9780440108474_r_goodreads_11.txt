Bernice Reubens is certainly an amazing story teller. Unlike many family and historical sagas this one keeps you engaged with the characters all the time. This is where its focus lies, and Bernice Ruebens skilfully weaves in historical facts into the character's experience, never losing sight of them in long descriptions or diversions.I was not aware of Jewish history in Russian n the 1700s and persecution's by successive Czar's. The novel provides insight into Judaism ( family love, faith and culture) and how it often set Jews apart. The challenge to integrate or follow your faith is a constant in the brothers' lives.If you like your history, this is a fractional snippet of the rich history told in the Jewish museum in Berlin. At times it was hard reading. We are not spared the violence of the persecution that took place in the pogroms. It makes the constant flight of jews from the Eastern Europe to what they believed would be places of safety in Europe and the US totally understandable.Another constant is the love between the brothers of different generations. This human kindness is the redeeming feature that saves the cruelty of the 20th century episodes in 1940s Germany, and then East Germany and authoritarian Russia where the persecution becomes political. There is also love and kindness from non- jews towards their jewish friends. In both Russia and Germany, the regimes targeted free thinkers, Communists, poets, artists, and any who did not conform, In fact it's love that saves everyone. The last place of escape is Palestine (Israel), but not for everyone.An extremely readable book and a clever compelling epic narrative. Why Bernice Reubens was not celebrated more for this book I struggle to understand. It is a huge achievement.
