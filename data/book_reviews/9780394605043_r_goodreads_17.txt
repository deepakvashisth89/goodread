“The innocent are so few that two of them seldom meet-when they do meet, their victims lie strewn all round.”
Well, this was good. Written in the inter war years, it is acclaimed as Bowen’s masterpiece. It concerns Portia, a sixteen-year-old orphan, who goes to live in London with Thomas her half brother and his wife Anna. Here she comes in contact with Eddie, who, by his own admission, is no good. Inevitably she falls for him and he is no good. There is a solid cast of minor characters as well. So, it is coming of age, but don’t let that put you off. Portia knows that Thomas and Anna don’t really want her living there and her only support comes from an older female servant Matchett. Portia is very aware of her circumstances and Bowen describes her trying to slip under the radar rather well:

“Getting up from the stool carefully, Portia returned her cup and plate to the tray. Then, holding herself so erect that she quivered, taking long soft steps on the balls of her feet, and at the same time with an orphaned unostentation, she started making towards the door. She moved crabwise, as though the others were royalty, never quite turning her back on them – and there, waiting for her to be quite gone, watched …
Her body was all concave and jerkily fluid lines; it moved with sensitive looseness, loosely threaded together: each movement had a touch of exaggeration, as though some secret power kept springing out. At the same time she looked cautious, aware of the world in which she had to live. She was sixteen, losing her childish majesty.”

The relationship between Portia and Anna is also important, Eddie revolves around them both and Portia is damaged by both of them. There isn’t a great deal of plot, but it’s beautifully written.
