There's definitely some practical value to this book, especially in terms of its advice about things like scene analysis and considering how to distinguish between the action you need to perform and the thing you need to feel (since you have less control over feelings). I also like that they emphasize trying to perform with the other person/people on stage, rather than focusing principally on yourself and your own performance.

However, I have a couple of major recurring problems with this book. First, there's a lot of abstractions in the explanation of what actors should ideally do. Things like "perform truth" don't genuinely mean anything. The phrase "common sense" is such a cop out for any actual meaningful recommendation that it's worthless. These things are meant to suggest some kind of self-evident authenticity, but--and maybe this is the postmodernist in me--there isn't really any such thing as a self-evident authenticity, especially within the patently constructed space of the theatre. Finding ways to perform "as though" the events are real might make for a more naturalistic performance, but that isn't "truth," it's a facsimile of truth.

Additionally, I don't understand the advice for scene analysis to think about the necessary action "as if" it's something else. So, this is basically a three step process, where you determine the action the character is taking in the scene, then put that in terms of an achievable objective, then come up with a different scenario in which one might try to achieve a comparable objective. While I can see some slight value for this in terms of thinking metaphorically, I don't see any real practical value to it as an acting technique. It doesn't seem useful to me to add that layer of distance, especially if the "as if" scenario basically pushes you to do the same thing you'd have done if the focus was on the original objective of the scene.
