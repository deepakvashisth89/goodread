Andrew Worth is a science journalist. He’s finishing up a long project, a documentary on “franken-science” covering: reviving the head of a corpse to help identify the man's killer, a man transforming himself into a walking biosphere, actuarial implants, and a lobbyist group for Voluntary Autism. Andrew’s tired and having relationship problems, so he turns down his next engagement (to cover an emerging epidemic anxiety disorder, nickname Distress) for a cushier assignment: attending a physics conference on a tropical island. The floating island nation of Stateless is hosting the conference, focusing on new developments in competing Theories of Everything (TOE). Once Andrew starts interviewing famous physicist Violet Mosala, learns more about nut-job activists and receives disturbing warnings from various Anthrocosmologist groups, he realizes the project isn’t going to be so cushy after all.Distress is chock full of intriguing ideas—there are seven different gender/sexuality types—but not always well executed. There is decent dialog but the characterization is somewhat flat. I did not always understand how Andrew’s leaps of logics motivated his actions, and, well, not buying into all the metaphysical stuff. If I understood more of the book, I could give it another star. Unfortunately much of the physics (TOE, Aleph Moments) were beyond my comprehension, which meant I couldn’t always follow the various factions (scientists, activists, Ignorance Cults) and their beliefs. Of course that may be faults in the reader, not the book. Nit: I also wondered about the title since the “Distress” disease is irrelevant for most of the book.Still, Distress, like Egan’s earlier novels—they do seem to be improving of late; seeZendegi,Incandescence—is Take or Leave. Most of Greg Egan’s short stories? now those are Must-Read.
