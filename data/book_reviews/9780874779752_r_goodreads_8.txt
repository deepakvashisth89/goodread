The first part was mostly an abridged history of NASA, including mission details, politics, and historical causes for dormancy of the space program. It was enlightening because I’m so poorly versed in history, but not really what I was hoping for out of a section that claimed it would “describe how humans could fully become a type I civilization” though some scientific details were interspersed and the conclusion of part 1 nicely wrapped up the ideas of 1.) improve global transportation, 2.) lunar base, and 3.) research and commerce in space, which served as benchmarks for type 1. I do wish it talked more about the importance of and how to navigate global politics in pursuit of type 1, as well as highlighting potential for research collaboration and subsidization, but those topics were omitted. Part II, on Type II civs was somewhat similar, but towards its end and well into part III there were some great high-level suggestions and descriptions of ideas for sci-fi tech, benchmarks, and mission plans that could push humanity further along. Those ideas while less detailed were more interesting to me because he provided the pitfalls, difficulties and possibilities without blanketly telling the reader what had to be done and how- he provided some brainstorming and left the application up to the readers- which I think was more impactful. Sadly, I found the ending of the book somewhat lacking- Zubrin talked about the likelihood of alien species which I agree with in premise, but he really highlighted his habit of making a calculation (in some cases, a relatively baseless one with lots of assumptions) and then claiming that there are no other ways of looking at the problem except for his way and that one bad way that he (successfully and thoroughly in most cases) debunked. Overall the book was encouraging and well researched, (which I’ve come to expect from Zubrin) and I learned a lot.
