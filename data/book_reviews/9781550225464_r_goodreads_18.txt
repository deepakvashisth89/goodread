I read this book years ago but forgot to write a review. Being reminded of it today (because it is relevant to something in my own life) prompted me to say something…Before reading this book, I had only a thin understanding that Neil Peart had developed from the drummer (and drumming teacher) I remember from the early 1980’s, into a long-distance motorcycle rider and travel writer. I knew nothing about the tragedies in his life, so when they arose in this book it affected me so much that I walked around in a daze for a couple of days. I even wrote to Neil to offer condolences, and was amazed to get a reply, but saddened to read that he himself was ill (and has now left the building…).This book describes how Neil coped with adversity: he got on his bike and rode for long distances, while keeping a diary. Neil’s natural stoicism obviously stood him in good stead both with his problems and with his travels, because stoicism is essential for long distance two-wheeled travel - powered or otherwise. Two wheeled touring can be an exhausting drudge, interspersed with small moments of excitement. Except, when you are on two wheels on a highway in an underdeveloped country, you really don’t want too much excitement! I haven’t read any of the other reviews of this book, but I can imagine that much of the repetitive touring detail (which he also went through in his day-job while playing with Rush) might have been a bit dull for some people. But to repeat my point: would you really want anyone to go through any more of the non-dull stuff that Neil experienced? Neil needed something that kept his mind occupied. In that regard, staying upright and alive on a motorbike was a perfect choice.I think we can all learn a lot from Neil. One way or another, the problems he experienced are versions of problems we will all go through one day. For me, that moment is right now, so I am inspired to read this book again. I think that’s as good a recommendation as I can give.
