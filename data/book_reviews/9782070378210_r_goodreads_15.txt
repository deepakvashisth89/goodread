Masterful prose: terse, exact, mindful of negative space. Actually, there's a lot of negative space, and I didn't really "understand" most of the stories, which unfold like parables, hazed by historical (and mythical?) distance, and which land on obscure conclusions. It is difficult to separate the fantastical elements of witches, fates, and synchronicities from their remote historical settings in early renaissance Italy. Is this fantasy or historical fiction? It's not magical realism, but it's not really realism either, since the characters of many of [i]Last Tales[/i] are driven to ponder supernatural conditions relating to their own ambiguous identities - the surviving twin, the witch, the giantess, the insomniac. These call to mind stories of medieval miracles and claims of visions. When a supernatural incident aligns with a material explanation, are you really willing to dispose of the spiritual account completely? Or does it remain a curious thread that connects others, selves, earth and heaven in a rapturous cloth that swaddles chaos into order? Those of the Italian [i]Last Tales[/i] play on the ambiguities of reality and coincidence inherent in lived experience and history."Copenhagen Season" begins with an indomitable exposition on the political economy of early modern European society: the difference between country and town political cultures, the supreme importance of family reputation and inheritance, the view of the woman through the gaze of the aristocracy. These paragraphs should be not just read but [i]memorized[/i] in any class which reads a European parlor novel like Bronte, Austin, or Tolstoy; they should be printed in every preface of a a novel up to The Great Gatsby.Then, "Copenhagen Season" - the size of a novella - delves into a extraordinarily simple and forceful tale of chivalric romance that embodies another literary thesis about the nature of tragedy: though Ib loves Adelaide, he would have to do so secretly, as doing so openly would dishonor her. The supreme expression of his love lies opposite to their being together in an adulterous tryst: he removes himself from her, and throws himself into danger. Honor is upheld, tragedy is forged, love is preserved in the amber of its anguish.
