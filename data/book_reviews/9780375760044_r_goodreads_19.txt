Redburn, a novel that could be subtitled, "The Moby Dick Prequel."It's Melville's coming-of-age story, thinly veiled autofiction, and follows the unexpectedly funny exploits of young Wellingborough Redburn. Melville called it a "pot-boiler," which in itself is rather funny. Reader, it is not.Instead, it is a charming tale of a very naive young man who sets out for his first sailing voyage. It's got all the harbingers of the great Moby Dick--and even a reference to Ishmael, which for reasons I can't quite pin down, felt SO SATISFYING.The thing about Melville--or why I believe readers keep coming back to him--is that in between incredibly minute descriptions about EVERYTHING, he just comes up with these banger life observations so that you can't help but admire the guy.When Redburn comes across a woman and her three children slowly starving to death in an abandoned Liverpool building, he tries to get food to the woman but no one cares--not the police nor his fellow sailors nor the people on the street.He writes upon returning to a restaurant after the woman is dead: "Surrounded as we are by the wants and woes of our fellowmen, and yet given to follow our own pleasures, regardless of their pains, are we not like people sitting up with a corpse, and making merry in the house of the dead?"Truer words I can't find to describe then as well as now. 4.5 rounded up.
