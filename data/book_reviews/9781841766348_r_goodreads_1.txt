GREEK AND ROMAN ARTILLERY 399 BC - AD 363 was a little disappointing for me as most of the written material covered the design evolution rather than the employment of the weapons. A mechanical engineer would have understood this better than me, but I still got something out of it.

When one hears the word 'catapult' one probably thinks of that contraption that hurls a rock up into the air in an arc to come crashing down on the target. Actually that is what is referred to as an onager. The catapult was also a torsion-spring weapon but  it was much more like a giant crossbow that shot huge arrows as well as round stone balls. What was confusing was the words catapult, bow-machine, arrow-firer, and ballista seemed to be used interchangeably in the text.

One thing that was interesting was that after years of experimentation the Greeks came up with this formula that gave all the sizes for the various pieces for building a ballista to shoot a specific size stone shot. I couldn't follow it, but I would know the best position for the weapon on the battlefield!

As many as 2000 ballista stones have been found on ancient battlefields. The largest one recovered was a Roman one of 26kg.

Some reenactors, most likely in Britain, have built replicas of ancient artillery. The book says Alan Wilkins built a ballista that can fire a replica bolt (arrow) that can punch a hole in 2mm sheet steel at a distance of 50m. Wow! The historian Josephus claimed that a Roman ballista could shoot a 26kg stone 400m. This was accurate enough to fire at a town under siege.
 
Pictures and artwork are found throughout the book.

I don't regret buying this for my collection.
