Alan Dean Foster's 1974 Star Trek Log One contains 3 stories adapted by Foster from the screenplays of the early 1970s animated cartoon television series that had been spun off from the original acted series of half a dozen years earlier.

Never having seen the animated series, I can make no comparison between the original episodes and Foster's adaptations, in the way I occasionally have with James Blish's adaptations of the original series. I can comment, though, that the cartoon series evinces some differences from the series. Here, for example, we have a handy piece of technology called a "life-support belt," which creates a very thin but tough force field, meaning that characters can stomp around in vacuum or poisonous atmospheres as if in a spacesuit. And of course another product of the animated nature of the show is that we have a few alien crew members--three-legged and three-armed, cat-like, or winged, for example--who would have been too expensive to produce every week via elaborate costuming, along with other odd aliens occasionally encountered. These differences are commonsensical, at least in science fiction, and they do not draw attention to themselves unduly.

"Beyond the Farthest Star," whose original screenplay was by Samuel A. Peebles, finds the on its way to historical research at the Guardian of Forever, moving a bit "wider than its best course to the Time Planet, to enable it to take readings and star-map this section of the galaxy's fringe" (1976 Ballantine paperback, page 5). Yet here, where the starboard "view of almost unrelieved blackness" is broken only by very scattered "tiny dots of luminescence, dots which [are] not individual stars, but rather distant galaxies" (page 5), the ship is pulled inexorably into "the peculiar gravity well" of an "imploded" star (page 11). That sounds, by the way, like it should be a neutron star, but it unfortunately is described as "negative star-mass" (page 11), so...hmm.

In any event, a gigantic hulk like "A thousand cathedrals all thrown together" and "lit like a Christmas tree" (page 170) already has been in orbit there for "not less than three hundred million terran years" (page 20), and when it appears that the crew may have destroyed themselves purposefully (page 25), Kirk's problem of whether they can "ever break out of [the star's] grip" (page 15) is complicated by the question of whether they .

"Yesteryear," whose original screenplay was by D.C. Fontana, is the jewel of this book. After reaching the Time Planet, Kirk and Spock beam down with three historians and the tagging-along McCoy. The Captain and First Officer accompany one of the historians on a jaunt back in the beautifully "barbaric" timeline of the "early Empire" period of Orion (page 85), while the other two historians who lost the tossing of lots remain behind and scan different histories racing across the Guardian of Forever.

When the three return, the ever-wisecracking Bones "jerk[s] his head in Spock's direction" and deadpans, "Who's he, Jim?" (pages 86-87). Kirk's first supposition of it all being an elaborate "practical joke" that is "carr[ied]...off in style. Too much style" (page 88), of course is exploded by a check of the computer ordered by this timeline's Andorian second-in-command: Spock died at age seven, his parents then split up, and his mother was killed shortly afterward in "a shuttle accident at Lunaport, on her way home to Earth" (page 95). With the year of his death revealed, Spock suddenly remembers the "cousin [who] saved [his] life when [he] was attacked by a wild animal in the desert" (page 98).

And so into the seemingly stoic First Officer's past we go, with the so-called "cousin" seeing the young boy taunted and driven to furious yet impotent fisticuffs by jeering peers (pages 105-107), observing the subtle yet obvious disappointment of the youth's self-righteously overbearing father (pages 110-11), meeting a mother "more lovely than any memory," and "[i]ntelligent, gentle, and gracious" (page 112), and once again stroking the comforting fur of the boy's gigantic and fearsomely fanged lion-panda-like pet, "loving, intelligent[,] and protective" (page 110), of "advanced age" (page 110) and "snuffling in the early morning air like an old man with a sinus condition" (page 116), and yet never, ever willing to abandon its master. Really, it is a very touching episode.

"One of Our Planets Is Missing," whose original screenplay was by Marc Daniels, is one of those stories in which something huge never-before-encountered object is moving through space, only suddenly it appears to be destroying planets, and now its course is altering to-- Well, you know. Think of Norman Spinrad's "The Doomsday Machine" and Robert Sabaroff's "The Immunity Syndrome" from the original series, for example. Here, as expected, "[e]ighty-two million people will die" on Manitilles (page 144) if the cannot stop the object in "[f]our hours, ten minutes" (page 146). The tale does contain a nice twist, though, which definitely elevates it over the simpler-premised "The Immunity Syndrome," and the fact that I suspected it coming did not make it any less enjoyable.

My single complaint about the book is a minor punctuational one. When quoted speech continues without break from one paragraph to another--the speaker has a fair bit to say, that is, and breaking it into more than one paragraph makes it easier to understand--the convention is to keep opening quotation marks on the beginning of each paragraph but not use closing quotation marks until the end. Speech flowing across three paragraphs, for example, thus will have three beginning quotation marks--one set starting each paragraph--but then only a single set of closing quotation marks, at the end of the quote. Usage here is very spotty, occasionally correct but often incorrect, and 'tis a minor-ish annoyance. I think it likely, however, that the error is not Foster's but that of some random dingus down in Copyediting.

In any event, Alan Dean Foster's may begin with stories originally from a cartoon show, yet the adaptations are well done and aimed at an adult audience, and for any fan of the starship and its historic five-year mission, the book will be a swift and enjoyable 5-star read.
