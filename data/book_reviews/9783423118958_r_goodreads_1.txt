Move along folks, nothing to see here! Just a woman who got tired of her married boyfriend and so she drugged him and tied him to a bed in his birthday suit an torments him a little. Some way to deal with a failed relationship. Blood isn’t flowing but just about any other bodily fluids is. She regains her freedom by restricting his. So what. He’ll survive … possibly.

This is the first work in prose by Ulla Hahn who previously only published collections of poems. After I read the overall fantastic Hilla Palm tetralogy I became curious about this author. I found to be totally different from Hilla Palm. A part of the setting (a little town on the banks of the Rhine river) is the same, but that’s about it. The woman, Maria, who decides to take revenge on her boyfriend has nothing in common with Hilla Palm. Or has she? One biographical detail of Hilla Palm (in the second book of the series) might cause a woman to act this way and I wonder if that’s the reason for Ulla Hahn writing her novel in the first place. But I guess it's better to leave that out to psychologists.

The book was a mixed bag for me. There are some wonderful, hilarious scenes outside the bedroom (the “crime scene”). The people Maria meets on the street or in her shop (she’s a goldsmith) are aptly characterized with only a few sentences or utterances.
Inside the bedroom things get a little too yucky for my taste. Don’t worry, no trash or porn or trash-porn or anything like that. Some limits are explored, and, considering the state the man is in (tied to a bed for days on end), it’s inevitable that some not so pleasant but all too human incidents happen. I would have liked to hear more from “the victim”, but unfortunately a plaster over his mouth severely limits his ability to communicate. On the other hand some flashbacks reveal his bad character pretty well. So the reader is bound to root for Maria, although her manners are not exactly lady-like. It’s a strange little book. Interesting and perhaps polarizing. I wouldn’t recommend it as a starting point to Ulla Hahn’s work though.
