Car Sharing: How DaringThere is a very famous (at least, in its day and age) mystery novel by Fergus Hume,The Mystery of a Hansom Cab, published in 1886, which I have never read, but I suppose the basic premise to be similar to the one in Ngaio Marsh’sDeath in a White Tie: A dead body is discovered in a rented vehicle, and the question is, who did this?In Marsh’s novel, the victim is Lord Robert Gospell, a quirky old gentleman, who, for whatever reasons, from time to time gives a hand to Roderick Alleyn from Scotland Yard when it comes to solving a crime, but this time his mission in the case of a professional blackmailer in high society proves fatal because the Svengali behind the blackmail racket finds him out and then kills him. This is my seventh Roderick Alleyn novel and one of the best I have read so far because the author departs from her often-used system of leading us through uncountable detail-ridden interrogations, thus keeping the story suspenseful and varied. We also get some interesting characters such as Lady Carrados, who is haunted by a shameful mystery from her past and therefore a likely prey for the blackmailer, and her husband General Carrados, a man many years her senior, hopelessly in love with her but ravaged with jealousy of his wife’s former husband and inclined to torture himself as well as her with the knowledge he has happened to pick up.On the other hand, although ranked among the so-called Queens of Crime, Ngaio Marsh is definitely not able to hold a candle to Agatha Christie, whose plots are more of a maze, and Dorothy L. Sayers, whose Lord Peter Wimsey is simply more likeable than the rather condescending – e.g. in his dealings with his immediate underling Inspector Fox – Roderick Alleyn. Even though this is a captivating mystery with a denouement that seems plausible, I would give preference any time to either of the two other writers named above. As to the remaining Queen of Crime, Margery Allingham, I’d say that you would be better off with Ngaio Marsh.
