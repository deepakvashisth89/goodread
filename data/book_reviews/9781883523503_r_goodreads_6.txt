Private investigator Kate Brannigan is investigating a car financing scam on behalf of her client – the owner of a group of franchised car sales outlets. She and her boyfriend, Richard, are posing as purchasers looking for a high-end car, taking out finance so they can trace which outlets are running the scam. They buy the car, which is immediately re-bought by their client. All goes smoothly until one night, while the latest car is still in their possession, Richard decides to drive it into town. Leaving it parked while he works – he is a music journalist – he comes back to find the car has been stolen. The client is far from happy! But worse is to come – a couple of nights later, Richard sees the car parked on a street, and since he still has the key, he gets in and drives it away, thinking how pleased the client will be. And then the police stop him, since the car has been reported stolen. While Richard is trying to explain, they search the vehicle, turning up a hefty supply of illegal drugs and, worse, a photo of a child that is borderline pornographic. With Richard arrested and refused bail, Kate sets out to find the drug dealers and clear his name…It has been decades since I last read a Kate Brannigan novel, written back in McDermid’s earliest days as an author, and I believe they’ve been lightly re-edited for a modern re-launch, with ‘sensitivity readers’ scouring them for anything that might upset the easily offended. Given that McDermid has always been an anti-racist, pro-LGB+, liberal-progressive type, I can’t imagine what they found, but so it goes. Maybe she said black when she should have said Black, or perhaps she used ‘queer’ to mean strange – obviously shocking and unacceptable and really a cause for burning the book, if not the author. Anyway, I didn’t find them offensive then and I see nothing to offend now, but my sensitivity meter is old and set on low.Kate is a first-person narrator, past tense, and there’s a lot of humour in her voice, which keeps the tone of these considerably lighter than her much darker Tony Hill series. I had completely forgotten about Richard, though my memory refreshed, I now think he’s a steady presence in her life even if they both prefer to preserve an element of independence. They live in adjoining houses rather than together, but their relationship is stable and they clearly enjoy each other’s company. This gives the books a warmth which is often missing from the loner PI genre. To add to Kate’s problems, Richard’s young son from a previous relationship is due on a visit, so she has to wrangle all her friends into performing baby-sitting duties while she’s out on the job.The set-up is very good, but it stalls somewhat in the middle, with nothing much happening for a bit too long. Eventually, though, it picks up again, and takes on elements of the fast-paced thriller towards the end. As hinted at by the photo found in the car, the case turns out to be about child abuse and pornography as much as drugs. Apparently this must have been fine with the sensitivity readers, so long as she got the paedophiles’ pronouns right. (I promise I’ll stop being sarcastic now…) McDermid keeps it low-key, though – there are no graphic or disturbing images, although there is also no ambiguity about what is happening.I enjoyed revisiting this series. I don’t think it’s McDermid’s best work in retrospect, but it probably felt more original back in the ‘90s, when female PIs hadn’t yet become ten-a-penny. It shares some of the usual tropes of the PI genre – the tendency for the PI to be a bit superheroic and the need for the police to be incompetent to provide a reason for the PI to do their job for them. But her writing is always good, with that easy effortlessness which works so well for genre fiction, and Kate’s likeability and sense of humour make her an entertaining companion. I’ll happily read more, and I’m glad to see these being reintroduced for a younger audience. Hope it doesn’t traumatise their sensitive little souls too much… (Yes, I know I promised.
