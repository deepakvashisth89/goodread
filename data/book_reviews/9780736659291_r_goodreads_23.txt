This is the last Nero Wolfe novel Rex Stout wrote (he passed away not long after it was published, aged 88). It's maybe the least characteristic. I think it's evident throughout that Stout- a stolid center-left liberal with a strong commitment to the ideals of American government- felt deeply betrayed and upset by the Watergate scandal. The book is littered with references to Watergate, it's hinted that the central mystery might be related to the scandal, and Wolfe expresses disgust bordering on outright hatred for Nixon and his cronies (whose names are rattled off a few times). (Also curious: the 1974 elections come up; Archie mentions he voted for Hugh Carey, the Democratic nominee for governor- who won- but did not vote for Ramsey Clark, the Democratic nominee for Senator.)Both Wolfe the character and Stout the author seem shaken by Watergate (Stout directly, Wolfe via Stout, obviously). The mystery kicks off with a betrayal: a man is murdered in Wolfe's home while Wolfe sleeps; Wolfe is depicted behaving intensely and sometimes even irrationally in response, even to the extent of getting his detective's license suspended (and it isn't restored by the end of the book!); his movements are repeatedly noted as being out of joint with his (previously sacrosanct) routines, and he voluntarily leaves the house multiple times, while Archie notes that Wolfe has "regressed" to wagging his finger at interlocutors he disapproves of. Maybe the most overt evidence of Stout's upset is the use of explicit language that would never have appeared in a Nero Wolfe novel previously: "bullshit," "ass," a crude rape joke from Archie(!). It's not hard to read the plot as a treatment of Watergate, either: the initial murder is literally "close to home," and the culprit ends up being a long-running recurring character who is pressured into suicide as expiation for his crimes- a shocking, jarring thing to happen in a series as dedicated to the status quo as Nero Wolfe was. The overall feeling is one of rupture and dislocation from the norm, which makes for an effective novel, but which leaves this kind of an uneasy place to end the series.
