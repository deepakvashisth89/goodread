The political book club, which I WILL get back to this month, picked as its August read The Future of Success, by one former Secretary of Labor and occasional Dropout special guest Robert B. Reich.

This is not Reich’s newest book; in fact, it was published in 2000, and boy is it a blast from the past. Most of the content is not, to me, entirely new; a lot of the changes in the economy Reich talks about have been amply discussed in other books about economics and culture over the past quarter century. But even as Reich talks about the high societal cost of the fast-paced “new” economy of rapidly evolving technology, megabrands, and the commodification of the self, the most dated thing about the book seems to be its sense of optimism. Reich refers to the new economy as being characterized by “terrific deals”--technology that was useful and that you bought on purpose and that got better when it changed instead of worse; goods that were faster and cheaper to obtain but not noticeably lower in quality; entertainment that was increasingly entertaining and that was made to hold up when you watched it with both eyeballs. These days, we still have all the problems Reich talks about, and on top of it, the “enshittification” of all the stuff we’re supposed to buy (or increasingly, rent).

Overall this was an interesting time capsule, an easy read, and a decent enough overview of what was going on with the economy and the shape of American life in the time it was written. I think it’ll be fun to discuss as a precursor to more current books that touch on similar topics, like the attention economy.
