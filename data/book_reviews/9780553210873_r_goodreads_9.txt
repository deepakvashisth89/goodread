”It is one thing to mortify curiosity, another to conquer it.”A spoiler-filled review!(Still enjoyable even if the ultimate revelation is exposed to readers. After all, I’ve seen many reviews that include it as a part of the plot summary.)The story unfolds mainly through the perspective of Utterson, a lawyer and a friend of Dr. Jekyll. Strange sightings and events have occurred, involving the mysterious Mr. Hyde. And Utterson is determined to uncover the connection between Dr. Jekyll and Mr. Hyde, making this a story that leans towards a mystery.The Strange Case of Dr. Jekyll and Mr. Hydeis strange indeed, delving into the ultimate cost of ambition.”But the temptation of a discovery so singular and profound at last overcame the suggestions of alarm.”Once again, a familiar trope emerges, focusing on a man’s consuming hunger for reputation and discovery. The discovery in question is Dr. Jekyll’s core conclusion that‘Man is not truly one, but truly two’. One is the upright self, the moral, intellectual and reasonable side. The other is the ‘primitive’ side, mostly the evil, callousness, and unsettling part of man.He creates a potion that separates these two halves, each serving as an ‘avatar’ he can inhabit at will—essentially, an alter ego. Ultimately, this novel serves as an exploration of dissociation. Dr. Jekyll uses the potion to conceal his darker side by inhabiting his Mr. Hyde personality (and physical form, which differs from his original body), keeping his ‘main’ persona safe from public scorn while allowing himself to act immorally through his alter ego."Jekyll had more than a father’s interest; Hyde had more than a son’s indifference."But what is so engrossing is that both bodies and personalities have shared memories. Jekyll feels protective of Hyde, his creation. While Hyde shares the same memories, he’s also a being of unrestrained evil and has no capacity for empathy, reasoning and judgment, which explains his indifference towards Jekyll."The hatred of Hyde for Jekyll was of a different order. His [Hyde] terror of the gallows drove him continually to commit temporary suicide, and return to his subordinate station of a part instead of a person; but he loathed the necessity, he loathed the despondency into which Jekyll was now fallen, and he resented the dislike with which he was himself regarded."The relationship between the two personas gradually deteriorates, reflecting a parasitic, reciprocal hatred that mirrors a toxic parent-child dynamic. Hyde commits crimes that provoke Jekyll’s anger, and in turn, Jekyll resents Hyde. Hyde, evil as ever, wants to further disrupt Jekyll’s life, perpetuating a destructive, self-reinforcing loop between the two halves.However, technically and scientifically speaking, there is no chemical or medical procedure that can physically ‘extract’ the evil parts of a person and give them a separate body. This novel serves more as an allegory than literal science, showing what would happen if the ‘evil’ part of human nature is given a bit of attention."I saw that, of the two natures that contended in the field of my consciousness, even if I could rightly be said to be either, it was only because I was radically both."In the end, Dr. Jekyll realises that his experiment of splitting the nature of men into two has failed: that humans are an inseparable mix of both good and evil.Circling back to Dr. Jekyll's assertion of the duality of human nature, man is not one but two. With that in consideration, is evil stronger than good? Hyde eventually takes over the body more frequently, but is it because evil is dominant, or is it repression that is the ultimate cause of this breakthrough? Set in the Victorian era, societal standing and outward portrayal hold significant weight. That said, I think the constant repression of immoral desires and less 'civilised' actions caused Hyde's eventual acts of malice. With a chance to express himself fully, he doesn't hold back but instead commit atrocities.The Strange Case of Dr. Jekyll and Mr. Hydeis a brilliant book on its own even without thorough and profound reflection. I find that my reading experience—delving into it with no prior knowledge of the synopsis—was largely engaging and thought-provoking. Questions surfaced throughout, especially as Utterson continues his investigation. I absolutely love how, at times, I felt a sense of uneasiness—even if it is dull in comparison to traditional horror. The prose is elegant but not overly adorned, an easy read for those seeking a short classic to indulge in. Ultimately, an incredible story that I’m happy to have finally read.4 stars
