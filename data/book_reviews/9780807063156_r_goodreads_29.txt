★★★☆☆I get it. I really do. Corregidora is clearly less interested in giving you a conventional plot and more interested in making you sit inside the generational trauma of its characters—how slavery, sexual violence, motherhood, memory, and resentment can be inherited and carried long after the original violence is gone. Ursa’s story is basically a collision between her own life and the history she has been told to preserve.And honestly, Gayl Jones can write. The prose is raw, stripped-down, almost claustrophobic, and the dialogue has this hypnotic, blues-like rhythm. There are passages that feel genuinely haunting. The way past and present bleed into each other is probably the strongest part of the novel, because you can feel how Ursa’s ancestors are constantly occupying her life without literally being there.But also… where is the plot? 😭I kept waiting for the story to go somewhere. Things happen, relationships change, people hurt each other, Ursa reflects, remembers, has conversations, enters and exits relationships—and yet it often feels like I’m watching the same emotional wound being examined from seventeen different angles. I understand that this repetition is intentional. I understand that the lack of a neat narrative progression is part of the point. I understand that the “plot” is essentially the psychological inheritance itself.But understanding what a book is trying to do and actually enjoying the way it does it are two different things.The relationships are fascinating but also incredibly frustrating, particularly because so many of them seem to reproduce the same cycles of possession, violence, sex, resentment and dependence. Ursa herself is compelling precisely because she is so deeply trapped in this inheritance, but I sometimes wanted her to do something rather than simply remain caught inside it.And that’s probably my biggest issue: I admired this book considerably more than I enjoyed reading it.It’s short, but it feels dense. Not because there’s an enormous amount happening, but because every conversation seems to carry generations of history underneath it. The book is much more concerned with psychological and thematic movement than conventional narrative movement.So, 3 stars. I absolutely see why it is considered an important and influential work, and I can appreciate how radical it must have been when it came out in 1975. But as a reading experience, I kept finding myself thinking:Okay. I understand the trauma. I understand the symbolism. I understand the cycle. I understand what you're saying.But… where’s the plot? 😭Beautifully written, deeply disturbing, intellectually fascinating—and somehow still left me feeling like I was waiting for something to happen.
