This one's tough to review for me. I obtained a lovely used but like new hardcover complete novels and stories of Zora Neale Hurston last summer and, having read the first two and loved them (and being the completist that I am) gave this one a shot. I have to admit, however that I've always been an atheist by default, had no religion at all in my upbringing, and noticed at an early age that there's usually a pretty strong correlation between religious faith and other forms of gullibility and illogic. Yeah, I have over the years met many intelligent and cultured people who still, for one reason or another, believe, yet I've always found them to be odd anomalies that I cannot explain. The masses of the faithful appear to me to accept religion in place of thinking, as a way out of the responsibility for defining and making moral choices. At the same time they think their faith makes them better than the infidels, while statistics tell us they kill, rape, cheat, lie, and steal far more than their atheist brethren. Thus, to me, faith appears to be the ultimate conformity to mindlessness and the prime cause of the zombie-like stumbling around of the masses, charging off to war when ordered to, staring at the images on one screen or other endlessly to avoid questioning their divine leaders or thinking at all, and of course, in the democracies, voting for the worst of the worst, always against their own interests.

So, yeah, a Biblical retelling is not my favorite thing, the Bible stories that wow the easily amused generally bore me as stories and their supposedly heavy morals strike me mostly as ludicrous propaganda which usually leads men to be manipulated into doing the most heinous things--war, terrorism, child abuse, slavery, etc. etc. all the handiwork of religion. Add to that my own former nation's aiding and abetting at this very moment Israel, the most belligerent nation on Earth (pace to Russia and the USA who are also endlessly vying for this title) waging a mostly unprovoked war against thee of its neighbors simultaneously... Well, not a great time to read the novelization of the life of Israel's founding father as it were.

Like most of the reviewers here I did very much appreciate the Black American idiom used for the dialogue of the Hebrews, making the reason for the novel's existence abundantly clear: it's a metaphor of sorts for the African-American experience of slavery. In that, the novel is pretty great, especially considering the tensions between the Black American and Jewish communities, which I saw a lot of when I lived in NYC. Yet, for me, the spiritual/supernatural elements of the story--which, to her credit, Hurston often plays down or finds more rational explanations for--made my eyes roll endlessly. What rot. I can't believe people live by these fairy tales when there are so many better, smarter books with much clearer and better morals in the library--well, those that aren't banned by the war mongers, conformists, and persecutors who can't stand anything that competes with their holy book of dumb lies.

The epic scope of the novel is also impressive. Hurston put a lot of work into this long and very consistent novel. Although I was bored from the start due to the subject matter, Hurston was not and she very patiently writes Moses through every moment of his story with attention to detail, super perspicacious observations and evocations of the personalities of the many characters, and consistently beautiful writing. On its own terms, the novel deserves no less than the four stars that for me mean a very good book or a certain thoughtfulness, beauty, and stature.

The novel's best section--and where it almost made me change my mind about it, anyway where it really earned its fourth star for me--were the later chapters describing the years in the wilderness and the many instances of the community turning against Moses with their endlessly petty and self-centered resistance to his and God's will. This was so very true to how humans operate and is exactly why we can't have nice things. When it comes down to fixing the evils of the world or eating a hamburger, driving your car, watching a stupid game, making more money, giving up your bigotry, grousing about any old thing, the burger etc. wins every time. While I can't relate to the God thing, I was able to identify with Moses as an idealist or utopist--and a practical one at that. And the struggle of the idealist, no matter how smart, charming, impassioned, etc., is a hard road indeed. Although I found this section true, it was also remarkably pessimistic and seemed Hurston's indictment of her own African-American culture, the people in her community who were holding their progress as a minority back by their selfishness.

Also reading in this time of war, I had to note that, coming out of slavery in Egypt meant that the Hebrews had to then found Israel. They didn't find a vacant lot and build it there. No, they fought, killed, obliterated, and built their promised land on the bones of those they conquered and the land they stole. Just like the USA. Just like most cultures at some point in history.
