possibly my favourite Patricia Highsmith novel so far 😃I've only come to read Patricia Highsmith novels (and short stories) this past year or so, and in all of those I've read so far, I've come away with the feeling that her stories are very psychological - any suspense/crime/peril aspect is in largest part psychologically built, with any actual act being unsensational and overshadowed by the thoughts and feelings of the characters involved. the tension created is often thru the reader being equipped with an oversight of the characters thoughts and feelings, and any plots and plans they might have... by knowing what is likely to happen at some point, but not exactly when and how. and also thru some of the internal conflicts of the characters we're privy to.🏠🌳🏘🌳🏠I thought this particular book was very much a character novel, rather than a story novel. tho there is a story, and several stories within, all of which engage and hold interest, awa holding a sense of subtle suspense.and overall there's a sadness in this story, rather than any shock or suspicion. and it's there in both what would likely be considered the main storyline, and in the substories that surround it.I very much enjoyed it 😊🏠🌳🏘🌳🏠I enjoyed the characters and events, and how they were all linked together. the novel is set in a small area of New York, and revolves around a couple and their friends, all artists, or allied to art.and while the main characters were straight, there were realistic LGBTQI characters amongst their friends - not big characters, but enough to normalise, in a positive way, a diversity of sexuality within the book/novel, which I appreciated 🙂there was also overt criticism of racism - deliberately describing racism of the time in a critical way. it came in the form of the character Ralph, who is also antisemitic and has alot of other opinions around women and gay men. kinda neatly bundling all the problematic attitudes into his role as the creepy/inappropriate/problem character.🌟 🌟 🌟 🌟accessed as an RNIB talking book, a little siltingly, but well enough, read by Pauline Munroe.
