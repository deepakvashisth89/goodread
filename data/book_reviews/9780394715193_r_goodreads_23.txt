You know, I always used to shy away from non-fiction books, because most modern non-fiction writing is a single idea, at most worthy of a blog-post, padded out to book-length. The Gift is the exact opposite of that. If you'd ask me to summarise it in a single sentence, I don't think I could. A rare joy in a book. It's kind of like Wilson's The Outsider - yes, there's a general theme, but there's no one message, rather it's the author examining a theme from angles.

The original subtitle of the book is Imagination and the Erotic Life of Property, which is far closer to the themes than newer editions' . The new subtitle makes it sound like it's a book focusing on human creativity - some kind of 'you have a gift, you have to use it in your art' pep talk - but that's not really it. Like the original subtitle says, large part of The Gift are about , the first half is all anthropology and economics. It's all about gift economies of indigenous peoples: how a gift cannot be kept, it had to be transformed, , and how there was pride in 'squandering' a gift (and much confusion to Western colonists). After that there are two chapters/studies on Walt Whitman and Ezra Pound, but it's again not directly about an artistic gift, it's more complex than that. At the end we finally get a summary chapter that's closer to the 'new' subtitle, how do artists live in modern capitalism? Can we learn from gift economics? Can artists, when building their art as a gift to others, dip in and out of capitalist systems?

Lots to learn here - Hyde puts words to concepts to people like me who tried to grow up in the open source/open science space. The closing chapter even mentions PLoS, the Public Library of Science, one of the earliest Open Access science publishers, as an example of structures closer to a gift economy than classic capitalist economy. A place where everyone can publish their science and share it with the public.
(I guess Hyde could not have foreseen how classic capitalist journals have eaten up the Open Access movement: other OA journals like the Nature group now charge up to $15,000 to publish an article online openly. The movement has been captured by money.).

I think this one will stay with me for a while.
