In August 1961, the U.S, Navy commissions the submarine U.S.S. Thresher. Like the shark she’s named after, she’s designed to hunt and kill - in her case, not fish, but Soviet submarines. The first of a new class of subs, she can dive deeper, operate more quietly, and move through the ocean faster than almost any other submarine ever built., with the only real limitations being the supplies carried onboard and the endurance of the crew.In April 1963, the ship goes out to sea off the coast of New England for routine testing after a visit to the shipyard that built her. During a test of the ship’s condition and performance while submerged near her maximum operating depth, the Thresher reports that she’s having what appears to be a minor problem and that she’s taking action to return to the surface. A few days later, the worst is confirmed: the Thresher has been destroyed, with the loss of 129 people on board. The problem now is, the only people who know for sure what happened to the ship died in the sinking - and the Navy needs to figure out not only what happened, but what to do to keep it from happening again.Naval analyst Norman Polmar shares the story of the Thresher, the first nuclear-powered ship of any navy to be lost. Reviewing her brief career and the reports of the investigations into her loss, as well as interviewing former members of the crew and consulting with veteran submariners and oceanographers, Norman tries to piece together what might have happened in the moments before catastrophe struck. Considering numerous scenarios, Norman shares possible explanations for what might have occurred, how navy policies and procedures changed after the sinking to prevent similar sinkings, and shares the stories of other subs. American and Soviet, that were lost during the Cold War.“The Death of the USS Thresher” is an interesting look into an era that saw the advent and implementation of new technologies at sea, and how the prior success in bringing new technology to life might have led to a complacency about the limits risks associated with such technology. In some ways, it’s a naval whodunit, featuring a group of talented and experienced men trying to tell a story from a few seemingly random threads. The book also get that even in peacetime, protecting a nation can be a very dangerous - perhaps even deadly - business.
