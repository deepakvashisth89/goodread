2 stars,Metaphorosis reviewsSummaryAn informal, conversational style autobiography of one of the world's best songwriters and singers.ReviewSome time in the late 1980s, I ran across a Johnny Cash autobiography on my father’s bookshelves. A fan of Cash, I said, “Hey, I’m gonna read that.” My father said, “You’ll regret it.” I read it anyway, but he was right. For all his talent, Cash’s book was about a man behaving like an ass, and proud of it. Some years later, I read Ian Gillan’s autobiography with a similar lack of satisfaction. So, I was prepared coming into this autobiography of one of the best ever songwriters and singers; I expected feet of clay.Haggard makes no bones about the fact that Tom Carter is the writer of the book, though toward the end it becomes unclear just how that worked and whether Haggard had already done some writing. I know nothing about Carter, but presume him to be a professional ghostwriter. I was surprised, then, to find just how badly written the book is – from poor word choices and misspellings to an almost aggressive lack of organization. It reads – and perhaps is intended to read – like a stream of consciousness infodump, jumping from topic to topic and only occasionally back again. Half of Haggard’s early life seems to have been when he was fourteen – for years, it seems, if we go by the sequencing here. It makes much of the story hard to follow.That’s a problem, because, while Haggard is up front about what he did, he doesn’t seem very remorseful about it. In only a few of his stories, and highlighting that he expects his children to read them, does he admit that he was wrong. Much of the rest is grudging recognition that others found fault in him, mixed with thinly veiled braggadocio about things that now (but also when the book was published around 2000) seem pretty bad. One example – in castigating record executives, he notes that probably none of them would last a day in prison. Maybe not, and Haggard was clearly in prison a lot, but why is that a measure of anything at all?I didn’t expect to come out of the book with a better view of Haggard, and I did get a little insight. But largely I was frustrated that there was so little structure to the book, and so little of real interest. There are facts and anecdotes, but mostly it’s a surface-level picture of a man who did a lot of bad things and would do most of them again. There’s very little to tell us why and how he wrote such great songs and sang them so well. It feels, in the end, to be what it probably was - an opportunistic tie-in with an upcoming compilation album and show, rather than a serious attempt at a life story
