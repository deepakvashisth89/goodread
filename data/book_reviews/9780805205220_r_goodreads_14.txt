WOW. . . . This was a shocking account. I had no idea that the fight for women's right to vote was fraught with such violence and degradation :(For context I read this book after seeing the Broadway musical Suffs, a Hamilton esq musical about the suffragette movement. I'll admit I went into the musical with a bad attitude. I just knew in my bones that having a bunch of liberal 4th wave feminists writing about the suffragettes would be bad....and I was sort of right. There were a few songs that made me roll my eyes, and a few little plot points and lines that were BLEH...but overall, they honored the history and original purpose of the suffragettes well. They also inadvertently told a story that honored traditional femininity and bespoke a narrative of communion not competition (because this battle wasn't fought alone; it was only by working with men that the suffragettes succeeded). More surprising though was the plot itself. I had no be earthly idea how the 19th amendment came to be ratified....the musical had an excellent book and it was so surprising it made me wonder what was true and what was exaggerated and...well....it was all true and even worse than they portrayed!Enter this book. The first-hand account of the final year(ish) of the suffragette movement. After decades of playing by the rules these young women finally were fed up, and they began these rounds of civil protest that would lead to suffering but ultimately success.First, I want to say I appreciated that this was a primary source. It was literally the exact accounts compiled in the time they happened by Doris Stevens. Fascinating. YES sometimes the narrative overlapped timelines and didn't flow well, but it was still entirely immersive and fascinating.I also have to say this story hit home for me as someone who has spent much of her adult life participating in civil protests and as someone who at least twice a year finds herself standing on a sidewalk .... not picketing, but praying. My rights on that sidewalk...the ability to stand on a public sidewalk, to address people as they enter private businesses ...these simple things I take for granted but they were rights that others fought for enduring great hardship in the process. I cannot imagine being jailed for simply standing out of the way on a sidewalk. Or for carrying a poster at a march that we had permits for. But that is exactly what was happening in the 1910s. These women were beaten, harassed, jailed and essentially tortured....all for peacefully protesting and advocating for the right to vote.There were many times I felt myself shaking in rage at the many injustices wrought against these women, women ranging from their teens to their eighties! I even found myself in tears a few times. It was heartbreaking what some of these women went through ...Take for example the many accounts of suffering in prison. One woman reflected "It is enough to imprison people. Why seek to degrade them utterly?" They were indeed humiliated and dehumanized and degraded at every turn. And some of that was the abhorrent nature of prison in general for that time, but some of it was directed at them because of their cause.Take for example this direct quote from the attorney general "If you can just make what a woman does look ridiculous you can sure kill it" .....unreal!And then worst of all were the conditions the women endured while on their hunger strike. The musical Suffs depicted this so well. I remember watching it performed and I thought no, no... surely this is exaggerated for effect! It was not. The musical was directly quoting first-hand accounts of those on strike. They were literally strapped down and force-fed raw eggs and milk through tubes they violently shoved down their throats and noses....."Don't let them tell you that we take this well'"I didn't know where to breathe"Both direct quotes form their accounts ..and haunting lines from the musical.What was fascinating to realize too while reading this was the historical timing of these protests and the first world war. I had never placed the two events together in the narrative like that. . But as Wilson was advocating for and fighting for democracy overseas, he was allowing the women of his own country to suffer and remain disenfranchised. That really is wild...and the women were wise to capitalize on the hypocrisy. Using his own words against him was brilliant. This is an excerpt form one of their final speeches. It's fantastic:"President Wilson has gone abroad to establish democracy in foreign lands when he has failed to establish democracy at home. We burn his words on Liberty today, not in malice or anger but in a spirit of reverence for truth"🔥🔥🔥That is a fricken epic speech! I wish we spoke that way today regarding modern political struggles haha. It is clever and deep ...and anchored in Truth with a capital T! And a reverence for truth?! That makes me tear up a bit honestly ...All in all, it was a fascinating tale, though it was sad to hear how terrible the struggle had to be for it to succeed. It was sad to hear account after account of weak men who failed these women. From the lies of the President, the violence of police officers and random hooligans, vitriol from senators/state reps, and lies from the newspapers ...men failed to act in accordance with their dignity---- a dignity and strength which requires them to protect the dignity of those vulnerable among them. What a juxtaposition we saw in this story...that on the one hand men had the strength to go to war and die for liberty while others beat women at home for seeking that same right (something the author reflected on in the final chapters)What a gift though that good men did come forward and fight for this right. There were many who dissented from their party's majority. Who published papers in defense of the movement. And most notably I think of that little Tennessee statesman who voted against his party in obedience to his mother's will! That account still makes me tear up. It all came down to one vote...and he did it all for his mother!All in all it was a fascinating read, and I am glad to have learned the true story of our journey to women's voting rights. It's a little alarming we didn't learn this is school though ....wonder why that is 👀🫠😅
