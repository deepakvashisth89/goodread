EVENING

SLOWLY now the evening changes in his garments
held for him by a rim of ancient trees;
you gaze: and the landscape divides and leaves you,
one sinking and one rising toward the sky.

And you are left, to none belonging wholly,
not so dark as a silent house, nor quite
so surely pledged unto eternity
as that which grows to star and climbs the night.

to you is left (unspeakably confused)
your life, gigantic, ripening, full of fears,
so that it, now hemmed in, now grasping all,
is changed in you by turns to stone and stars.


Reading Rilke always reminds me how poetry seems to exist in another realm outside of reality. Fanciful, wistful, magical, and although it is shaped by reality, it refuses to be grounded in it. It’s curious how human beings developed the sophisticated tool that is language as a way to communicate as clearly as they can to each other, absorbing the words they lacked in their own from other languages, abandoning whole languages at times when they were no longer in use, but always remained with poetry. Which obfuscates, and where understanding seems to be based on a complicity between poet and audience that becomes a form of telepathy, which in part accounts for the difficulty in writing about it. Where language is codified, and transmissions emitted by the poet can be, and oftentimes are, missed by the reader and can register as babble, but at the same time there’s no greater point of sublimation than when they are received and understood. I’m at that point where incomprehension is no longer cause for frustration with poetry, there are many things I’ll never understand and it seems natural that that would extend in poetry too (though I thank the more advanced poetry readers and scholars who provide context and depth to certain text), but the most marvelous bit about it is that even when I don’t fully comprehend there is that echo that leans towards understanding and that still moves. It’s incredible.
