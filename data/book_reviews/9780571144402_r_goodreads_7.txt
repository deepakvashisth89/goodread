As the Western world again succumbs to dictatorship, bigotry, and militarism-cum-war, Vaclav Havel's words seem more than just cold war nostalgia. Again they read crisply prescient and even crucial. This collection of essays was offered in the opening period of glasnost, reflecting his Charter 77 experience under the Husak regime, with no foreknowledge of the New World Order to follow. There are six essays of his "dissident" period (though he loathed the label) and others tacked on, more or less as spine-filler, though some of them are worth reading in their own right. The volume and chief essay, "Power of the Powerless," are dedicated to Jan Patocka, inspirer of the Czech "underground university."Here we read his classic account of the "greengrocer" who decides to "live in truth" not by loud protest, but just simply refusing to "go along" with the everyday consensus demanded of "good citizens." In elevating the human spirit above all one finds the "platform" to defeat an order built on deceit and force. As such, it applies as much to the technocratic West entrapped in alienating technology and "democracy" as in the "post-totalitarian" regimes of the old East Bloc.His resolutions, however, to regenerate human society were in themselves nothing new: rediscover the natural, form intimate associations of mutual benefit, reject all that you know is false no matter from whom or how high. This follows the prescriptions of the 19th century anarchists, like Peter Kropotkin, moral philosophers like Tolstoy, or the non-violent analysis of Gene Sharp, as instruments of ethical social change. It would seem his message is as dated as these predecessors but is not, precisely because all remain eternally relevant.Yet Havel was caught up by political reality in ways such other philosophers (perhaps wisely) avoided. As (the former) Czechoslovakia's first freely-elected "post-totalitarian" statesman, Havel "in power" presided over the invasion of Western finance and technology, as insidious in its own manner as Soviet tanks; the "lustration purges," as ugly in process as the "people's courts" of 1945; and a whole-hearted embrace of NATO expansion as "the guarantor of democracy" - ideology again a masking ritual of militarism and empire.Though his latter career often upended his philosophy - philosophers in power do tend to surrender the ideal to the practical, like any greengrocer - his words remain as a necessary tonic to the vile swill the Western public is dealt by the post-truth, Artificial Reality regimes in which you and I live.
