Gershom Scholem was the preeminent 20th C. scholar of Jewish mysticism. This thin volume introduces Zohar, a central text of Jewish mysticism, which is also called Kababalah. The Zohar, a book of splendor, was written down, gathered, likely conceived and created by Moses de Leon in 13th century in Spain. It's a biggie -- many volumes.Scholem introduces 34 short essays, sermons, interpretations, homilies or riffs on Torah (first five books of the Hebrew bible). The Zohar itself, written in an approximation of 2nd century C.E. Aramaic (starting to get complex, eh?) often focuses on a roving band of rabbinic scholars, including Simeon ben Yohai.Scholem doesn't interpret or explain, but just lets the work and the words speak for themselves. One assumes it requires deep background knowledge. Yes, but it also works shimmering on the surface of thought. One assumes it works to see the invisible, unknowable God. Yes, but it reveals the aspects of God that might never be seen, except as weird, wonderful metaphor. Only 1% of what is there can be perceived by the five senses. What about the 99%? It's easy to see why Madonna, Ashton Kutcher, Ariana Grande, Britney Spears, Alex Rodriguez and Elizabeth Taylor have an interest in Kaballah. Yes, but Scholem presents this preamble to a subject that offers lifetimes of study. It's not just a hip pop culture thing.Grappling with issues of God, the meaning of life, life itself, death, the seat of the soul, the attributes of God and more is not casual stuff. In a homily on Exodus, with source citations to Job and Ecclesiastes, Zohar discusses three aspects of the soul after release of death. Nefesh (vital soul) hovers among the living. Ruach (spirit) returns to the pleasures of Eden. Neshemah (innermost soul) ascends to her origin, to reside by the light of Creation. Isn't that lovely?
