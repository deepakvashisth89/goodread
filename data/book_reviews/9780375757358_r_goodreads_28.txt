I often feel that much of the media that I consume can be traced back to Joseph Conrad. Here, the connection is to the trip-hop group Portishead; the closing track of their second album, , is titled . It is a great song, so when I had finished reading and , I wanted to see what had inspired Portishead to write it. I think I have found their common ground; but this is not a review of Portishead's , so I want to instead show why I found this book to be as interesting as it turned out to be.

The plot of rests on the back foot compared to the exposition and exploration of a country, society, and culture in the build up of a violent revolution. No matter, because the novel seems to benefit from it; I would've been glad to read another few hundred pages of Conrad's interpretation of pre-revolutionary Russia, but unfortunately the novel is only 314 pages long and does feel short because of how little actually happens in it.
Our perspective jumps across St Petersburg and Geneva, with the former focusing on Razumov and his interactions with, and surrounding, Victor Haldin, the Christ-like revolutionary whose sudden appearance sets into motion the story, while Geneva houses the rest of the characters, and later Razumov himself, who must deal with the fallout of Haldin's actions in St Petersburg.

Russia seems to be an impenetrable mystery from the point of view of our western narrator, a British language teacher who associates with the Russians of the story. It is a country where ideas of liberalism and freedom are not relevant to the body politic, and no matter what happens, there will still be tyranny and oppression, much to the wonder of the language teacher. This viewpoint comes from the bias Conrad likely held towards the Russian state, given their occupation of his homeland of Poland for the better part of fifty years, and is reflected in the novel as a thinly veiled contempt for the Russian people.
Along with this disdain, he lays out the suffering that innocents and bystanders face in the political battlefield played by both the tsarist and revolutionary intelligentsia, with characters such as Natalia Haldin and Tekla damaged by the institutional forces surrounding them. His cynical ideas were proven right unfortunately, as this novel also has the added complication of having become historical very soon after its publication, with the Russian revolution of 1917 ensuring another autocratic government would soon take the Tsarist regime's place.

This book does not wrestle with you to keep on reading page after page, but instead lets you sink into its story and ideas, like a handshake of sorts, because without the proper motivation for and understanding of the reading of this book, it could potentially be a ~300 page obstacle to overcome. I, for one, really did enjoy it and found fault only in its odd pacing choices, which are bound to be 'inspired' like any modernist novel, but were not as resolute as those in Conrad's other works. It is still ever relevant, over 100 years and multiple Russian regimes later, not only to Russia itself, but to any autocratic state facing its people's discontent.

As a whole, has only reaffirmed my admiration for Joseph Conrad's ability as a writer to weave compelling political narratives into layered and mature fiction that sheds light on the nature of human resistance and the pitfalls in fighting for a more just society.
