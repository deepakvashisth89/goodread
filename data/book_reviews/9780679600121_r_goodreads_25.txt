I know I said I would read this slowly, and I meant it when I posted it. The plan was to take four weeks, but the thought of it was daunting. I don’t know if this happens to anyone else, but I can’t just put a book down and pick it up again multiple times. When I do, I feel disconnected from the story and its characters. But I really wanted to try given the density of this book. The long and short of it is that the spirit was willing, but the flesh was weak.

That said, this was one of the strangest reading experiences I’ve had. I have a physical copy in French and a digital copy in English, so I kept switching between the two. Sometimes it felt like taking one step back for every ten steps forward, especially with the Kindle copy being 1,666 pages, about 200 pages more than the French edition. Still, I am glad I did it because it kept me engaged. NOW



is a book that is impossible to sum up neatly because it is about almost : justice, mercy, love, suffering, hope, society, identity, and sacrifice. Even setting aside its length, the story is so vivid that it can be occasionally exhausting.

Victor Hugo, like many writers of his time did not believe in brevity. He lays the groundwork of the novel brick by brick, layering politics, history, and character-building. He is so meticulous in giving background on each character it’s astonishing. He exhausts every possible analogy, and at times seems to get lost in his own descriptions. The bright side of it is from time to time, even he admits that some information may prove useless. There are long-winded commentaries before or after pivotal moments, and there are chapters entirely dedicated to essays on seemingly trivial topics like slang and the history of Parisian sewers. The structure is massive and complex, but there is a method to it. Everything has its place, even if it is sometimes hard to see in the moment.

The story is set in the years leading up to the . The tone grows more political as the story progresses, and it is easy to see now that the characters never truly stood a chance, neither in their personal lives nor against the sweeping political forces of their world. The personal and the political are tightly intertwined. I never read in high school, and while I knew it was about “miserable people”, I realised that I didn’t know the details of the story. That is why my review comes in two parts: a spoiler-free section where I share general impressions and a spoiler section where I go into my deeper feelings (aka rant). I think it’s only fair to do this since I imagine there are readers out there who are in the same boat I was before reading.

I kept wondering how to even begin reviewing a book of this scale. is monumental, and the structure is unusual. But that same structure also offers a kind of guidance. So I decided to follow Hugo’s architecture and reflect on the novel through its five parts.


Even though this section carries Fantine’s name, Hugo begins elsewhere, with the figure who becomes the very life force of the novel: . A former convict punished for stealing bread to survive, Valjean’s journey from hardened criminal to moral hero forms the central thread of the book, introducing its enduring themes of justice, mercy, and redemption. Hugo loves to put law and morality at odds, but in earnest, all he’s really saying over 1,666 pages is this





Before the novel delves into Fantine's story, it shows how Bishop Myriel’s extraordinary kindness becomes the catalyst for Valjean’s transformation and everything that follows. It is a slow opening, reflective and philosophical, yet deeply necessary.

Fantine’s story is devastating not because it is shocking, but because it feels inevitable. Poverty, social injustice, and cruel circumstance all take turns in dismantling her life piece by piece. There is something unbearable about witnessing suffering that could have been prevented. By the time Valjean steps fully into Fantine’s life, her tragedy is already in motion and the damage has been done. In the process, Hugo has already laid the social and emotional foundations of the novel.

After the bleakness of Fantine, Cosette’s section reads almost like a fragile light. Not happiness exactly, but relief. Valjean’s transformation becomes fully visible through his love and devotion to Cosette. There is tenderness here, protection, and the delicate rebuilding of something resembling hope. Here the story emphasises one of its central themes: humanity and interdependence. Small actions create a ripple effect. One good deed can set off a chain of others. Yet Hugo never lets the reader forget that safety is temporary and that history, politics, and society are always looming in the background.

Another reason I’m conflicted about this section is because this is where I fell in love with the second character to claim my heart: . Hugo develops her beautifully here, revealing her attachment to Marius, her jealousy, her quiet heartbreak, and the painful imbalance between the depth of her love and his indifference.

So, It will come as no surprise that I did not care for Marius and Cosette’s romance, if that is even the right word for it. On Marius’s end, it frequently read more like reverence for an untouchable work of art than any real emotional connection between two people. He feels scandalized and angry with her when she unknowingly shows her stockings to a passerby. He claims she is so “precious” that he would sooner imagine himself with a prostitute than with her. Man, are you mad? It’s such an insane way to frame love. It reveals something deeply unsettling about how he idealises her into untouchability rather than relating to her as a real human being. The contrast between his thoughts and his moral indignation is almost comical. But how lucky for him! How positively deplorable, because the obsession is mutual. I don’t know why I’m like this. In general, I love romance novels, but in books with this level of complexity, romantic subplots often feel out of place. Here, it was like seeing a coworker at the grocery store. Completely illogical, I know, but I can’t help it.

This section is quiet, reflective, and emotionally brutal in a different way. After the chaos of revolution and so much loss, you would think one might be granted some small respite, but Hugo has tasted enough misery to know that nothing else will do. The ending is not about relief, but about reckoning, love, and the lingering cost of a life lived in struggle.

I was almost pushed toward a one star because of how frustrating some characters are. With a story like this, it truly feels like all or nothing. I wanted to tear the book into pieces and burn it dead. But in the end, that would only hurt me and my wallet.

Experiencing the novel through its five parts makes it feel less like a single, contained story and more like an enormous tapestry of lives, ideas, injustices, and moral questions. I found myself drawn to Each section shifts in tone, focus, and emotional weight. The story is unruly and excessive, yet that very excess is part of its identity. Personally, I hope no other book ever attempts something on this scale again. Once is enough. Finishing this feels like an achievement, but it would be immoral to put a reader through this kind of torture twice. It is clear that for everyone involved with this book, characters and readers (whether they love it or not) alike, there is only one ending: misery.

Marius, bloody bastard. Frustrating, hypocritical, privileged, ungrateful arse. Of all the injustices in the world, he survives when everyone else dies. He doesn’t even last long once the attack begins. He passes out immediately, and Jean Valjean saves him. And how does he repay him? With disgust and disdain. True, he didn’t know it was Jean Valjean who saved him, but Valjean is still the man who raised Cosette with love and care. That alone should be reason enough to honor him. Instead, he tries to manipulate Cosette away from Valjean, as if he has any right to dictate her life.

And Cosette. I don’t even know where to start. Ungrateful twat. She is so naïve and easily manipulated. I will never understand how she didn’t pick up on the tension around her. She has to meet Valjean downstairs, the fires aren't lit, the room is cold and lifeless, and even little details like the chairs being moved from one day to the next escape her notice. Girl...



She acts like none of it matters, and I’m screaming at the book because the answers are right in front of her, but she’s completely oblivious. She went days without seeing Valjean and actually believed the excuse that he was traveling, despite all the alarming signs right in front of her. How could she forget the man who took her in and cared for her like his own? I hated that they got to Jean Valjean in time. I wish he had died before they arrived, so they would have been tormented for the rest of their miserable lives.

Poor Jean Valjean! His entire life was a tragedy. After losing his real family, the only person he came to love treated him so carelessly. What was the point of being so selfless? What was the point of all that sacrifice?

And Éponine! I know she wasn’t perfect, but now I can barely remember any of her mistakes. All I care for is her heartbreaking ending. The pain I felt when she died was visceral. How cruel! That she sacrificed herself for someone like Marius. That in itself is one of the greatest injustices in this novel. You know what? I’ll just deduct one star because I can’t —
