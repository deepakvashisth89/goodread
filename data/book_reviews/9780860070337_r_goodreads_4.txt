2024/72Victober Read No.3Sorry Thackeray, but Kubrick did it better.I was having a walk as I normally do in the afternoons, when I suddenly saw a family putting up the Christmas decorations at their house. It was so strange to me, because I felt we just had the Day of the Dead a few days ago, but then I realized it was already November 20. Time does go by when you are having fun, doesn't it? I also thought about the number of reviews I haven't typed, the number of books I have yet to read before the end of the year, and this air of nostalgia just set in. So here I am, typing one of my October reviews by the end of November. I guess better late than never.Barry Lyndonwas not on my plans for this Victober, if I'm being honest. It came to mind after agreeing withAlanthat we would be watching Kubrick's adaptation of the novel, and having done that, I felt I had to go to the book right away. The movie, by the way, is one of the greatest pieces of cinema ever made. Three hours that felt like one, a perfect cinematography that makes this film as wonderful as it is; shot after shot, just a few before you start to realize you won't watch anything like this in a while again. The story of Barry Lyndon in Kubrick's view doesn't change much from Thackeray's perspective, but when it does, Kubrick takes Barry to the next step. He always improves the book, which is supposed to be the purpose of every adaptation (alas, many filmmakers fail to do so). For the most part, both writer and filmmaker are holding hands, both are guiding our protagonist together and creating the same story (Thackeray with a pen and paper, beautiful descriptions, and a prose that surpasses that inVanity Fair; Kubrick with landscapes and visual effects that would amazed anyone who watches the movie for the first time—the drunk scene will never be erased from my mind).It baffles me that novels likeBarry Lyndonare usually overshadowed by a more famous novel when it comes to the same writer.Vanity Fairwas certainly not a good beginning for me when reading Thackeray a few years ago—even though satires are usually my cup of tea—and I was skeptical about picking up another of his books in the future as I thought it would be exactly like that. That's why the author was never among my priorities, until Kubrick came to the rescue.Barry Lyndon(the book), unlikeVanity Fair, is a picaresque novel, there are many humorous moments from beginning to end, but it doesn't feel repetitive and ludicrously long asVanity Fairdoes. Nor does it have a gigantic amount of characters that you end up forgetting half of them by the end of the story (that was my experience with his so-called masterpiece anyway). Lesson learned: don't judge an author by the first book of theirs you read.Finally, it is important to point out two big differences between the novel and the movie (don't worry, no spoilers ahead). The novel is narrated by Barry Lyndon himself, who is—Thackeray wouldn't have done it otherwise—very unreliable. You will notice this starting in chapter one. The movie, on the other hand, is told by an omniscient narrator. Whether this affects or not the course of the story is something you must find out by yourselves. The second difference comes at the end of both works. Even though Kubrick's ending is low-key similar to Thackeray's tone-wise, Kubrick does something mind-blowing here. The course of both stories changes abruptly, and both the novel and the film get different, yet successful endings (in their own way).I like to imagine that when Kubrick readBarry Lyndonhe enjoyed the character and his coming-of-age story that he wanted to have his own version. He took the opportunity and created his Barry, one that will be preserved for posterity. Well done, Kubrick.
