Actual rating: 3.5 stars

My thoughts on this one are a little all over the place....
I recently read "Exposed" by Kimberly Marcus, and similarly to that, I feel like this book had the power to be so much more than it was, but unlike exposed, I think this did better. It was a solid good, but ultimately, I think it could have been AMAZING given a little more volume, depth, development, and substance. Anyway, to make my life easier, I'm gonna copy and paste a piece of my review of Exposed that also fits this book, because I do have similar feelings about certain aspects...

SO HERE IS THE REGURGITATED, BUT FITTING PART:
"I'm not really sure how to rate this book. On the one hand, it discusses something incredibly important, and it's a novel that is necessary, and sadly, continually relevant, no matter how many years go by. On the other hand, it just didn't move me that much. [Though this book did better and moved me a bit more, but not as much as some other, newer issue books I've read.]

Perhaps it's because I'm spoiled when it comes to "issue books" (I call them issue books, because the central plot revolves around a legitimate hardcore issue, like rape, eating disorders, abuse, etc...). I have had the pleasure of reading books like Speak, Some Boys, anything of Ellen Hopkins in general, List of Cages, Hate List, The Female of the Species, All the Rage Reason to Breathe, I could go on forever (in fact, if you're interested, check out my teen-angst-and-tough-issues shelf). The problem is that because I've read so many of these books, I've seen when heavy issues are tackled well, where they're explored from all angles, the characters are raw and real, and the writing is done well enough that you honestly feel for the characters, that you hurt when they hurt, that you understand their struggle, that you relate to them, and the book lingers with you well after you close the cover, because it made you think and feel and consider your own life and your own values, morals, everything. When issue books are done well, they not only provide a compelling read, but touch you in ways that are hard to explain, and most importantly, make you think. These books are important because they make people think about their own lives, they foster discussion and help us talk about these things in the real world. But when they aren't done well, they just kind of fall flat.
And I think that's the real issue here. It's not that [in this case: Love, Sara] wasn't a well-written book, it's not that it was bad per se. It's just that I have come to expect so much more. I have come to expect deep, complex characters beyond just the main character. I've come to expect side characters and secondary characters, even villains to be fully realized and nuanced enough that they feel real. I've come to expect real emotion, the kind that's palpable, where you can truly connect with the character that's struggling, you can feel their frustration, confusion, hurt and anger, because it's written so well that their feelings are tangible and real. I've just come to expect so much, that books like this fall a little flat."

HERE'S THE PART THAT I'M WRITING SOLELY FOR THIS BOOK:
While I don't think this book fell flat, I think that it could have been even better. This could've been a truly moving exploration of both a girl who's suffered abuse and betrayal coming to terms with what acceptance and love feels like for the first time, and an exploration of how when love overcomes reason and leads to lapses in judgement, it can have dire consequences (i.e. teen pregnancy, and how NOT to deal with it). And I feel like while this book isn't necessarily in any particular department, if it were fleshed out more, it could be even MORE powerful. I think if we got to learn more about the characters, if they got to be developed further, if we'd gone deeper and more thoroughly explored what they were thinking from all angles, then this story could have been a riveting, and heart-wrenching masterpiece.

And this is where I think it maybe would have benefited the story to have some third person interludes, or maybe omniscient pieces, even a dual POV, so we got to see things from Dulcie's side. Because I think if we'd gotten to truly explore Dulcie's character, and how she felt about things, that would have been very powerful. I wanted to know what she thought, how she felt, I wanted to learn more about her relationship with her parents, and to better understand their reactions to her pregnancy, to better understand what drove her to make her choice. I think if we'd gotten to see both her conversations with Sara, but also diary entries from Dulcie's POV, then it could have made this much more real, much more deep, and much more touching.

And on that note, I wish that we'd gotten to explore Sara's character a little more. I wanted to learn more about her past, details, maybe more fictionalized short stories of Sara's that delved into her horrid past, or flashback style snippets, something. Because I think her backstory, not only with her father, but with the subsequent foster homes she was in after that, before she was with Carol, would have made for poignant reading. If we'd gotten to explore all her innermost insecurities, fears, anxieties, and explored her trauma a little more, it would have been profound. I wanted to know details about how she acted out, how the foster families reacted to her traumatized behaviour, and what sending her back and forth to foster homes looked like.

Now granted, I've read a lot of reviews where people describe things like that in issue books as "torture porn." And I suppose, to some, reading a long book filled with different traumas might be that, but for people like me, who have experienced comparable trauma for real, but are now healthy enough to not be triggered, there's something healing about it. I read issue novels that are so painful they move me to tears, because it helps me deal with and process my own pain in way that's controlled. I can close the book anytime I want and put it down if it's too much, or gets too real. But usually I keep going, because something about reading other people's torture, other people's hurt, helps me let go of and process my own and move past it, and it helps me feel a sense of solidarity. So that's why I like for my issue books to dive really deep and get to a place where they probably ought to come with trigger warnings, because sometimes, when you're in a good enough place to handle it, triggering yourself in a manageable way can be healthy, can help you become stronger, so that the triggering things upset you less and less each time. I can connect with a character enough to feel their pain, and thus, to some extent my own (which is reminiscent of that characters'), but all in a healthy and healing way, because the story is fictional and I can put it down at any time. Does that make any sense?

Plus, I think books like these, that talk about serious issues, are important, because it gives us a great starting point for real world conversations about these issues that we need to have. Especially given the age group they're targeted at, one of the most volatile age groups (by that I mean that teens are some of the people most susceptible to eating disorders or suicide. It's such a hard time already, transitioning from child to adult, from dependent and innocent, to grown and independent, that outside pressures make it even harder. As a teenager, just figuring out who you are and dealing with anything bad that is happening in you life or has already happened is near impossible, let alone facing the pressure of becoming an adult and all that THAT entails.) And not only are these emotion heavy topics important for teens because they're dealing with them, but also, because they are the age group that's going to be the newest adults, making decisions in the world and affecting it. So I think it's important to give them reading material like this that helps them start to confront really tough issues, and helps them to see those issues from different perspectives and think about them, because then they can go out into the world being more knowledgeable, compassionate people. And books like these essentially instill compassion, because even if a reader has never been through anything like that themselves, they can see how it feels and affects other people, and become empathetic to those situations.

Also, I think that the ending was wrapped up too quickly, and of all the parts of this book that could have benefited from some expansion, it was the end. I so wish we'd seen not just the brief snapshot of healing, but gotten to see more of the healing PROCESS. I wanted to read more about how Sara went from the not so great place she was in to one where she felt that it would get better. Because ultimately, THAT'S why I read these books. I like to see and feel the worst of the trauma, and then I like to go through the healing process of ti WITH the character(s), because it helps me heal. And I feel like this ending was just kind of rushed. Anyway, I guess my point is that I love books like this, and while this book was good, I think had it been fleshed out more, it COULD have been up there among the best issue books of all time, because it had the potential to be. If only for a little more character development/substance, and another point of view, this could have been truly amazing. It could have been one of those books that ripped your heart out, then stitched it back in your chest with a little hope at the end. But alas, it was just decent. I guess decent ain't so bad though...

My random thoughts I cared to write down throughout the book:

from page 8, in an email from Sara to Dulcie: "don't ferget waitin fer that friggin train..."
AGH! Teens don't talk like that. They don't even type like that. Even back in the days of the dreaded chatspeak (because messages at one point cost money after a certain amount of characters, so yeah, we did have to shorten the language), no one would intentionally misspell things in that way. If it didn't shorten something, it was still typed as normal. "Ferget" isn't any shorter that "forget," and doesn't save you any time or characters. That's just plain stupid. At most it would shortened to "4get." Same goes for "fer" and "for." No, just no. Don't do that. UGH. It drives me nuts when adults assume that teens chat a certain way and get it so wildly wrong its not even funny, it's just somewhat insulting. If you really want to include "authentic" teen dialouge, then you know what you do? You go find some ACTUAL teens to proof-read those parts, like a focus group, and get their input on how they'd write that email or diary entry or text or whatever so that is actually IS authentic and not insultingly stupid.

from page 33: "ASHLEY: [...] I am totally nervous. Does it show? KARA: You look totally great."
Too many "totally"s. People use contractions when speaking. Also, no one ever said totally that much. I was there as a teenager then. I totally remember. And the word totally wasn't that popular.

On page 57, Dulcie emails Sara basically saying that Sara was right about the boyfriend's rich friends. It took her all of 2 days to go from being pissed at Sara to realizing she was right. That was AWFULLY fast. I'm pretty sure that nothing is ever resolved that quickly or cleanly when the extremes are as polar as being pissed and hating someone for saying something, to being super apologetic and begging forgiveness. Especially when nothing happened directly to Dulcie, all of sudden her boyfriend just decided he didn't like his friends anymore. WTF. That seemed awfully fast and kind of random.

On page 93, we have to deal with "fer" again. And "shtuff"? Are you kidding me? No, just no. Adult trying to mimic teen language and failing alert.

The very next page, page 94, and this "fer" shit is really starting to irritate me.

From pages 112 to 118: Sara's "Silent Auction" short story is actually really good, and I liked it so much, that actually, I wouldn't have minded if it had been expanded to be an actual full short story, because I really would have enjoyed reading that. Damn, I wish that were it's own full short story, or even novella.
