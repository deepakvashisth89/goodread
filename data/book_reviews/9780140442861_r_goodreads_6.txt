Plutarch was a Greek born historian and moralist who later gained Roman citizenship. He lived and wrote in the latter half of the first century AD and beginning of the second. His most famous surviving work is his series of character studies called the Parallel Lives. He paired accounts of similar Greek and Roman historical figures together to illustrate ethical points. The 22 surviving sets of Lives cover six centuries of history. This book contains nine of the Greek lives and one standalone life he wrote of the Persian Artaxerxes. The Greek lives are of Alexander the Great and his contemporaries. For several of these people, the sources that Plutarch used didn't survive, so he is a primary source for some things that we know about them.The majority of the biographies presented here are of successful generals. There is one Athenian politician, Demosthenes. In all of the Lives except one (Phocion), Plutarch presents moral failings of the person that he is studying. However, he is very careful to also highlight the positive qualities of his subjects. Only in one Life, Demetrius, does he outright condemn the subject from beginning to end. In most cases, this is a very effective presentation, although I wondered if he was too easy on Demosthenes.Demosthenes was a skilled orator who continually exhorted the Athenians to resist the Macedonians. He was able to convince them to do so, but when it came to participating in the battles, he turned out to be a coward. It was also found that he accepted bribes from the Persians. After the Macedonians had triumphed, he escaped, but as he was being arrested in his place of exile, presumably to return to his execution, he committed suicide. Plutarch praises him for his steadfast, principled stand against Macedonia, and takes his suicide as further evidence of his principles. He chides Demosthenes' greed in taking bribes and his cowardice in battle. But I had to wonder, even though Demosthenes did not waver in his opposition to Macedonia, was he really all that principled? Or was it the bribes he received from Persia, who had an interest in tying Macedonian troops up with conflicts in Greece, that were the real motivation.The Life that I appreciated most was that of Phocion, a contemporary of Demosthenes. He is the one person that Plutarch praises without reserve. Partly this is because Phocion was part of the oligarchic party, and Plutarch was of the same persuasion over unfettered democracy. But Phocion was also an accomplished general and unwavering in his principles. While offered several bribes, he always refused, and lived very simply. At the same time, he was extremely pragmatic. While Demosthenes always railed against Macedon, Phocion argued for what he thought were sensible measures, including not doing things that would bring the full wrath of Alexander upon Athens. Yet Phocion also defended his enemies when he thought they were being treated unjustly. After the democrats were restored to power by one of Alexander's successors, Phocion was sentenced to death by the assembly along with associates that he tried to save by putting all the blame on himself.I very much like this line from Phocion when speaking to one of the conquerors: "You can make me act against my wishes, but you shall never make me speak against my judgement."One very helpful thing about the edition I read was a wrap up chapter at the end that puts all of the people in context with each other, describing the historical events chronologically in the world context.
