I have to say I was disappointed with this book for several reasons, not the least of which is because this book is one of the foundations on which “modern American” Christianity is based. I will admit that there are several conversations between characters that still hit the mark as far as a person’s personal, spiritual walk, but all in all I felt more ostracized than inspired by the progression of the plot.I often talk of Christians putting on the “wrong armor”, and it seems this book exhibits a great example of the first three pieces. First: supposedly several hundred members of these churches made the WWJD pledge, but the book only focusses on the endeavors of a few wealthy and influential individuals. This is clearly the “Belt of Bigger Purpose”, which focusses on the exploits of the champions and kings while filtering out the idea that God might have something to say to the rest of us. Not all of us can be wealthy newspaper owners or successful singers, so where in this novel is there inspiration and encouragement for the rest of us? Am I supposed to just assume that my role in Christianity is to be like the poor, reformed alcoholic whose only struggle for the rest of his life is to pray for deliverance from the saloon? Is it only my pastor and his few, wealthy friends who are the players in the game—and my only role is to show up on Sunday and tithe? Or do I need to somehow wrestle my way into a position of power so I can then do what Jesus would do with my ill-gotten wealth and influence?Second, the smoothness in which the characters succeed at their endeavors sickens me. This story was written more than a hundred years ago, but I can still smell the televangelism promising easy success if we just “pray hard enough”. I can tell you from personal experience, there is no millionaire heiress ready to step in and fund my efforts to write what Jesus would want me to write. This is the “Breastplate of Breakable promises”, and like the Stormtroopers from Return of the Jedi, those who wear this armor are no match for teddy-bears throwing rocks and sticks—which is to say, this book is great for those who want to feel “inspired”, but those promises of a happy ending are worthless to those who are really in the thick of battle.Third, this book makes it far too easy to put on the “Helmet of Self-Righteousness”. According to the author, the only real evils in the world are money and liquor. In fact, it seems, alcohol in all of its forms is purely evil. Money is only good if it is used to combat alcohol. Is this the origin of the eye-hole-lacking bucket-of-a-helmet that modern Christians don so they can see nothing beyond their own, finite cause and their own, limited definitions of justice and righteousness? Where is the deeper understanding of human nature? If I were to ask myself “What would Jesus do?”, would I really think Jesus would identify good and evil based on modern social, economic, and political issues? Wouldn’t Jesus look deeper, understand us to our very souls, and address the real issues instead of the saloon on the corner and the evils of industrialization?I’m not down on this book because it is behind the times. Human nature is still human nature, whether displayed by those who ride in carriages and communicate by telegraph or those who live in modern times with all of our technology. But I can trace many of the corruptions I find within “modern” Christianity to the ideas put forward by this book, and for that I cannot help but wonder whether the author now regrets having written it merely to “fill his pews.”
