I’m attempting below to write some kind of coherent review of Rabbit Redux, but I think these Updike novels are difficult to review. Please forgive me if I fail to turn the chaos of my thoughts into rational order.

Harry Angstrom, Rabbit, is a mid-century modern man in the midst of mid-life angst (thus Angstrom, or angst-storm). He is a flawed protagonist; at times he is hard to take. He is a man who makes mistakes. He wants to believe in God but that belief would interfere with the way he is living his life. He makes morally questionable choices. He is racist, misogynistic, homophobic and a philanderer. He views women in terms of their sexual apparatus and he is not above hitting women.

Harry is negligent. He wants to be a better son and a better father, probably a better husband, but he flees from commitment and invites trouble into his son’s life. Harry is visited by the consequences of his actions in both novels, but I won’t reveal them here as they are spoilers.

As in Rabbit Run, he once again escapes his perceived marriage trap, this time when his wife Janice has an affair and moves out. His world is disrupted and he is disoriented.

Janice is having her own mid-life crisis. There is a very interesting passage (with one extremely long run-on sentence) about the way that Janice (Updike) views her female life, in a reductive sense, from birth to death on pages 57 to 58. It is too long to copy here but a fascinating perspective.

Harry is caught or lost in the changing world of the late 1960’s, buffeted by those changes, cannot adapt and acts very much like those who feel that the good life is being ruined. Like many men from the 1950s, he expected more from the American dream. Like many today, he blames others. Don’t we all currently have certain relatives that feel the same as Harry, those with whom we avoid talking politics? We still love them.

Updike’s humanism becomes clear as the reader is prompted to forgive Harry’s behavior and failures. Personally, I put a high value on forgiveness.

In the process of writing such a complicated character, Updike shows his mastery of precise and observant prose by shining a light on the mundane and everyday beauty/ugliness/realness of ordinary life with carefully crafted descriptions of people, places and actions. As to actions, there is a passage, for instance, where the character Jill is driving too recklessly and Updike says Rabbit “keeps slapping his foot on the floor, where there is no brake.”

And Updike asks the big questions, too. Is there a God? What is the meaning of life?

I read this book as a Buddy Read in the Goodreads group Catching Up with the Classics. For more thoughts on the symbolism of Rabbit’s nickname, the meaning of Updike’s transparency descriptions of the character Jill and about the references to the first lunar landing and space, you can read my posts in that thread.

If you do read the Rabbit series, I think the novels should be read in order.

Rabbit Redux is a complicated novel. 4 stars, but worth 5 at times, and repeatedly. Updike is an important writer and should be widely read and credited, keeping in mind that he reflects a lot of views of his time, which may not be your own contemporary view. I guess in the end, I have to put my rating at 4.5.
