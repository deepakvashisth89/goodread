Rated PG.I really wanted to like this book! The problem was that, by the end, it couldn't decide whether it was trying to be fantasy, science fiction, or realistic fiction. (And it it's not realistic fiction.) The biggest disappointment was the last chapter--both versions! Because they tried to be too realistic, when the rest of the book was walking a fine line on suspended disbelief. Honestly,The Silver Crownreminds me ofA Wrinkle in Timewith fewer plot threads woven in and more holes. And a far more unsatisfying explanation of evil and wrongdoing. O'Brien did a decent job until the last chapter or so painting a fantastical tale of a mysterious silver crown that brought with it strength ... and a string of disasters, stalkers, and terrifying running from kidnappers. And then O'Brien had to try to logically explain it somehow, which ended up smashing my suspension of disbelief into shards. Because the explanation presented ultimately tried to drag a fantasy straight into the real world or very close to it, but the cause/effect of the silver crown in the first few chapters -- not to speak of things like the hypnotic black castle and the silver/black crown power struggle -- doesn't work in the "real world," Kentucky or no Kentucky.For instance, let's talk about Otto. Not the biggest plot hole, but worth talking about. Awfully clever of him to cause so many truck rollovers by disguising the curve warning. But it was horrific parenting on the part of his adoptive "mother" to say NOTHING to him about it and to assume he'd just keep doing something dangerous if she told him that she knew what he was doing and to quit. And to send him along with Ellen with the idea that AUNT SARAH WILL OF COURSE BE A BETTER "MOTHER." Maybe true, but quite odd.
