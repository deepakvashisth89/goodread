The Hunter family returns from England and is devastated to learn their pets are missing in the Canadian wilderness for nearly a month. Jim thought there was no way his elderly Bull Terrier (Bodger), the Siamese cat (Tao), and the young Labrador (Luath) could survive the devastation of such harshness and challenges found in the wilderness.

This story is a beloved tale of the fierce loyalty, survival instincts and unique bonds as the trio face everything that goes against them, while Luath wants to go home to his master. His pack follow and support each other on this journey.

During their 300-mile trek across the rugged Canadian wilderness the trio faces constant life-threatening dangers. ranging from a protective mother bear and her cub that make things so much worse for Bodger. The Cat is chased and nearly killed or drowned by a lynx and river currents that sweep him downstream.

I am sure without the intervention of some deeply compassionate strangers they would not have made it. Help from the Ojibway with scraps of meat, Helvi nursing Tao after his fight in the river, the farm couple with food, removal of the quills from Luath and a very gentle but lonely man with a chipmunk and stew.

At the lake house the family is celebrating Jim's birthday but in the distance Elizabeth hears barking but is it the trio? Young Lab...Tao...or the old Bull Terrier...Did they survive? Did all three make the final leg home to their family?

Burnford approach is masterful while writing the story without the pets using human words to convey their thoughts. She describes their choices as instinct and pack survival to communicate between them using and describing body language with verbs of sensory and smells.

Burnford's own real life trio inspired the story with the remarkable close relationship between her Bull Terrier, the Siamese Cat and her young Lab who would guide the Terrier with his failing eyesight. The story is that of her Bull Terrier, Bill with his fiercely loyal and fun side so that he would never be forgotten.

4.5 Stars for what the The New York Times praise calling it "a small masterpiece" that avoided the typical sentimentality found in animal stories.
