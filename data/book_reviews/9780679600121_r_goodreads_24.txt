If I don’t think too hard about it, this classic is definitely moving. But the more I sat with it, the more I felt like the emotional punch wasn’t all that genuine. Hugo’s idea of “humanity” feels more theatrical than real. A lot of the characters come off like they were stamped out of the same mold - big emotions, tragic fates, but not much depth. For me, a truly great book doesn’t just tug at your heartstrings - it makes you think. This one didn’t quite do that.

Take Jean Valjean, for example. He’s supposed to be this saintly figure, but I just couldn’t buy it. He spent 19 years in prison, was treated like dirt before and after, and somehow comes out of all that as a flawless do-gooder with zero bitterness or street smarts? I get that Bishop Myriel inspired him, but even then, wouldn’t someone who’s been through that much have a bit more edge or insight into people, or human? His blind kindness feels more like a symbol than a believable personality.

Bishop Myriel, on the other hand, makes sense. He had the background to be that kind of person. No complaints there.

Then there’s Fantine. She grew up in poverty, had no family, started working at 10, and moved to Paris at 15. Yet she’s portrayed as this pure, trusting soul. I’m not saying she couldn’t be kind, but her level of naivety doesn’t match her life experience. After being abandoned and having a child, she still trusts a random couple just because they have kids? And she never checks on Cosette, even when she’s told the girl is sick? It feels like wanted her misery to be poetic, even if it didn’t make much sense.

Another head-scratcher is Valjean’s confession and re-imprisonment. His past crimes were already served, and the silver theft was forgiven. The only thing left was a misunderstanding with a kid over some coins. So why confess to something he didn’t do, only to escape later and save Cosette? It felt like drama for drama’s sake, just to reinforce his saintly image.

And don’t get me started on his career glow-up and mayoral stint. He’s basically a superhero - except he doesn’t seem to understand people at all.

Overall, the classic feels pretty disconnected from reality. That might be why it’s beloved by the general public but gets side-eyed in academic circles. It leans heavily into moral and political romanticism, idealizing the poor to highlight the evils of capitalism. That message resonates with a lot of people, and Hugo’s fame and the historical setting help boost its reputation.

But for me, great literature should show real humanity - flawed, complex, and believable - no matter the character’s background. Without that, even a sweeping epic like this falls short.

I used to admire Hugo a lot, but after reading this 1, I’m less impressed. I still respect him - his humanitarian spirit is admirable - but as a novelist, didn’t win me over.

Maybe if I’d read it as a teenager, I’d have loved the grand gestures and tragic heroes. Now, I’m more into stories that reflect life honestly, with characters who feel real and endings that offer hope without sugarcoating reality.

2.4 / 5 stars
