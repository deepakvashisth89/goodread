'Do you ever feel that the world is not enough?' Peggy had asked him that at least twice. Ray had wanted to find out from her how the world was not enough, and Peggy had finally said she meant that the stars and the atoms, the systems of religion which stretch the imagination and still remain unfinished, and all painting and music--all this was not enough, and the human mind (or maybe the soul for Peggy) desired more.
By now, I've read most of Patricia Highsmith's novels and a good number of her short stories. 

Highsmith is today remembered mostly for two of her earliest works - 'Strangers on a Train' and 'The Price of Salt' (aka 'Carol') - and 'The Talented Mr. Ripley' (the first of 5 books about the titular character). Though there's little doubt that the author has her devoted fans, it seems that, with time, she is less talked about. 

Certainly a number of her subsequent novels have unfairly taken something of a beating (more so in the US; her rep appears to remain stronger in Europe). And that's hard to fathom since (overall) I've found her to be a consistently satisfying writer. Even something that I found to be a bit of a lesser work ('The Tremor of Forgery') still has much in its favor. 

'Those Who Walk Away', as it turns out, came as a particular surprise. I think it's among her finest novels; ultimately a strangely compassionate one. 

~ though some would not agree. One reader here at Goodreads, in fact, seemed to bemoan the fact that "nothing happens" in it. Well... I'm not sure what novel that person read, but it's sure not the one I have just finished. To a degree, I can understand how it can *seem* as though nothing happens in it - esp. compared to Highsmith's more popular work - but it's simply not so. 

The novel is a singular, intricately structured cat-and-mouse game. A few times the cat strikes - and the mouse seems to ignore that those things happen. At one point the cat is more definitive and the mouse strikes back. But the upshot of these thrusts is nothing that can be anticipated. As a result, the periodic 'scares' have their own uniquely unnerving quality (as does the masterful conclusion).

On top of all of that is the setting: Venice; a particularly leisure location which lends a sort of laziness (mixed with luxuriousness) to the tone and atmosphere. Highsmith's step-by-step depiction of the city is evocative, almost hypnotic. 

But even that takes a back seat to the neurotic pas de deux in the minds of the two main characters; two men unexpectedly entwined by a death which, in the final analysis, has nothing to do directly with either one of them (though each believes it does) - but is more deeply personal. 

[Knowing Highsmith's work as I do (as well as certain information about her personally), I couldn't resist the urge in thinking that her portrait of the main character - Ray - sort of feels like a capture of the kind of person Highsmith wished she herself had been.]

As the narrative flows, Highsmith gives the reader plenty to bring to the table and privately chew on. A good part of the prize here rests in the peripheral conclusions you yourself will come to - or will have to, since Highsmith does leave a few things up to your own understanding. 

On display is a tension of a different kind. Much of this has to do with the residual effect of unexamined guilt. One thing Highsmith understands quite well: how things can fester.
