It definitely feels of its time - what with all the psychoanalysis and its very specific perspective (that of a cis, heterosexual white woman), but I think Molly Haskell articulates the allure of the Old Hollywood stars so well — what directors and the actresses themselves were trying to bring to the table, how the women on screen were ultimately perceived especially by female audiences, the myths America likes to tell itself about women, and how its idea of what a woman is and can be has changed over time. It's a great dispelling of the misconception that the trajectory of women's representation in Hollywood film is on a positive linear trend: Haskell describes the dip in great characterizations of women in the 40s onwards, acting in part as a reaction to the "ruckus" women were making offscreen (women's lib).I'm most familiar with the the 20s-50s, so I enjoyed those chapters the most, and I thought that most of what she said there was spot-on. I especially loved her writing on Dietrich and Lubitsch. Compared to Classic Old Hollywood, though, I'm not as familiar with New Hollywood and the mid-century European arthouse films, so I skimmed much of the latter half.All in all, I really enjoyed this. Never have I highlighted so much passages in a book, she just has a way of words that enlightens or brings a new dimension to my previous understanding of Old Hollywood, perhaps my most beloved corner in film history (well, it's in competition with the Filipino melodramas of the 1970s and 80s, but I digress!), and I could see how she influenced the writers I turned to during my teenage OH fan life, like Karina Longworth. Two thumbs up for me, as they say, especially if you're familiar with the movies she's talking about!
