Mood: Critical
Theme: A dispassionate description of the enigmatic Ronald Reagan as told by his jealous biographer.
Pace: Choppy and disjointed, jumping between author's memory of events and facts.
Writing Quality: Creative but not fluid, often hard to follow
Length: 672 Pages

Edmund Morris, the Pulitzer Prize winning biographer of the Theodore Roosevelt trilogy, was chosen to be Ronald Reagan's authorized biographer and White House historian at a dinner given by Senator Hatfield in the White House, circa 1983. Morris was quite reluctant to do so because he was still working on his Theodore Roosevelt series at the time. (Ronald Reagan told Morris that he and his wife read his books at night in bed, which Morris was almost offended by, as he did not believe this at all). It felt almost a cloying way to win him over. He spent the next 13 years researching and interviewing everyone he could to write this overly thorough account of Reagan's life.

There are so many things wrong with this book, it's hard to know where to start...

The most annoying part of it for me was the fact that Morris takes a creative approach to this memoir by interposing and interweaving his own made up biographical life story with Reagan's- as if they had known each other in their boyhood. Through various twists and turns their lives periodically intersect and the author hopes to revive the time period and story with various viewpoints. After a young Edmund Morris leaves Eureka College to go to flight school, his friend "Paul" continuously writes him letters obsessed with young Ronald Reagan as he's a star lifeguard at the time and mediocre football player. The two keep tabs on their "friend" over the years as he rises to stardom in Hollywood. It's very off-putting and just strange to read these made up letters and stories often mocking Reagan. It's as if they are two very jealous peers who can't stop gossiping about him. This sets the tone for a very weird biography from a man who is up close and personal with Reagan, as well as his friends and colleagues, and clearly doesn't like him. It becomes quite apparent very soon into the book that Edmund Morris was always slightly jealous of Ronald Reagan. The constant criticisms and witticisms overlay the narrative, and it's often jocular and trite. Morris also has an obnoxious habit of persistently using French phrases throughout the entire book, yet never translating them into English for the reader, a cocky nod to his rich and snobbish upbringing. One gets the hint that he feels he had a way higher I.Q. than Reagan, and if only he could have been noticed...

Reagan was known as "Dutch" as a youngster. The son of an alcoholic father and very religious and stoic mother, he found God at an early age. His deep convictions and religious background clearly shaped his policies and genial demeanor during his various careers as sportscaster- film star- soldier- union leader- corporate spokesman- Governor, and President. His favorite book "That Printer of Udell's by Harold Bell Wright also greatly impacted him (and is a FANTASTIC book, I highly recommend everyone read it.)

One quote by Francis Mitterand's crew, President of France and highly intelligent, he observed Reagan at a dinner during the G7 summit and Morris gleefully includes this quote..."This is a man without ideas and without culture. A sort of liberal, for sure, but beneath the surface you find someone who isn't stupid, who had great good sense and profoundly good intentions. What he does not perceive with his intelligence, he feels by nature." I thought this pretty well summed up Morris' views as well.

Here is another quote that elegantly sums up Edmund Morris's viewpoint on Ronald Reagan..."formidable will, based on mediocre understanding of the facts. As often in politics, ignorance sustains strength."

I often wanted to set the book down. It was so chaotic and dry. I also felt it unfair to Reagan because he achieved some amazing accomplishments, like ending the cold war, massively rebuilding our military defense, boosting the economy with "Reaganomics", dramatically improving morale in the country, earning the highest approval ratings of almost any president in history, ect., but Morris was able to pick apart his character and cast doubt on his truly good nature as if he was an actor playing the ultimate role of his life, and not really earning the position on his own merit. I just couldn't get past the clear dislike by the author. He was sparing with his praise but did give it at times when praise was due. I really had to push through to finish this book which is sad because I've read and LOVED all 3 of his Theodore Roosevelt books. His writing is top notch, evocative, and even poetic. Overall there was too much of the author in this story, he overshadowed his subject and came across as petty and dull.
