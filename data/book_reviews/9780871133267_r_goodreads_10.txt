By now, along with a few collections of her short stories, I've read most of Patricia Highsmith's novels. At the moment, at least, I'll be skipping the last of the Ripley books ('Ripley Under Water'), as well as the one novel Higihsmith herself regretted writing  ('A Game for the Living') and her last novel ('Small g: a Summer Idyll'), which just doesn't interest me.I think - for one reason or another - all of the other Highsmith novels did very much interest me. But not 'Found in the Street'. Sad to say that, overall, it's a surprisingly boring novel.I was tempted a few times to DNF it. I had a feeling early on that the spark wouldn't be there. But, out of respect for the sheer number of her novels that captivated me, I pushed forward. Even though, for most of the way through, the book never seems to get off the ground or get in real gear.Eventually one thing does happen - and it's more sad than shocking - but, after that, there remains about 100 anti-climactic pages to go. Specifically, to go nowhere much.Part of the problem is that Highsmith has a focus on just a few characters - who do next to nothing other than their day-to-day existence. (One main character does a lot of stalking - and that could be called 'doing something', except here it's not.) There are subordinate characters on the periphery but little is done with them as well. Things just sort of plod along, without plot development to speak of.What's odd is that you can *feel* Highsmith in the text, trying to work some of her usual magic - yet, simultaneously, it's as though the author is fighting boredom. ~ as well as a sort of pointed distaste for the '80s and the era's culture.Having lived in New York City's West Village during that decade, I recognized a good deal of the atmosphere that Highsmith describes. Yet Highsmith extracts nothing of the Village's charm during that time. I realize she was writing a thriller but there are many times when Highsmith paints things a bit too unrealistically scummy and generally dismal.I did sort of like the undemanding nature of the central marriage and its sense of openness. But I'd've liked that more in a more interesting storyline. At close to 400 pp., the story goes on way too long. Odd that Highsmith would extend things to that degree, considering it all sadly reads like automatic writing.
