I didn't really focus on any one thing that stood out to me this time, but a couple things from above:I didn't find John and Suzy as bad towards Vicky as I did before. I still don't think it fits the story or is a fitting resolution for the themes. One thing that did really stand out to me this time was Grandfather's comment that people are mad at God for being more forgiving than we are, and I really like that. I think it's true, too. God is slow to anger, so when people get so upset about evil people "getting away" with their sins, it's because they want God to punish them right away, in our time, but they don't realize that He isn't like us--He wants people to have time to change, and His time is soooo different from ours. Like Jesus says, "To you, any time will do," but that's not the case for Him. I also had interesting thoughts about oral versus written tradition because it's discussed in the book as if writing is progress, but I actually think that, after reflecting on it during this read, writing narrowed culture and society a lot. What I mean by that is, yes, writing is preservation, but who writes things down? In the beginning, privileged people, right? I mean, who learned to read and write first? Men, wealthy people, religious leaders, etc. Even in the book, Grandfather goes to this African village and writes down their stories, and it's seen as a good thing because he's "preserving their culture," but really, he's taking control of their narrative. Why? Because he comes from a place of privilege. He can read and write, and so, he's setting their stories down as they are now, killing them, basically, because oral stories are living and breathing. Oral stories and histories were for anyone and everyone. And I think, yes, oral stories can be corrupted and changed, but there's more societal and less personal responsibility, so it holds more people accountable to stick to the truth because more people have a share in the history and the stories. When stories and histories get written down by one person, they begin to own that story/history, and they can change it however they want, and who's to say they're right or wrong? Again, this privilege was taken by the privileged, and so, it inherently creates marginalization. I think about this largely because I teach Children's Literature, and we discuss the oral beginnings of fairy tales. But people started writing the stories down, and even though women started this first, how many people really know Countess D'Aulnoy? They know Hans Christian Andersen and Andrew Lang and Charles Perrault, and of course, the Grimm Brothers. But what about the women who told and wrote these stories first? Their voices are lost, and it's because they didn't have the privilege in written culture that they did in the oral cultures.All that being said, I still love this book and will read it again, I'm sure. I didn't dog ear any new pages, and some that I had in the past, I wouldn't have now, but it's always cool to see how I've changed by rereading an old favorite.
