"Good. Because it doesn't bother me either. Nobody owns Elsie, not me, not anybody. That's part of her charm."I can understand why so many Highsmith fans are disappointed with this. We go in with expectations of something dark and suspenseful, helped along by Highsmith's introduction of creepy Ralph, a middle-aged security guard-turned-stalker who takes it upon himself to 'protect' Elsie, a young coffee-bar waitress, from what is essentially modernity in mid-1980s New York.But that's not where the book goes. Instead, it's more a slice of urbane city life that is outspoken about the breakdown of conservative rules and which operates in the space of obsession, fantasy, neuroticism and desire. Reflecting Highsmith's own unconventional lifestyle, this gives us Jack and Natalia, a young married couple who have abandoned the values that reactionary Ralph wants to preserve: they share cooking and childcare duties; they both have arty jobs: he as a book illustrator, she in a trendy art gallery. Most of all, though, they have dispensed with old ideas of what a marriage and primary relationship should look like: Natalia's best friend is gay Louis with whom she is out till the early hours of the morning without any kickback from Jack. And when he realises she also has sexual relationships with women, he's accepting, even a bit excited (though not in a voyeuristic way, I felt) as it keeps the spark in their marriage alive.Into this heated situation comes Elsie: not yet twenty, beautiful, alive and as free-spirited as Natalia. Ralph is already paranoid about her morals and what New York will introduce her to: but her new-found friendship with Jack and Natalia sends his neuroticism and fantasising soaring. And they all fall a bit in love with her in their different ways.What is so refreshing about this book is Highsmith's lack of a moralising narrative and her no-judgement stance. If anything, this is an early tribute to polyamory and open relationships - no-one is jealous, no-one is possessive, Amelia is still being brought up as a well-adjusted child and, seemingly, everyone is happy. The only drawback is Ralph whose racism and misogyny punctuate the text, and a rather uncomfortable portrait of a 'butch' lesbian that marks this book's age.Alongside this sophisticated approach to erotic relationships is some urban unease: there's a Highsmithian coincidence that brings all these people together in a few local streets; and Ralph's pressure is heightened by the noise of overcrowded apartment blocks and his racist interactions with anyone Italian, Black or Jewish.That there is a crime, eventually, almost feels like an add-on. It also feels weird that at sophisticated parties people in 1986 are dancing to The Beatles and the Beach Boys! But, that aside (and I think this was written when Highsmith had essentially exiled herself from the US), I found this compulsive reading. It's not just Jack's wallet which is found in the street but also Elsie herself, a luminous figure who brings Ralph into the charmed circle of Jack and Natalia as much as the mislaid money. But, the intimation is by the end, even she is not irreplaceable - fantasy continues.
