My favorite of the Martha Quest Children of Violence series, maybe a benefit of a pentalogy - by the fourth the author isn't required to spend time on back story, etc. It is the end of WWII and Martha and Anton have found an equilibrium in their marriage. Martha is in love for the first time with Thomas Stern, a Polish Jewish refugee who is married.The back cover states that Martha is "determined to resist the erosion of her personality" (I love this line) - there is pressure from her mother to visit her dying father more often; there is pressure from Anton to "settle" or give in to the status as husband and wife; there is pressure from her friends to step in and intervene in Maisie's life; there is Martha's awareness that her youth is fleeting; there is the breakdown of the Communist Party which gave Martha's life purpose. The race relations in southern Africa have gone nowhere; they are no further than when they started (the futility of these political actions was explored inA Ripple from the Storm). I suppose at some level the reason that this book resonates has to do with the maturity that comes when one realizes that they are not special, that they are the same as everyone else, that life only holds the adventure one makes for oneself. In the past, Martha saw herself as taking a major step forward, making a decision that would change her life, open a door - this included reading books, getting married, having sex, motherhood, participating in Marxism, etc. (For Martha the best things in life were still to happen, which is rather sad.) In this book, that adventure is the love that Martha feels for Thomas, but she resolutely refuses to give in, to settle down, to resign herself - she is still striving for purpose.What is Martha striving against? In her lifelong antagonism with her mother and with members of the white society to which she belongs, she rages against conformity, bigotry, racism, the banality and repetition that seems to suffuse her life and the life of her friends/family.
