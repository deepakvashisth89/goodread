This was a "re-read" for me, but with the audiobook the second time around. Although the narrator was fine on the version I had, I don't think his voice fit the particular project.But listening to again reminded me just how much I loved this story. Multiple layers of love, sensation, and sensuality. Everything, living, dead, and even inanimate came alive. This story delves into and portrays masterfully the emotional mixture that can happen with love and hate embodied in and/or directed against, the same person. I was also, again struck by the writing that showed the inherent connection of one creature begetting another creature, but with the unique vampiric twist to it.And I was struck, yet again, by the theme of the inherent "wrongness," and arguably, evilness of "passivity."Cannot,cannot,CANNOTrecommend this one highly enough. Even if you don't like vamps or horror, this has GOT to be a classic in my humble opinion.UPDATE -- THIRD READING JANUARY 2024This is my third reading of this masterpiece. I've gotten a better appreciation of it each time. This most recent reading came courtesy of the (new at the time) Terror Tomes GR group. And for that I thank all the mods and participants for encouraging this go-around.By typical standards,Interview With the Vampireshouldn't work. It's a story within a story. It's largely recitational, rather than immersively narrative in a MC's life as they experience it. Auntie Anne eschews normal chapter breaks and instead organizes it into four very long sections. Some of the paragraphs are ginormous; on my Kindle there were several paragraphs spanned several pages. The world building isInterview With the Vampireisveryroundabout.But it does work.Or at least it does for me.The closest thing, as I think about it as a comparison, is how someone unfamiliar with chicken and waffles (bear with me here) might have reacted to seeing it on a menu in the 1990s (as an aside-it's amazing and if you haven't tried it, dude get on it!) and had the courage to try something new. Or a long-time skier (circa 1970, give or take) faced with one of them new-fangled snowboard thingies, who then tries to give it a whack.[Enter said person's epiphany wherein they realize the awesomeness of either thing here].In other words, something that seems to the uninitiated that shouldn't work, but does, AND their creator had the confidence to know somewhere deep down inside that it would work and that they were creating a delicious, now iconic dish or creating a new winter sport. I've got to think (though I obviously don't know for sure] that Auntie Anne had to have had some inkling that this wild novel she'd created had something special. (At least that is what I am telling myself. I hope that was the case.)I don't know. I'm rambling, I know. It's just one reader's thoughts.Anyway,Interview With the Vampirein my humble opinion, despite its structure, really works as a masterful story. It is immersive despite the "interview," reflective format told from Louis' perspective. It's immersive. I really do feel like the guy listening to his interview. A contemplative monster fighting his inherent nature and need to feed and kill.Uncle Stevie is an awesome storyteller, but somehow Auntie Anne did the sensory/sensuality/emotive elements a lot better than he has/had. Again, just one reader's opinion.I'm embarrased to say I haven't finished the Vampire Chronicles, but I will. I've made myself that promise. Maybe they aren't all as masterful. But one thing is for sure and that it's gonna be one helluva unique ride.
