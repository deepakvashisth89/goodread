Have you ever noticed that when it’s a male detective, they usually have a daughter, and when it’s a female detective, they have a son? I’ve always wondered about that dynamic, as it seems to pop up in almost every mystery I read.Trope observations aside, I have to admit I don't love mysteries where the crime is technically solved but nothing actually changes. What is the point then? Just to show that "that's life"? I get enough of that in real life—I want some justice!That’s especially true in this story. Poor Esmir was just a pawn while the ones in power stayed in power. I get the point the author is making: war doesn't just destroy buildings, it destroys the "normal" meaning of things. It makes corruption a survival strategy and rots institutions so deeply from the inside that the idea of justice becomes absurd. Solving one murder won’t change anything when the guilty are protected by power, the innocent are forgotten, and the system is too damaged to enforce consequences. Every character carries trauma, every choice is shaped by grief, and survival itself becomes a burden.I might sound a bit dramatic in my assessment, but that is exactly the heavy feeling this book invoked in me.
