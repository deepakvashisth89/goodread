The story of Main Street in Gopher Prairie, MN, USA is not, of course, only the story of one street in one town in one state in one nation. It is the tale of every small town in the country, every village in the world. The story is of Carol, but there are millions of Carols, millions of Sam Clarks. Main St comes to mean, especially later in the novel, not only a place but an attitude, a narrow mindset: selfish, solipsistic, crass, callous, low, base, simplistic, intolerant, ignorant, and inhumane. It is a scathing appraisal of small town humdrumedness.

Dr. Will Kennicott, Mrs. Vida Sherwin, Ms. Bogart, and the rest of the proletariat of GP are not evil, individually. Carol is not an overt heroine. She is incredibly naive and capricious. She is genuine, though, and has what some people refer to as a "good heart." Lewis doesn't spare her or the conventional townsfolk much scrutiny. Overall, the people of GP try to be helpful and kind. That's as long as the recipient of their benevolence is... rather like them.

Published in 1920, a century of events has dated Main Street, but the novel is good enough to enter the list of timeless stories.

When locals, about halfway through the work, begin whispering about a new boy they dub "Elizabeth" who wears fancy clothes and works as a tailor, I could not help but wonder if he might be gay. No, he is not even effeminate. He is merely "artsy." He likes poetry and reads many books, enjoys working with fabrics and sketching. But Main Street in Gopher Prairie is not too interested in art. Not unless it has a positive message for the youth. Pep, vigor, punch, good business sense, and work ethic are the qualities of a leader and venerable man or woman on Main Street. Industry over creativity.

When it comes to American literature, we hear plenty of Poe, Whitman, Hemingway, Steinbeck, and Faulkner. For whatever reason one has to dig just a bit to get to Sinclair Lewis, even though he was the first American to win the Nobel Prize for literature. I was first drawn to him when I came upon some insightful quotes of his. As all good literature is philosophical, and vice versa, Lewis saw the world through the eyes of an imaginative sage.

Anyone who has lived in a small town, whether born and bred or not, should completely comprehend the message. Though he uses detailed descriptions rather than straightforward polemics, and by today's standards his aspersions are subtle, Lewis is clear. There will always be those who hold back progress and cling to the familiar, those who cherish how it's always been done, whether that was the best way to do it or not. They are "Main Street." They gave the hemlock cocktail to Socrates. They burned heretics at the stake. Main St. held that the earth was the center of the universe when learned scientists were realizing, correctly, a heliocentric solar system in a vast galaxy floating about in a seemingly infinite universe.
