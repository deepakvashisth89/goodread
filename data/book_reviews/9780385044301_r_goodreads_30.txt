God's Bits of Wood is a book with many great individual scenes, it's thought-provoking, and it keeps you guessing as to both the fates of the characters and the outcome of the strike that is the main subject of the book. However, the book is not structured in a way that makes the most out of these virtues, in fact the structure undercuts much of what the book does best. With a small handful of changes, God's Bits of Wood could have been one of the best works I've read so far this year, but as it stands its many high points do not translate into a strong work overall.The book opens almost immediately with Senegalese railway workers declaring a strike, with the lives of the strikers and their families quickly thrown into chaos as the French colonists that run the railway retaliate with violence and by shutting off the community's water. While Ousmane Sembène's choice to start out the book with this pivotal strike vote certainly makes these first couple of chapters more exciting than they might have been otherwise, I think that overall the book would have been stronger if it had taken a chapter or two to introduce the characters & locations, and to give a description of what life was like before the strike. Without this introduction I found it difficult to keep track of certain individuals and the different key locales, but more than that I found it impossible to determine just how much life had gotten worse after the strike was declared (since I had no baseline for comparison).After beginning the book in medias res, Sembène wastes no time in introducing a high degree of philosophical and moral complexity. Niakoro, a village elder who witnessed a strike many years ago that was violently suppressed, predicts that this strike will likewise end in disaster, and she laments the fact that the youth of today did not even consult her, much less take her wisdom to heart. In many books, the perspective of this elder would be a prescient prediction, foreshadowing a tragic outcome for the strike at the heart of the book, but in God’s Bits of Wood Niakoro shortly thereafter receives pushback from another elder, Madadou Keita, who points out that the young know things that the elder does not, like how to operate the new machines brought by the colonists, and merely being older does not make one correct. Instead of a boring elders-know-best trope, Sembène leaves you guessing as to how the story will play out.The book’s depiction of machines is another example of its intellectual complexity. Some of the characters see these machines as merely a new class of overlords, demanding servitude from the native Senegalese, but the perspective of one of the main characters (and leaders of the strike) Bakayoko is that machines are mere tools, and more than that they are tools that do not care about skin color, making them a means by which they can get closer to racial equality. God’s Bits of Wood has no intellectually lazy division between things native to Senegal and things brought by the whites where the former is good and the latter is evil, instead it recognizes that even concepts brought by whites can be of value and better than current practices. One character realizes that they need to put a strike defector on trial (an idea he got from a French book) so that the people believe in the strike with their hearts and minds instead of merely complying out of fear of a beating, and this decision proves to be controversial but ultimately effective. Merely being native does not make something good, and merely being foreign does not make something bad.Not that the colonists in God’s Bits of Wood are all morally complex, indeed they are all straightforward villains with the exception of Leblanc. The colonists almost uniformly think of the natives as inferior, are quick to use violence without justification, and are crude and unwarrantedly proud. It is important to note, though, that they do not have a monopoly on villainous acts in the book. As the strike goes on and people get more desperate for food and water, the strikers begin committing acts of theft, blackmail, and destruction of property. In one particular scene a mother, desperate for water for her children, cheats a water carrier and eventually assaults him, an understandable act that is nonetheless reprehensible. The railroad company cheats its workers; a family of one of those workers in turn cheats a more vulnerable minority.It is scenes like the one of the family cheating the water carrier that are the most likely to stick with me after having finished this book, as this work is more about scenes than it is about a cohesive plot. Though the strike loosely ties everything together, it’s scenes like the trial, the death of Sounkare the watchman, the killing of the ram Vendredi, and the apprentices with their slingshots where the book is at its best, but frequently after such a scene is complete the story moves past it, and it doesn’t feel as if these occurrences have much of a lasting impact. With these many vignettes God’s Bits of Wood is almost more of an interconnected short story collection than it is a novel.As I hope I’ve made clear, this book has many strengths, with its well-written scenes and its consistent choice of the interestingly complex over the boring cliche. But, in addition to the absence of a solid introduction, the structure of God’s Bits of Wood also suffers from Sembène’s choice to jump around in time and location, splitting up many of the book’s scenes for no sufficiently good reason that I could determine. There is no reason why the scene of Vendredi the ram being killed and the aftermath of that event has to be broken up by the trial scene, the former scene could have been completed and then the latter scene could have started with no detriment to either one (indeed, it would have been an improvement). If the book had multiple distinct plot threads interwoven and converging into a finale, that would be one thing, but there are not so much separate plot threads in this book as there are individual isolated pieces. These pieces build toward a final resolution of the strike, but this does not end up being an exciting finale. Instead, the book’s ending is decidedly anticlimactic, with the book not even telling you the terms of the victorious strikers, and indeed the book’s final pages cover the death of what I’m generous as describing as a tertiary character. It’s a strange note on which to end the book, and not one of its best scenes.Perhaps a good encapsulation of the impression God’s Bits of Wood left me with is the aforementioned character Bakayoko, who is of central importance but who fails to actually make an appearance until well after the book is more than halfway over. Even in his absence, his shadow looms large, with some characters seeing him as a rival, while others see him as a savior waiting in the wings, who need only swoop down and solve the problems of the strikers. With all this buildup I was also looking forward to what Sembène would do with the character when (or if) he made an appearance, and what I got was the character delivering a decent speech before disappearing on a trip to Sudan that had no resolution, making me wonder why the character even showed up in the first place. There were some interesting things the narrative could have done with Bakayoko, whether the character ended up appearing or not, but in practice Bakayoko ended up being an underwhelming feature of the story, just like the book’s conclusion.This is a book with many strengths, and individual pieces that were very good, but that failed to coalesce into an above average work. If it had introduced its setting and characters a bit better, had told its many vignettes without unnecessary interruptions caused by the book’s structure, and concluded in a more satisfying manner then God’s Bits of Wood could have been something special, even if it was a book about standalone scenes more than it was a cohesive narrative. As it stands, though, even its high points and thought provoking complexity don’t succeed in elevating it. It’s good, but not great. I give it a 3/5.
