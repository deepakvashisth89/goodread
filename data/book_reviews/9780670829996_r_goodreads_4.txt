This novel revolves around the central character, Marcus Vallar, a brilliant innovative mathematician, painter, philosopher, healer, and spiritual guru. Or maybe he’s none of those things. Maybe the only reason he appears to be great is that greatness is attributed to him. Maybe he’s great because the people around him desperately want him to be great, to mean something to have insight into the world and to be the bearer of a message to the planet that rights wrongs, addresses injustices, and answers the unknowns.In some ways, this book is an extension of one of Murdoch’s favorite themes, the fallibility of ego-based views of the world, that invite us to see the world (and the people in it) as the objects of our desires and motivations. Whereas Murdoch often takes up this theme from the viewpoint of the egoist, the perspective in this novel is somewhat different in that our attention is centered on the object of that egoist vision, Marcus. Marcus is made aware of all the ways that people want to see him, and we come to realize that one of the dangers of being the self-aware object of such high expectations is that, as with Marcus, you can start to believe what others believe about you. As this reciprocal belief gratifies the egoists’ desires, it can produce a kind of megalomania. We can all probably think of historical figures in the arts, philosophy, politics, music, religion who have inhabited these kinds of transcendent roles given to them, to both good and bad effect. These kinds of figures are alluded to throughout the novel, some more directly than others, but it is not difficult to see reflections of people like Paul Gauguin, Andy Warhol, Rasputin, Glenn Gould, Wittgenstein, L. Ron Hubbard, etc., people who are inscrutable in their genius and their stature feels like a mixture of accident and intent but whose actions cannot help but feed the mystique.I most liked how Murdoch handled the people around Marcus, focusing on how they formed their projections of Marcus, why those projections mattered to them, and then letting the readers see how their actions and reactions myopically and subtly reinforced what they wanted to see in Marcus. Of course, Marcus never really fully delivers on any of those exalted expectations. Marcus, just like the rest of them is only human, and like all human beings “live[s] on top of a jumble, mess, chance, they understand nothing, they are surrounded by darkness, they’ve just got to keep walking” (42-43). Marcus’s own daughter, Irina sometimes seems to be the only one to see through it all: “You are encouraging him, you’re making him feel he’s a great man with a message and it’s do or die. Whereas really he’s a helpless solitary person with a thoroughly confused mind” (196). Another character observes “he’s a Sphinx without a secret” (301).A good novel, but not great one — many of the themes and oddities that I like about Murdoch’s novels are there, but this story never really seemed to come together either as a story or as a cohesive message. I wonder if Marcus ended up being a receptacle for too many examples of figures to which we attribute significance and meaning, and the result of this collision of character types and Marcus’s haplessness interpreted as detached wisdom are some enjoyably comical scenes but with an elusive message. Then again, maybe in a meta-way this is Murdoch denying the reader’s faith in the author as spokespeople of elusive universal truths. I like to think that maybe this is true.
