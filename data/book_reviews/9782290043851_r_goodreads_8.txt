Coelho’s books feel like traps. For me, the only way to truly appreciate his work is to take it apart piece by piece. You keep the parts that resonate and leave the rest behind. His prose is undeniably beautiful. It is also, at times, annoyingly preachy, and he often presents ideas as though they are absolute truths.

The book is about Pilar and her childhood friend who fall in love in their small town of Soria. Their relationship is cut short when he leaves to explore the world. Pilar eventually leaves as well to attend university. Eleven years later, after receiving a letter from her long-lost love, they reconnect at a conference.

. Pilar, our heroine and narrator, is a 29-year-old woman who feels emotionally stunted. She has followed the expected path: leaving her small town, going to university, building a stable life. She is not particularly adventurous and does not believe in God or any grand design. She questions, and sometimes even mocks, her childhood love’s faith.

In contrast, her childhood love left their town, traveled extensively, gained experience, and ultimately found religion. He joins the seminary, committing himself to a spiritual life.

Fundamentally, these two people have grown in completely different directions, like branches of the same tree stretching away from each other. In my opinion, they have no business being in love. Yet they reunite and spend several days together, wandering, reflecting, and engaging in long conversations about love and faith. One is eager to start over, while the other remains hesitant. I particularly appreciated the questions the book raises about love and forgiveness. Pilar’s internal struggles felt authentic. Is love only meaningful when it is possible? Can she find the courage to choose love regardless of where it might lead? Can she forgive her childhood friend for choosing religion over her? Can she forgive the pain she endured? Should she wait for love or attempt to forget it entirely?




Parts of this story resonated with me because it reminded me of my aunt, who once fell in love with a deeply religious man while she herself was not religious. I witnessed how agonizing it can be to feel something you cannot fully express or sustain. She constantly weighed the cost of love, questioning what she might have to sacrifice.

I am not opposed to religious themes in books. However, this was outside my zone of comfort. As I mentioned earlier, Coelho’s tone can feel overly didactic. I think even for a religious reader, his arguments may become uncomfortable rather quickly. Coelho favor one religion over another. In fact, he occasionally alludes to . It would be more accurate to say that he explores spirituality rather than religion. He questions profound ideas, but he also seems intent on supplying definitive answers through Pilar’s contemplations on faith. Coelho also critiques the patriarchal structure of religion (which I appreciate), emphasizing the feminine nature of love and faith.






I found parts of the book difficult to digest because it stirred memories of my own experience while studying in South Korea when I unknowingly befriended people who tried to lure me into a cult that believes in “God the mother”. I want to be clear that this was my personal misfortune, not a reflection of the country or its people. When I shared the experience with other friends, I learned that the group was a well-known cult known for recruiting and isolating people from their families and support systems.

I wasn’t sure how I was going to rate this until I reached the ending, which honored Pilar’s journey.

This is very much a book readers will either love or dislike, as it tends to inspire strong reactions. Unfortunately, it didn’t inspire any in me. Rather, it left me thoughtful, even if somewhat detached.
