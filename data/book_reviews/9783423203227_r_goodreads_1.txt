As a teenager and young adult (from about the age of twelve to twenty-five or so), I read the extremely popular and published by DTV (Deutscher Taschenbuchverlag) in multiple editions German language translation of Una Troy's 1955 novel We are Seven (and which is titled Wir sind sieben in German) as least ten times (and I also repeatedly discussed and debated both contents and themes with my mother, her younger sister as well as their mother). And all four of us (my mother, my aunt, my grandmother and I) have never found (and indeed, my grandmother was very staunchly Roman Catholic) Wir sind sieben as being in any manner morally, spiritually repugnant (or representing inappropriate, inherently offensive to Catholicism and to Christian dogma reading material) and thus textually dangerous, textually despicable and somehow as such supposedly celebrating debauchery, sexual deviance and sinfulness incarnate.

So indeed the three of us (and after encountering the mostly glowingly positive online reviews of probably also the vast majority of Germans who have read since its publication) we were hugely enjoying Troy's (and by extension of course equally so her translator Dorothea Gotfurt's) storytelling and totally, gleefully cheering for naively innocent, perhaps even a trifle childish but always delightfully personable Irish villager and single mother Bridget Monaghan, her seven illegitimate children (fathered by different village men) and how the self righteous villagers (including the diverse and unfaithful to their wives fathers as well as the local teachers, the local priest etc.) are gloriously and lastingly thwarted in their attempts regarding booting wonderfully unrepentant Bridget and her children (with each of Bridget Monaghan's children also totally physically resembling their respective fathers) out of the village (and that Troy also majorly exposes small-town gossiping and strife, religious and social hypocrisy, gender bias, double standards etc. etc., and that is therefore not just fun and humorously entertaining but is also a very pointedly critical, often bitingly satirical assessment and judgment of Irish Catholicism and Irish culture and mores, a bit dated perhaps for today, but Una Troy most definitely observes and delightfully as well as sweepingly criticises the Ireland of her time, the religiously rigorous and often pretty backwards and repressed if compared to much of Western Europe Ireland of the 1950s).
