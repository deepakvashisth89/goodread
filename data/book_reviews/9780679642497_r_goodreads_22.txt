What I loved about this history is its frame of reference. Today we take "companies" for granted, as apart of the background noise of our lives. But by providing a history of the concept and its incarnations, we see just how revolutionary "companies" have been. The authors use the term to refer to limited liability corporations that sell stocks or shares, revealing that some form of them goes back as far as the 1100s, but not really proliferating until several centuries later. Its power to enrich the middle class goes a long way in explaining why social class or socialism have never taken root in America. The books covers the Industrial Revolution, the rise of the managerial class, and why Britain fell so far behind by not embracing the institution (preferring family-led firms). I was fascinated how the German form included a social dimension, incorporating stakeholders besides merely management and stock holders--which also explains why the German economy remains robust despite losing two world wars. The authors play down the threat of multinationals since corporations are more rooted than most people imagine, and why it does not really make sense to compare corporate revenues to a country's GNP. I highly recommend this for history buffs looking for a new lens through which to see our times.
