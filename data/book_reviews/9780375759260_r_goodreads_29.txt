The book was written in 1860. This translation by GC Middlemore could have been written yesterday, so modern is its mode of expression and indeed its treatment of its subject matter. Above all, this is not a history so much as an attempt to enter the psychology and assumptions of those who lived at the time. It examines presuppositions, beliefs and identities of people who lived in Italy from the fourteenth to the sixteenth centuries, and as such it provides invaluable accompaniment to the understanding of events gleaned from elsewhere.

The Civilisation of the Renaissance in Italy by Jacob Burckhardt is no gazetteer of events. This is not the book for anyone wanting a chronicle of Renaissance history in Italy. For anyone already in possession of the basic historical facts, however, this book will provide insights into the mentality of people who at first sight might seem “modern” or “enlightened”, but who in fact still harboured ancient beliefs. The paintings of Hieronymus Bosch, full of demons, devils and visions of hell date from the fifteenth century. They are not flights of surreal fancy but deeply held beliefs about life, or damnation, after death. Now Bosch was not Italian, but to realise that these visions of hell were “real” at the time is a good introduction to this book.

Burckhardt describes the rulers of city states, dukedoms and ancestral holdings of Italy in this period as bastards competing with bastards. The almost incredible territorial instability of the period he ascribes to the fact that the rulers had no right to rule other than military power and yet, when an opportunity arose elsewhere, they felt that they had birthright claim over those lands as well. Hence the resulting free for all which led to interminable war, land-grabbing and, perhaps most important of all, alliance by marriage and thus by lineage. The fact that many of those marriages were both arranged and unhappy gave rise to new generations of bastards that only perpetuated the process.

The author analyses religious belief, witchcraft, superstition, alchemy as well as politics and inter-family rivalry. He draws on many sources, both from the Renaissance itself and from classical texts. Indeed a reader may be utterly surprised that there were so many authors in a period when we know so much about its visual art but so little about its literature.

But probably above all, a contemporary reader will probably assume that Renaissance “man” was a pious, God-fearing churchgoer. Burckhardt explodes this myth by showing that, far from being a guiding light, the Church and its doctrines were in fact often held in low esteem. The point was that the Church was powerful and if an individual denied it, that power could be used to destroy. Indeed, the status of the church and especially its clergy was not trusted. Burkhart writes: “She set up as absolute truth and by the most violent means, a doctrine which she had distorted to serve her own aggrandisement.” The mistrust also applied to the clergy: “The dead, they say, is none the wiser if an ape stands in a black dress in the pulpit, and beginning with a hoarse, whimpering mumble, passes little by little into a loud howling.”

What the author is at pains to do, also, is illustrate that “modern” rationality was very much alive by 1500 and that the Church did everything in its power to suppress it. We have to remember that the author was a Protestant and that he would have had little sympathy with the Roman Catholic position. The following quote from a Renaissance author illustrates:
“The same Guicciardini is of opinion that we are all in the dark as to all that is supernatural, that philosophers and theologians have nothing but nonsense to tell us about it, that miracles occur in every religion and prove the truth of none in particular, and that all of them may be explained as unknown phenomena of nature.”

But these may be only the reactions of the gentry, recognising that the power of the Church is a competitor. The author qualifies everything we know of the period in writing that: “The value to be attached to them is all the more qualified as they mostly touch on the life of the upper classes, with respect to which we are better informed in Italy than in any other country in Europe at that period.”

And these people were quite modern in their capability for snobbery: “Where there was so much care to show the honour to the contents of a book by the beauty of its outward form, it is intelligible that the sudden appearance of printed books was greeted at first with anything but favour.”

We do have to remember that when Burckhardt entitles a chapter “Women” that we are only talking about the female elite. The lives of ordinary people are not described here, possibly because their status was too often merely that of cannon fodder.

The Civilisation of the Renaissance in Italy by Jacob Burckhardt is a long book that would appeal to anyone with an already extensive knowledge of Renaissance history. It is not for the beginner.
