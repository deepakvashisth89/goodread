I was not familiar with this novel until it came up in one of my lists, not sure which. Having finally tackled it, I can say it was a pretty decent experience, though perhaps not transcendental.Alain-Fournier published Le Grand Meaulnes in 1913 and this is his one and only novel. The most likely reason for that is him dying the following year at 27, in the early weeks of World War I. The novel is narrated by François Seurel, a quiet boy who becomes captivated by the magnetic and restless Augustin Meaulnes, a new arrival at his school and a few years older. One day, Meaulnes runs away, gets lost and stumbles upon a mysterious fête at a remote estate. There, he falls hopelessly in love with the ethereal Yvonne de Galais. The rest of the novel is essentially his obsessive, and quite self-destructive quest to find her again. Also, he kept trying to recapture that original feeling of wonder he had at the party.There is something genuinely beautiful, and maybe dreamlike, about the atmosphere of the book. Fournier captures that bittersweet adolescent conviction that some moments of beauty are unrepeatable, and the pursuit of them can ruin a life. I understand why it became a classic. And yet, for long stretches, I found it quite boring. The plot wanders, scenes repeat themselves emotionally and I could not understand Meaulnes’ actions at times. The novel also leans heavily into the sentimental. The grand passion, the pure and untouchable beloved, the tragic misunderstandings. I got a whiff of melodrama more than once and you know how I feel about that. The plot is a bit over the top, and the ending has a convenient tragedy to it, as expected with French classics, which I usually love.It is a novel I appreciated more than I enjoyed. Still, it is worth reading for its atmosphere and descriptions, but perhaps it is not the masterpiece some claim it to be.The narration was decent, but maybe put me to sleep more than others. Or maybe it was the book, who knows.
