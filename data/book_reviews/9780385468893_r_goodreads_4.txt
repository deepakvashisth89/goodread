This is the fourth book I've read from Studs Terkel (after Giants of Jazz, American Dreams: Lost and Found, and the Great Divide). I continue to struggle with his project.As in his other works, this book is comprised of short interviews with all types of people, ranging from the average man on the street to the C-Suite CEOs at big companies. Most though not all of the interviewees are from the Chicago area, where Terkel lived. The book was published in 1990 and asks the interviewees for their thoughts on the current state of race relations in America at the end of the Reagan era.Again, as in his other books, the responses to his questions vary greatly. Some respondents are thoughtful and have unique experiences which give them insight; some are the literary equivalent of a guy on a barstool talking in your ear.These books have earned Terkel plenty of admirers but I confess I've had a hard time connecting with them. We have a collection of people saying things, but does the book itself have something to say? It's hard for me to parse what Terkel thinks I should be taking from this experience. Maybe that's the general danger of oral history projects but I feel like I could have just look at a poll conducted in 1990 about race relations that asked "are race relations better or worse than 10 years ago" and gotten the gist of it.I guess that's a little flippant. I think the more generous argument in favor of this that it offers the reader a snapshot in time of how people thought about race and gives them at least a little space to say why they have the opinion they do. It asks us to put ourselves in the minds of various types of people and see it as they do.And I try to appreciate that but quite obviously I am impatient with the exercise and a little dubious of the results. I'm starting to feel like I have given Terkel's books several chances and they may simply be not for me.
