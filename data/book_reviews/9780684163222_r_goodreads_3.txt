There is something about wolves that stirs something deep in me - an admiration not just for their strength but for the way they move through the world with quiet uncertainty. They are neither reckless nor hesitant, but guided by instinct and bonds that run deeper than what the eye can see. They belong, yet remain untamed and resilient against the relentless trials that shape their existence.

Barry Lopez’s Of Wolves and Men is a beautiful mix of natural history, cultural reflection, and storytelling. It’s not just a book about wolves—it’s a meditation on our deep, complicated relationship with the wild.

Lopez writes with clarity and deep respect, blending science, myth, and human nature in a way that is both insightful and moving. He reveals how wolves have been misunderstood, feared, and admired throughout history—ultimately urging readers to rethink not just the wolf, but ourselves. Most books on the history of wolves discuss their removal from various regions, often centering on Yellowstone National Park. However, few explore their deeper past and the intricate relationship between wolves and humans as thoroughly as Lopez does.



The book is rich in detail without ever feeling dry and it is evident Lopez thoroughly researched the topics presented. The writing is clear and consistent, whether describing wolf behavior in the field or ancient stories and fables passed down through generations. Lopez never lets the wolf become a symbol stripped of truth—he honors the wolf as the creature it is: intelligent, social, complex.

This is a book for anyone who loves nature writing, but it is also for anyone curious about the uneasy boundary between the human world and the wild. Lopez doesn’t give easy answers—he invites you to sit with the questions.

Powerful, elegant, and timeless. lingers long after the last page. This is a book I intend to revisit often.

I started reading in late December and made it through Book Three before I had to step away—the emotional weight of it was overwhelming. The cruelty, the hatred, the profound misunderstanding of the wolf—it sat heavy on my heart, too much to carry all at once. This wasn’t a shock or surprise as most books will touch on the subject of removal.

What’s especially heartbreaking and made me fumble with emotions is the cruelty Lopez writes about is not just history from beyond 40 years ago—the cruelty towards wolves still exists today. Just last year, in Wyoming, a cowardly man named Cody Robert’s ran down a young wolf with his snowmobile, taped its mouth shut, and paraded the injured animal around a bar before killing it. He was fined ONLY $250, because in much of Wyoming, wolves are considered “predatory animals” and aren’t protected by animal cruelty laws.

Knowing about the incident made me think even more deeply about the themes presented in Lopez’ book. It is a reminder that our relationship with the wild is still fraught with fear and misunderstanding — and that we have a long way to go.
