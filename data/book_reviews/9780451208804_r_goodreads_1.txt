As an interesting mystery set shortly after the death of George II,The Complaint of a Dovehas a rich historical flavor and a somewhat predictable, but entertaining mystery. The title comes from a sad ballad sung by a beautiful actress on stage, an actress with which one of the principals of the story falls, at least, in “calf love.”And, of course, such infatuation proves to be the young man’s undoing.Yet, the story really isn’t about the young man so much as about the young man’s tutor. Robert Fairfax is the scion of a once-distinguished judge who fell into disgrace, as did Fairfax. The elder Fairfax had his career attenuated by rumors surrounding a case where he should have recused himself; the younger Fairfax was disgraced in a duel as we eventually discover, but he is an intelligent and graceful gentleman who seems to have been bitten by circumstances and is making the best of what he has left. The misfortune which occurs to his young charge doesn’t help his future.I like several little phrases and ideas in the book. The young gentleman, Matthew Helmsley, critiques the way Members of Parliament are apparently “bought and sold” by saying, “Give and take, but who keeps their soul?” (p. 80) Fairfax at one point warns Matthew that London “…breeds gossip as a dung hill does maggots.” (p. 83) An older character once suggests that “Young cocks love no coops” and again, asks, “Where would lovers be without the quarreling? ‘Tis the wormwood in the purl, but it gives spice.” (p. 90). Since I also love interesting words, I was quite taken with the use of funambulist (p. 182) for a tightrope worker and abortifacient (p. 190) for an illegal mixture designed to induce, even as it sounds, abortions of unwanted pregnancies.As an instructor in the history of games, I enjoyed references to the popular gambling games of the era—particularlyFaro, a system I demonstrate in a lab period (with no money at risk!). A gambler losing at cards suggests at one point, “What say we switch to faro? The game’s pure chance, so I may lose more creditably by it.” (p. 88) A popular predecessor to the modern casino game ofCrapsis calledHazardand it plays a role in an ugly metaphor: “But gaming for life or death—now there’s a hazard for you!” (p. 113)One conversational phrase that I enjoyed in the book was included in a supposed reminiscence where Robert Fairfax remembered his days working on the firstDictionary of the English Languagewith Samuel Johnson. Understanding Fairfax’s frustration with his situation, the literary lion would say, “Not so glum, young Robbie, not so glum. Remember that though Alexander had conquered an empire at four-and-twenty, by four-and-thirty he was dead.” (p. 176)Another memorable phrase was captured in a musing belonging to Fairfax with regard to one of the suspects or vital witnesses in the case. “That this thief, opium-eater, extortioner, and trader in shady drugs should find the stage morally reprehensible bore out a theory of Fairfax’s, that nobody believed themselves to be without ethics.” (p. 243)Appropriately enough, one of the more interesting characters (and I hope we see more of her in future installments) says to Fairfax, “I hope you find your grail, Mr. Fairfax. But then—perhaps, it’s best if we all learn to live without it.” (p. 265) Such a sentiment proved a most satisfying bit of closure.
