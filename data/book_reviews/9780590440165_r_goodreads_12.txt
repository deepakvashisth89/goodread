A stranger clothed head to foot stumbles into an Inn seeking shelter and board. The owner of said Inn, Mrs Hall, notices that when he removes his hat and coat, he is shrouded in bandages. She assumes he has had an accident or maybe an operation and so mentions nothing of it. The stranger states he is an experimental investigator and his luggage will be arriving forthwith, and once in his room he should not be disturbed. It isn't long before the stranger goes on to create mischief and mayhem in the small village of Iping.I never expected this to be as comedic as it was; it was almost slapstick in places. I've only read two other books from the Author, one beingThe War of the Worlds, which I had mixed feelings about because I didn't like the protagonist. Here again, our main character, the invisible man himself, Griffin, wasn't particularly likeable either, but at least in this one Griffin wasn't meant to be pleasant, considering he's basically a diabolically deranged scientist.There are several interesting themes raised concerning egotism, scientific integrity, anonymity and its lack of accountability – all of which eventually lead to the moral and mental decline of Griffin. It was written in 1897, and as is the case with a lot of these classics, it does sound dated to its time; I also find they get so watered down in movies and other adaptations that the original tale, once you get around to it, bears scant resemblance to the lore you've come to know.The first part of the book was pretty good, but then it soon descends into a slow-paced crawl, and the narrative gets long-winded. It does pick up again as we draw to the final showdown, and there was a little surprise at the end in respect of Griffin's true self. The writing is fairly straightforward and not as overly florid as other classics I've read, and the concept of an invisible man is a terrific idea, but in parts it's monotonous, lacked substantive plot and had some pacing issues.
