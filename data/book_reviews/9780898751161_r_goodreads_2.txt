This is a great book for understanding the growth of socialist feeling in Russia between 1905 and 1917. It was published shortly after the revolution of 1905— a rising of workers and peasants against the autocracy of Tsar Nicholas II, one that set the stage for the revolution of 1917.

I think there is a tendency, at least in the US and western Europe, to simplify the formation of the Soviet Union into something of a stupid idea had by silly people: The Big Bad Bolsheviks promising gullible people true economic equality and instead committing atrocities. This ignores the genuine suffering of workers in the years prior to the Soviet Union; it does not consider why the soviets might genuinely be offering something people wanted. The Russian peasants were not . They were fed up with the pitiful lives they were living under the monarchy— and many who remembered the oppressive conditions of the late Russian Empire would continue to defend communist ideals long after it became clear it was not working.

's titular character is symbolic in a couple of ways. A poor, beaten wife who, along with her son, becomes a fierce soldier for the revolution— Pelageya Nilovna functions as a microcosm of the Russian peasantry as a whole. Her transformation from a passive victim of oppression into a committed revolutionary mirrors the political awakening of the Russian workers. Her role as a mother is also as figurative as it is literal. Gorky is saying: this is the birth of a movement.

That being said, I do think this story is at its most rousing at the beginning and end, with the middle being a bit of a slog. There's a large chunk of the novel that's dry and repetitive, with constant secret meetings, arrests, and regurgitating the same ideas we've already covered.

The ending largely redeems it, finishing on a very dramatic kind of note.
