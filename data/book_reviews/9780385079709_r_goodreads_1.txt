This book is very helpful for getting a reader familiarized with theory of mind that Freud formed, and which has influenced some aspects of how we think about the mind today, as well as with key terms associated with psychoanalysis (e.g., instinct, idea, repression, etc.) Wollheim is a clear writer, and he is sensitive to the fact that Freud's thought changed over his lifetime; the chapters are roughly organized in accordance to the chronology of Freud's main claims, revisions, and development of ideas. My only complaint about Wollheim's writing is that he could've given more examples to illustrate his definitions/analyses of Freudian concepts. On top of that, I have some general objections to psychoanalytic thought. Let me summarize some key ideas from this book and my responses below.

Freud's first theory of mind, before he posited the id, ego, and superego at all, involved positing a network of "neurones," or the physical units that compose psychology. Freud had a sketchy, mechanistic picture of energy flowing through these neurones. Some neurones are resistant to the energy, and others naturally are re-shaped by it; this sets the stage for Freud's later concepts of the unconscious and consciousness, where the unconscious is like this energy, and what's shapeable or not is somewhat determined by our desires, beliefs, and what we repress. Freud theorized that whenever the mind beholds a memory of a painful event, this memory triggers a rise in tension/energy, which needs to be discharged. If there is a strong wish in place, which activates memory of a desired object, this energy can invest this remembered object with a certain power, which mimics that of perceived objects. So this lets a mind mistake a memory for reality, and while satisfaction is expected, it cannot be fulfilled, in the absence of reality.

This sort of idea leads into a general complaint I have with psychoanalytic thought. I don't think we can actually confuse that which is imagined, remembered, or represented mentally through some other mental activity with that which is perceived. There is plenty of developmental psychological evidence for this. Young infants can tell the difference between what they imagine and what they perceive. Moreover, while it's truthful that when we're fearful or in some other negative state, this is usually accompanied by the impulse to overcome this state, and to enter something more pleasant — this is by no means an original insight. Sometimes I feel like Freud's posited mechanisms of the mind are just fantastical constructions to explain this basic insight; but they are superfluous and have little justification.

Another part of Freud's earlier thought is a distinction between "primary" and "secondary" processes. Primary processes involve instinctual energy/drives/wishes, which seek satisfaction, independently of our conscious control; secondary processes are volitional, in contrast. Freud's overall idea is that the wishes, of the primary processes, can be satisfied regardless of whether the means of satisfaction are merely imagined/fantasized, or whether they are actual, involving action in the world. These wishes are responsible for the unconscious creation of the "phantasy world," an inner realm of posited states of affairs that, if true, would actually satisfy these wishes.

I think there’s something legit about certain bare bodily affects needing expression, and this expression can be found in media based in the the real world or in make-believe (or something generally mental) alike (e.g., a tense state that makes us need to be relaxed, which can be satisfied by either simply taking deep breaths, or by changing the worldly situation that is causing this tension). But an alternative way to explain this, which is less sketchy than Freud's explanation (not requiring the postulation of particular mental structures that have no evidence) is that one has a desire that is general in character. The desire isn’t about particulars in the world, but is about something more amorphous, like just needing some physiological sensation to be altered, regardless of the means of achieving this. In this case, imagining v. perceiving something may equally do the job. The question remains open how imagining fantastical things can sometimes alter our bodily states as forcefully as perceptual experiences; this is something to think more about some other time.

A major concept found in Freud is that of repression. Freud thought that all mental disturbances result from our repressing certain desires. Through repression, these desires become unconscious, and influence our lives in unpredictable ways, e.g., driving us into behaviors which are incomprehensible from our conscious standpoint.

This leads to another major complaint I have with Freud's thought. It seems that often there aren't specific desires that are repressed. There are only general desires, which are general in the way I defined above, which seek satisfaction, and can be satisfied through very different means. Here's a way to visualize this: there are various options or paths, and encountering one over the other will have different practical consequences. There are many factors that lead us to one path rather than another. I wouldn’t consider this vulnerability or hindrance (as Freud considers repression to be) but it’s the source of our freedom and creativity. So it's not that the discharge process of an unconscious wish doesn’t pay attention to reality, but it’s rather that our experienced reality is amorphous and determinable by us, and there is commitment and value that shapes that, with the bottom up perceptual factors.

Various Freudian concepts are vulnerable to this line of criticism. Concepts like "substitution formation," "internalization," and psychosomatic symptoms (in the particular way Freud conceives of this) all rest of the assumption that there is a desire, for a specific or particular outcome, which is repressed, and so gets transformed or converted into something overt that is a hindrance to our wellbeing. But as I said, there is reason to think that many of our desires are general in character; it's not that there is something real that we've obscured from ourselves, but rather there is something that needs to be created in some form or another, and whatever we end up with is shaped by us and contingent circumstances of the moment.

Once Freud posited the concept of the unconscious in his later thought, he did so in connection with this concept of repression. So my criticism continues to Freud's notion of the unconscious. I wonder whether an alternative, more fruitful way of thinking about what the "unconscious" might really amount to is to keep nonhuman animal considerations in mind. Nonhuman animals have emotion. We may ask, what do they think about or see, which provokes their emotion? It's something that's not propositionally structured, as human thought is, and so is 'general' in character most likely; but it nevertheless has some positive form.

Obviously, we do sometimes have particular or determinate desires, which we repress, in the sense that we refuse to think about or attend to them. Freud thinks that when something is repressed, it gains ever more "energy," and so has power over us. But here's another way to explain this observation that determinate desires which we fail to attend to have particular power. Only the desires which we do engage with can have the chance of being assessed for their further presuppositions and implications, which can sometimes turn out to contradict with facts that we know to hold. This can reduce our motivation to pursue the desire. For example, if I first off the bat desire to fly by jumping off a boulder, I may then attend to the desire, and realize that if I jumped, I would not fly but would get hurt. This makes me lose the desire. But if we repress a desire, we don't get the chance to notice how it may interact with reality, and so we lose any opportunity to reduce the forcefulness of the desire, where this reduction would follow from seeing contradictions or undesirable implications that'd flow from the desire.

I'd recommend this book to anyone who wants to get familiarized with Freud's thought. I've also read Jonathan Lear's ; it was a while back since I read that (and during a time when I was reading a bunch of Charles Taylor and somehow developed a positively anti-Freud streak), however. I can't quite remember how it compares to Wollheim's. My best assessment is that both books serve as reliable introductions.
