Carl Jung = the neighborhood alcoholics after their third drink.

I make this comparison because, despite the complex topics he discusses, he doesn’t use unnecessarily complicated language—everything is precise and clear.

I expected the book to be more focused on psychology—after all, Jung is a psychologist—but he chooses to step beyond the boundaries of psychology and creates a deeply philosophical and social essay that raises the question of how individuality can survive in a mass society, under ideological subjugation and spiritual disorientation. This short yet idea-rich piece was written in 1957, but its themes are eerily relevant today.

Jung analyzes the crisis of modern man, trapped by ideologies, institutions, and collective thinking. He argues that the greatest threat to freedom doesn’t come solely from dictatorships, but from the individual’s inability to discover and accept themselves. Societies become dehumanized because people abandon their inner core in search of security in the crowd, in group identity, religion, or politics.

But here's something very important—he's not against religion or the state, as long as they are seen by individuals as noble purposes and not as daily routines blindly followed. He even believes that if a person is religious or concerned about their country, their chances of suffering from mental illness are lower. This, however, applies to any higher purpose—it could be in science, art, sports, family, and so on. I was actually surprised to find that my own views completely aligned with Jung’s—there’s even a chance I read excerpts from this book when I was younger, which may have shaped my current conclusions.

Jung’s central idea is that every person must take personal responsibility for themselves and discover their authenticity in order to truly exist as a moral, conscious being in the world. Without this effort, people easily become faceless cells in the social fabric—manipulable and dangerous.





To conclude: C.G. Jung’s text may sometimes come across as slightly anarchistic, but I think that’s because, when he wrote it, he was mainly drawing from examples of dictatorial regimes and didn’t have much exposure to countries with functioning democracies.
