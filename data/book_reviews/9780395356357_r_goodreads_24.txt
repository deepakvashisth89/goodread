After seeing Tolkien's essay "The Monsters and the Critics" cited repeatedly in Flieger's book "Splintered Light," I wanted to read the whole thing. The title essay of this book is an excellent essay about Beowulf, but also about literary criticism generally.The second essay, On Translating Beowulf, will probably hold less appeal to most readers, but since I read it while taking New Testament Greek, I found a lot of his comments on translation to be not just fascinating, but very helpful.Sir Gawain and the Green Knight is the third essay in this collection, and it's another very good one--I think almost necessary for understanding that poem, since the conflict between courtly courtesy and morality is not one many modern readers are familiar with, and I at least was confused by aspects of Sir Gawain the Green Knight because of that. Tolkien also just obviously loves the literature and languages he works with, and this comes through in his literary analysis.On Fairy Stories, perhaps Tolkien's most well-known essay, comes fourth--I confess I didn't read it in this book, because I already read it this year in Tree and Leaf! Still, it's an excellent one and I revisit it every couple of years.The three final essays (English and Welsh, A Secret Vice, and Valedictory Address) were less interesting to me but I still appreciated them:- English and Welsh discusses the two languages, aspects of their history, and ways that oppression has influenced how people view them.- A Secret Vice is about Tolkien's "secret vice" of creating languages. It highlighted for me that while I have enjoyed making languages in the past, mine were definitely for practical reasons (codes in letters to friends or to round out fictional worlds) and not artistic ones like they were for Tolkien (something I already knew--I have dabbled in languages, even for pleasure, but am so far from having the great delight in their sounds that Tolkien had).- Valedictory Address required a little more work to understand as there was background information I didn't have, but it touches on the relationship between language and literature and how they really cannot be separated into separate schools.All the essays are worth at least a cursory reading; The Monsters and the Critics, Sir Gawain and the Green Knight, and On Fairy Stories are worth deeper reading and having on the shelf for future reference.
