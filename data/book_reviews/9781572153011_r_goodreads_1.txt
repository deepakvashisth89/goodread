I learned about Ansel Adams when I first visited Yosemite National Park in central California in 1984. He had actually just died a few months earlier, but I was not familiar with him when that announcement came. Two years prior, a colleague of mine in Detroit shared her passion for photography with me, and I bought a Pentax SLR and began a hobby that I still very much enjoy, including submitting photos to county and state fairs. In Yosemite, my wife and I visited the Ansel Adams Studio, and I was struck by his work and started learning about this fascinating man. Since then, I've read several books about his photography. A print of Moonrise, Hernandez hung in my office and is now in our family room. My brother-in-law recently gave me this book.The book contains more than 80 of Adams' photographs; quite a few of them are lesser-known. The quality of the reproductions leaves something to be desired. Anyone who wants to pay for books that Adams himself was involved in the production of will get top-quality images. The upside to books like this is that they're available to the masses, many of whom will not notice the difference. Pritzker, according to the book sleeve, does not have an art background, so it is not surprising that the focus of the book is on Adams biography, and not on technical or artistic evaluations of his work. For those Adams fans who know his work well, the inclusion of lesser-known photos is a good thing. I learned quite a bit about influences that his early jobs and other photographers had on his outlook. When he was 14, his dad took him out of school for a year so that he could attend the Panama Pacific International Exposition every day, where he learned, among other things, about art, specifically Impressionism and Cubism. At 17, he was a custodian at the Sierra Club headquarters in Yosemite. When you read history like this, and see in retrospect where all of this was heading, you can see the work of God in his life. Adams said, "I knew my destiny when I first experienced Yosemite".Before I opened up the book, I jotted down what I thought Adams' contributions to photography and the world in general were. I'm sure others would mention several more.- Critical early contributions that helped the art world and the public accept photography as art.- Technical and conceptual advances in photography.- An intentional message about the value of our country's natural beauty.- Raising awareness of the threats to the sanctity of wilderness.- Personally teaching photography to thousands of people in his workshop classes in Yosemite.As I perused the selection of photos that Pritzker came up with, a few of the ones new to me were striking. Boaring River, Kings River Canyon has a nice geometric composition. The scene is dominated by a symmetric mountain forming a diamond shape in the top half. The dark mid ground is a V of rock in shadow, and the foreground is a stripe of brightly lit white flowers. Probably my favorite "new" photo in the book is Evening, McDonald Lake. Dark, almost black clouds form a horizontal slit through which we peer, as they are reflected in the foreground lake surface. Between the dark stripes is a band of light sky, also reflected on the lake. Dark mountains in the back create a central dark stripe. I love Lichens, Glacier National Park. Adams took an apparently mundane rock surface and turned its variations into a symphony of shapes. A roughly circular formation of white lichens surrounds smaller lichen patches, while regular diagonal fractures in the rock keep our eye moving back and forth. In Navaho Woman and Infant, Canyon de Chelly, we are treated to an Adams portrait. Blurred background rocks frame the central mother and child, whose faces glow in the bright sun. The vertical line between the mother's dark blouse and the baby's light wrap divides the composition in half.Pritzker makes a good point about Adams' preference for landscape photography that I hadn't considered - that "intimate studies do not easily lend themselves to emotional interpretations". He briefly discusses Adams' philosophical disagreement with colleague Minor White, whose works emphasized "the introspective and symbolic - Adams believed that art is a mystique and does not tolerate the dissections of cold critical analysis and aesthetic definitions". As a dabbler in art photography, I come down somewhere between these two ideas, and tellingly, my favorite works of Ansel Adams tend to be those with the simplest compositions and a more abstract feel.
