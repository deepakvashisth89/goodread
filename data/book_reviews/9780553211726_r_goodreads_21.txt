This is the only classic I’ve read that kept me consistently engaged from beginning to end. I know many would disagree, as its plot unfolds at a leisurely pace and is often described as “bland,” but I found its steady pacing and restraint surprisingly compelling.

I think one of the biggest misconceptions about this novel is that people tend to refer the monster as Frankenstein rather than the scientist himself—something I also used to believe when I was younger. Regardless, I find this novel surprising in many ways.

For one, I never knew reading through a monster’s perspective can be so illuminating, and at times pitiable. Its viewpoint of the world not only shows human cruelty and hatred, but also their kindness. I actually found myself sorry for the monster—especially at times when it is mustering the courage to face the humans it liked and admired, all to be rejected at the end. Its eventual journey through the woods, seeing the beauty of nature and the joyful, brief and luminous interactions of human beings kindled in it a desire for love and affection; for hope. And at times, an envy and a sorrow.

Earlier, —upon beholding his creation as a monstrous being, an abomination—rejects it. As a result, the monster wanders into the world utterly alone, endlessly yearning for acceptance. At one point, it is rejected again, not by this time, but by a family whom it loved fiercely. This moment stands as a pivotal turning point in its life, one that will eventually lead it to face its creator once more.

This quote, which I find extremely evocative, captures the creature’s appreciation for the life given to it with aching clarity. The constant rejection—by the people it cared for—wounded the creature deeply, yet its desire to live and its reverence for life made it resolute. At one point in my life, I felt the same way as the creature; despite encountering disappointment and bitterness, my desire to move forward remained unbroken.

And along its journey, the creature—capable of love and hatred—finds itself in a position where it can no longer love, and instead exacts its anguish upon those who have wronged it as an act of revenge.

This duality of its nature reveals that when it is constantly pushed into a corner—denied compassion, understanding, and belonging—its capacity for love is not extinguished, but violently transformed into rage. Like many people in this world, those who are continually underestimated, wronged, and mistreated may find their love turning into affliction—though never on a scale as colossal as the creature’s.

Beyond the creature’s moral decline, this novel offers numerous critiques as well. ’s obsession with knowledge drives him to commit unspeakable acts—actions that test and ultimately distort his moral compass, culminating in the catastrophic deaths of those he loves. This underscores the vulnerability, peril, and despair that can descent upon those who grapples with knowledge without restraint.

This reflects ’s final realisation of the consequences of his actions—how, by acquiring too much knowledge than his nature would allow, can lead to the collapse of one’s morality, and the destruction that follows.

On top of that, I find ’s writing exceptionally outstanding. If I had to choose, she would be my favorite writer of all time. Her prose—rich, gothic, elegant, and endlessly evocative—is among the finest I have ever encountered, weaving together beauty, horror, and profound insight with remarkable skill. Her ability to evoke profound emotion through her writing makes this novel both enlightening and meditative, inviting readers to ponder on the nature of ambition and human morality.

In the end, this remarkable story not only highlights the cost of ambition—and how one’s moral compass governs their actions—but also illustrates how humanity, by perceiving someone (or something) as other, can drive them to respond with malice and hostility. Even the creature, capable of love and compassion, is never given the chance of being treated right, to be included.

Ultimately, is a beautifully told story of the profound, aching longing to belong, and the despair that follows when one is repeatedly rejected and wounded. It is a story I find both enlightening and, at times, haunting. To me, it is a masterpiece—my favorite classic to date.
