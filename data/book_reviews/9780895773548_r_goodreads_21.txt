Cather:

The more I read your stories, the more I fall in love with your narrative. The first time I picked up one of your novels seems too far away, let's be honest, a quite short story called A Lost Lady. I remember reading one of the first chapters with my mother, being both of us very fond of birds, especially those that every morning come by the window at home and sing their mellow, pleasant melody; I remember looking at her in horror, with sadness in my eyes, as one of your characters, a hideous monster, was being cruel to a poor, little bird. I might have forgotten the rest of the story—except for the ending—but that scene, a scene that made me cry immediately afterward, is still (sadly) vividly in my mind. Let me tell you that you were too kind to that wicked, heartless boy. Once I finished that book, I knew I would keep visiting you now and then. Two other novellas were my next companions: one of them about an engineer that reminded me a lot of a guy I met in college: a sturdy fellow of few words with the broadest smile I have ever seen; no, it wasn't because of the traits of your character, or because his (your character) fate was the same as my classmate's, but because he, too, is a civil engineer who, in my view, engineers with passion, so to speak. The last time we talked—a very long time ago—he told me he was working on an enormous project, his first important work, or so he said; ironically for you, it was a bridge. I wonder if he knew I had a crush on him back in those days.

The other novella—did you truly believe I forgot about it?—, taught me a good lesson on love (relationships) and friendship. The former, I'll have to pass—you know me, it ain't for me. As for the latter, you taught me right: friendship is the most valuable treasure anyone can have, so when you finally find it, the last thing you want to do is let it go. You must cherish it, and nurture it, as true friendship is as palpable and healthy as it can be. A ubiquitous component of human life.

Let's move on to the thick of this letter. Before —we'll talk about it later—came . Of course, I don't mean the publication order, but rather my next step on this journey. Let's be real: it is your masterpiece, and please don't get me wrong, I genuinely believe the rest of your works will be as thought-provoking and incredibly written as , and yet I'm afraid to say that, experience-wise, there won't be anything like this (hopefully I am mistaken). Reading the story of an archbishop who had to take over his day-to-day responsibilities for decades was at the same time a journey to the inner life of a man who had to overcome too many changes and obstacles in his surroundings so that he could become a "whole person." Thus, when I was finished with the audiobook, I left my phone on the desk, ready to go to bed, and my great-grandfather was the first person that came to mind before doing so. To tell you the truth, I never met him; he passed away a long time ago—my grandpa turned 97 years old this April, can you believe it?—and the only things about him that came to my ears were told by my grandfather more than a decade ago. Surprisingly and, perhaps, mysteriously, my grandpa hasn't mentioned anything about his father to me again in all these years. He (his father) was a very tall man, almost 1.90 m, taller than anyone in my family that I am aware of, dark-haired, had a thick moustache, and a little scar under his lips. My grandpa never knew why his father got a scar in the first place, but he believed he got it because of a fight; 'he was a man of few friends, reserved yet impulsive, usually having trouble with someone else, despite being a very religious man. He made us go to church every Sunday, and regardless of the weather, regardless of me or my brothers being sick, we had to go to church. It was more likely than not that we, humans, would go extinct than missing a day of church—you can say that,' he chuckled. 'Was it nice, grandpa? Going to church, and overall your daily life, despite your father's stiffness?' I asked, a little bit afraid to bring back possible nostalgic memories in my grandfather's mind. He said his father was not a bad man, he was a working man, but he was by no means a loving father. 'He would spend his days working hard, and going to church not only on Sundays but during the week too, you know, when there was no need,' my grandpa laughed, 'but aside from that, we scarcely saw him at home. He barely talked to us, let alone taking care of us—that was my mother's work. We always had food on the table though, you can be sure of that. He was very strict, yet absent.'

One day he decided it was time to move. It was a very unexpected, sudden decision—there was a tiny place (it can't be called a town as there were less than thirty people already living there) where his father took his family and all their belongings with them. Apparently, there would be better opportunities for him there. My grandpa reminisced about that time, a slight smile appeared on his face: 'We had to walk six kilometers to go to school, around seven kilometers to go to church; there was absolutely nothing around us, just our little home, a small, rustic shelter where my two brothers, my parents, and I used to live, nature, nature, and more nature, and the land that my father was working. Then, as time passed, I vividly remember how new people started to come.' My grandfather said, slightly tired, partly because of his age—I thought—partly because of my inquiries. 'My father passed away shortly after that, if I remember right, he fell off a roof while working as a mason and never recovered from that fall entirely. He was always helping "his neighbor," as he used to call other people,' said he, holding his Bible that he took from his bookcase, as if willing to read it immediately after I leave, ‘man of God he was. A man who knew where to start a new life, for the sake of his family.'

Cather, as you can tell, your novel reminded me a little of my great-grandfather's story. I do believe when, for such-and-such circumstances, you must leave your place of origin and move to your next destination, at times unwillingly, stubbornly, and other times fearfully, is a sacrifice you must make for the sake of yourself, but most importantly, for the sake of others, usually your loved ones. Maybe my great-grandfather was not the best father according to his son; however, he looked after his family as much as he could, put food on the table and a roof over their heads so that they could have a decent life, despite it all. is about people making a living while trying to survive and prevail despite the adversities they are facing at the places where they go, but you already know that. I must finally thank you for all these years of enjoyment while reading your books; part of my experience has been about this, reading novels that have changed the way I perceive these little, yet meaningful events in some people's lives. I must confess I'm not only grateful but happy.

Yours sincerely, Axl Ramírez.
