This novel stretched my mind more than most. First was the format of the novel. The beginning of each chapter includes both a right-side up and an up-side down printing., as does the cover of the book. Furthermore, even numbered pages are upside down at the top of the page; odd numbered pages are right-side up at the bottom of the page. Even the dedication has a mirror upside-down image.Robin is a "piggy" as she refers to herself. You never know just how big she is, but the novel keeps track of the way she eats. There is a naivete to Robin that is difficult to understand. She has a certain bluntness because of things she doesn't know -- and they seem to all be related to sex. Robin is also engulfed in a world full of secrets, seemingly the parallel universe of liars -- and there are plenty of them. Her father, her next door neighbor, and her stepmother seem to be all involved in this parallel universe.Robin's best friend, Melissa, has moved to Alabama. They pledge to write each other, but Robin finds she has trouble writing -- she wants to match the beautiful verse Melissa uses, but she can't. Underneath the tree at Melissa's old house, where they used to meet, she meets the new inhabitants, a black family whose nephew is named Tri "like in Maple tree". Tri leads Robin on a new way of understanding the parallel universe of liars.I had a bit of trouble with this short but intense novel. I'm sure there are YA females who could totally relate to it, but as a librarian I wonder who I would actually recommend this book to read. Tri is a mixed child -- Robin sees him as "butterscotch" -- and there is whole issue about this issue. Tri claims to have a "meany" aunt, but is she really? I find Robin so sexually immature that this book truly becomes difficult to handle. I am just not sure about this novel which seems to try to be literary, but seems to fail in some measure.Recently re-read this novel (9/2013) and still feel pretty much the same. I also realize the ending is truly unsatisfying.
