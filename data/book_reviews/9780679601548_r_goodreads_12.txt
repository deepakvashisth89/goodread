Years ago when I first read James Baldwin's masterpiece of love, of sin, and the struggles between life and faith; I did not understand its themes of isolation and rage.I was an ignorant kid who thumbed his nose at this slim work of terrifying power, "he would not trace them on this new day of his life, were nourished by the wellspring of a despair not discovered" (Baldwin 216-17).I have read all of James Baldwin's fiction now, and I suppose it is fitting that it his the first book of his that I read that should be the last work of fiction of his that I end my Baldwin fictional journey.Baldwin's novel is about the tragic Grimes family. We begin in Harlem where the fearsome Gabriel Grimes resents his sensitive son John, and favors his fiery older son Royal.When Roy is stabbed in what is assumed to be a racial altercation between blacks and whites in New York during the 1930s-40s; Baldwin takes us backwards to the stories of how Gabriel Grimes because a preacher; of how he met his weak-willed wife Elizabeth; and they had two daughters, Sarah and Ruth as well. The life Gabriel led in the South is one filled of hypocrisy and sexual intensity.Gabriel has a first wife, Deborah, and has an affair with a sex worker, Esther, who ends up giving birth to Roy. Elizabeth is attached to a man named Richard, who eventually dies and John is born.John and Roy's lives will collide and become part of an uneasy blended family where husband and wife are constantly at odds with one another- with Elizabeth just trying to embody what being a good Christian is- and Gabriel trying to atone for his past sins being unfaithful to Deborah. Roy and John are caught up in the middle of this.Mr. Baldwin writes about class, sex, race, and repressed homosexual desire. John is a central character to the story, where his he is a dreamer and a lover of movies and white culture that lead to his guilt perhaps he might've been responsible for Roy's stabbing.This is Baldwin's most violent novel in terms of physical abuse, and one of terrifying power and empathy for his characters.Biblical allusions to Cain and Abel are apparent in this one, as it will be in "Tell Me How Long the Train's Been Gone" and "Just Above my Head", which I consider to be some of the most ambitious and savage novels I've ever read about love.I am glad I read this older, and with more appreciation.
