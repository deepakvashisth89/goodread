2.5 stars

I was finally convinced to read this book by Rebecca Romney in Jane Austen's Bookshelf, which is a fascinating look at the women writers Austen admired, their works, and what causes a book to become a classic. In Romney’s opinion, Frances Burney, who was widely read and admired during her lifetime, fell out of fashion largely because her writing was “too similar” to Austen’s for male taste-makers, who pitted female authors against each other in a zero-sum game in which only the best one was worth reading at all. Romney also opines that Burney had her own strengths Austen doesn’t share (such as an authentic portrayal of sexual threat, and a willingness to write about violence), and that while Burney’s work might not be quite as good as Pride & Prejudice, it’s better than some of Austen’s other books.

So it was interesting to read the book in that context (as well as the context of my having read a lot of 18th century history, but not 18th century novels). To an extent I agree. I would hesitate to recommend this book, but also I’d personally only recommend about half of Austen, and if you like Mansfield Park you should definitely try this. This book does encompass a somewhat wider sphere than Austen’s, and with more of a sense of threat to the protagonist. On the other hand, the epistolary format is clunky, it’s long and does not need to be (and no, it was not serialized, though you’d be forgiven for suspecting), and the amount of personality displayed by any character is inversely proportional to the degree of sympathy they are supposed to merit—i.e., everyone we are supposed to like (there are about half a dozen of them, including Evelina, her love interest, her guardian and a few friends) is an absolute cypher. But it’s also interesting as a historical document and perhaps that is emblematic of a era when there was only one “correct” way to be, when being too “singular” was frowned upon.

It's a bit difficult to describe the plot, because on the one hand it is a marriage plot: Evelina is a beautiful teenage girl raised in the country, visiting the city for the first time and being absolutely beset by suitors, most of them unwelcome and extremely pushy (except the love interest, of course). But most of its pages aren’t devoted to that, but to various social engagements which often devolve into long arguments amongst secondary characters. Much of this is repetitive, does not affect the plot, and honestly this book could probably be half the length and lose very little—but on the other hand, the secondary characters are mostly free from the stifling strictures of “likeability,” which means they get to have personalities and are often entertaining and sometimes even funny, albeit drawn rather broadly.

(Perhaps the best moment is when a group of wealthy young men, frustrated by the sharp wit of Mrs. Selwyn, a learned family friend of Evelina’s, declare that women should be beautiful, good-natured and nothing else. Mrs. Selwyn’s response, paraphrased: “They do say no man should marry a woman smarter than he is. But where will we find ladies dumb enough for you?” Meanwhile it feels like half the book consists of another family friend, a sea captain, playing often cruel practical jokes and railing against the French—most of this is directed at Evelina’s French grandmother, who although not very bright, is always ready to argue back. I have to say I had more sympathy for the grandmother than I think we were supposed to—first because her speech patterns are charming, and second because from the ages provided she would have given birth at about 13, only to soon lose her several-years-older husband and then have her baby taken from her. No wonder she’s a mess.)

But then, the deep structure of the book isn’t about the romance, either, but centered on Evelina’s complicated family situation. She has been raised by a family friend after her mother’s death in childbirth, and gone unrecognized by her biological father—which because he’s wealthy and she’s of marriageable age, is a situation her friends now want to rectify. This plotline takes awhile to get going and goes in some hilariously absurd directions (I laughed aloud at one soap operatic turn, albeit with great eagerness to see where it went). But the parental drama ultimately provides the climax and the most emotionally intense scenes, albeit ones that are a bit difficult to relate to today. As it turns out, “filial piety” was a term in use in the 18th century—to describe the values of English society, not foreign ones—and Evelina displays it in full toward both her adoptive and biological fathers. The extremes of emotion and everyone’s manner of expressing it feels very over-the-top today, but it’s culturally interesting. And from , these types of relationships were not confined entirely to fiction!

Another cultural tidbit that caught my attention, albeit one of far less consequence, is when Evelina and her friends attend a fireworks show that “told, with wonderful ingenuity, the story of Orpheus and Eurydice”—what? How? I am fascinated by the idea of telling a story with fireworks, apparently an art we have lost in the intervening centuries.

Speaking of storytelling methods, the novel was still finding its feet when Burney wrote this one—which on the one hand, makes the fact that it did entertain me quite impressive. For all that I often struggled to find anything good to say about it, those pages turned fast, and the deeper I got into it the more true this became. But the epistolary form perhaps evidenced a view at the time that a text needed to explain its existence, and it does the novel few favors. When Burney takes it seriously (in letters not written by Evelina), it’s tedious, as people exchange elaborate compliments and exhortations to good behavior. When she’s trying to entertain and tell a story (in Evelina’s letters, which make up the bulk of the text), she succeeds, but by throwing away any semblance of believability as letters—a tradition, of course, followed by almost every writer of epistolary novels ever since.

In the end, I suspect you have to have a deep love of early English novels, or interest in the time period, to truly enjoy this. That said, most of its weaknesses are likely attributable to the era rather than the writer. And despite being nearly 250 years old, it entertain me, as well as being an interesting view into the mind of another era.
