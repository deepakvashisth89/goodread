Over the past few years, I’ve been trying to revisit (or in this case, read for the first time), the classic dystopian novels of the early 20th Century. 1984, Animal Farm, Fahrenheit 451. Now, having just read Becoming Madam Secretary, in which Sinclair Lewis has a big role, I decided to pick up It Can’t Happen Here. Unlike the others, ICHH is much more satirical. Or I should say, it starts that way. Jokes are layered one on top of the other. But underneath the humor is a serious message. And the story gets darker and more serious as it goes along.
The book was written in 1935, during the Great Depression but before WWII. It’s a reminder that at various times, fascism holds an appeal for large swaths of people. There’s even a group in this book called the League of Forgotten Men. What’s scary is that at times, this didn’t read like satire so much as a blueprint for the second Trump administration. The candidate starts off as a populist, making conflicting promises to those on opposite sides of issues. He espouses racism, anti-Semitism, misogyny. He promises free money to every citizen. His plans are to grow the military, take over the money supply, only allow Congress to follow the orders of the President and make it so that the President can’t be held accountable by the Supreme Court. I mean, doesn’t the following paragraph seem eerily familiar?

Summarized, the letter explained that he was all against the banks but all for the bankers-except the Jewish bankers who were to be driven out of finance oentirely; that he had thoroughly tested (but unspecified) plans to make all wages very high and the prices of everything produced by these same highly paid workers very low; that he was 100 per cent for Labor, but 100 per cent against all strikes; and that he was in favor of the United States so arming itself, so preparing to produce its own coffee, sugar, perfumes, tweeds, and nickel instead of importing them, that it could defy the World...and maybe, if that World was so impertinent as to defy American in turn, Buzz hinted, he might have to take it over and run it properly.”

Or this:
But after eighteen months of Presidency he was angry that Mexico and Canada and South America (obviously his own property, by manifest destiny) should curtly answer his curt diplomatic notes and show no helpfulness about becoming part of his inevitable empire.

After Windrip is sworn into office, he proceeds to do exactly what he wants. Colleges are closed down and reworked “free from the very taint of intellectualism”, newspapers and other media outlets are taken over. A private army (the Minute Men) begin to arrest people and kill those who get in their way. And needless to say, the promises made to the everyday Americans are never fulfilled. Those who try to fight back tend to end up in concentration camps or dead. As time goes on, even the loyal can find themselves on the outs and removed.
I enjoyed the book, even if it does go on and meander more than necessary. It’s like Lewis wanted to cram in every thought on the subject and a stricter editor could have pared this back. The book is much more focused on ideas than character development. But one thing at the end struck me as something I’ve often thought. A lack of a strong education system that actually teaches people to think is at the heart of the ability of dictator wannabes to rise up.
I listened to this and Grover Gardner was the perfect narrator.
