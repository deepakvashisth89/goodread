Negative appraisals of this novel tend to focus on the abundance of description. Normally I'd agree, since I'm the kind of guy who tends to regard the construction "lifeless adjective" as a redundancy. But here all of those adjectives constitute no less than Maney's naughty parody of '60s culture -- if one can call it that -- and advertising, which I imagine is the apex of that culture anyway.So one is left with a mystery novel that might be a bit challenging for poolside reading (what, *two* Betty-Sandy pairs?), and one might wonder whether too much fuss is being made over a "murder" for which there's no corpse. But otherwise the narrative is fiendishly well manipulated, which leaves me with the complaint, here as in the previous novel, that Maney still hasn't done her martini homework. Granted, this time there's no talk of (egads!) vodka, but putting a swizzle stick into a martini -- which one would never do anyway -- does not constitute "stirring" (as opposed to "shaking") as part of the drink's preparation.So all in all a well constructed romp. This time the publisher has contributed (I use that word sarcastically) a cover with crystal-clear images and typeface, but the typeface of the text is ever so slightly blurry. A few typos/syntactic train wrecks, mostly toward the end; I imagine Maney had to act as her own copy editor. Can't win for losing! But then, I suppose HarperCollins regarded Maney as a kind of orphan in a basket left by destitute Mama Cleis on the corporate doorstep: I bought my copy just a few months ago, brand new, from a major online vendor, and was delivered a first edition. Is this aging faggot seriously the only member of the Mabel Maney Fan Club? . . .
