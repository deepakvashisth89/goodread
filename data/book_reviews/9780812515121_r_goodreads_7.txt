Normally I just wait to add any book read after the cute lil GR infographic comes out to the next year's count, but yikes. This book can be in the yikes pile of " Kinda want to forget this book happened."I DNF'ed the first story, was made thoroughly uncomfortable by the second--not in the way it wanted--and was mostly chill with the third.I might go into more detail later, but I think I'll go drown myself in Christmas cookies and write a thoughtful review later.****LATER HAS ARRIVED*************There are three types of disturbing. Viscerally disturbing material (Watching someone break a bone), Mood-disturbing material, where the silent horror just sticks with you for days (The Lottery), and what I like to call "Cupcakes" horror, where the material is gross/horrifying, but the REAL disturbance is that not only did the author think of this, they put it on paper, made an effort to get it published, and no editor stepped in to say, "Please Stop."Normally, you don't see that third category in published material, because not only does it transcend good taste, it goes beyond making the point, or trying to scare someone. It often careens right into gratuitous, to the point where the impact the story was probably trying to make is buried beneath the excessive nature of the writing. My first encounter with this was an infamous Creepypasta called "Cupcakes", where the characters from My Little Pony turned canabalistic and bake each ot...I'll stop. You get it.The second novella in this collection contains the rare example of published material in that third category.It's hard to talk about the other two with any amount of real criticism. The first story--Anderson's--I DNF'ed. The style wasn't for me, and I just couldn't find any footholds to let myself dig in. It was a decent concept though, and I can see why people who enjoy a more offish style of writing would love it. The third--De Lint's--was, predictably, my favorite of the collection, and blended two separate horror tropes well. My main criticism was that there is a scene towards the end that was yet another classic example of the aforementioned third category of Cupcake Horror. It could have easily been cut, or simply references to, and the impact on the story would have been the same.But back to the second.It deals with a hard topic, and presents a villain that does the sort of thing that causes the words "vigilante justice" to come to mind. And the vigilante justice that is dealt seems fitting. And it would have been a horrific story, if, just of, the writer would have exercised even a little bit of restraint, and let some things be assumed, or just written the first scene in a sequence then faded to black.But no.Really, this is the kind of collection that is the poster child for "Needs an Editor". All the authors clearly have some talent, they just need to be curbed, whether it's in word count (Anderson), a scene here or there (De Lint), or in literally everything. (Garton).
