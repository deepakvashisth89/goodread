It's not a stretch to say that James Dyson completely revolutionized vacuum cleaners. Before Dyson, Hoover dominated the market with their bagged vacuums, which Dyson found to be totally ineffective. He then spent over a decade designing a vacuum cleaner from first principles, which led him to the awesome dual cyclone design that is miles better than anything that existed before. It's an inspiring story of a determined inventor triumphing over an outdated, bloated monopoly.In this book, Dyson tells that story in self-deprecating British prose. It's a great story, and one that I would certainly recommend, but a major issue I had is how much time Dyson spends railing against a lack of innovation and risk-taking in the modern day UK. He probably spends over a quarter of the book going on and on about it, which gets old really fast. He also has a remarkably consistent ability to enter partnerships with organizations (vendors, manufacturers, marketing agencies), and somehow the partnership turns horribly sour and he ends up in a lawsuit and has to spend a ton of time fighting that organization tooth and nail. I understand that he's fighting in the face of a massive industry, but at a certain point you have to wonder if Dyson is bringing on these problems for himself. After reading about these situations over and over, it gets annoying. That being said, on the whole this is a fun read.An interesting wrinkle is that this book is it's almost impossible to find in 2025. It was a bestseller back in 1997, but in recent years it seems like Dyson spent some time making sure this book couldn't be bought. If I had to guess, it's probably because his chapters on Japan are a little bit...outdated? I get where he's coming from in those chapters, and I don't think he means to be insensitive or anything, but I can also see why the CEO of a massive company might not want to promote those viewpoints in this day and age.
