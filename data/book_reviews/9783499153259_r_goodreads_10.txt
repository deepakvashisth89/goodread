The Turning Point by German writer Klaus Mann is part autobiography, part history. The book spans the first four decades of the twentieth century: from Klaus Manns birth in 1906 up to 1946. During this time, Klaus, son of famous writer Thomas Mann, finds his way in life as a writer, traveling Europa and America, and after the Nazi takeover in 1933 flees to Zürich, Amsterdam, Paris, and finally ends up in the United States. In the final years of his life, he is completely taken over by the worldwide fascist threat and even joins the US Army in 1943 to fight in Europe. The book ends, rather abruptly, in the final months of 1945.

I picked up this book because I was under the impression that The Turning Point offered a unique insight into the interesting times of the 10s, 20s, 30s and 40s. In this, the book disappoints. It is much more a literary sketch of a young, restless and gloomy man seeking his way and station in life, never really finding happiness. Reading this book, one gets the impression Klaus is always on the move, always reacting to situations and people, never really embracing any agency in life. He becomes a professional writer (like his father Thomas), he travels with his sister Erika, he responds to crises (both personal and political), but he never really settles or makes his own decisions. Through the lines of The Turning Point, one glimpses a fundamentally unhappy and restless man, always on the run from life.

In the final chapter, Klaus Mann describes how he, while waiting for a formal invitation to join the US Army, finds himself in a very depressive mood and for days on is obsessed with the thought of ending his life. Somewhat later, in real life (a couple of years after the events in The Turning Point end), he would finally end his life. This book, thus, is the story of a tragic man who tried to wrestle with the tough world he lived in, but in the end succumbed to it.

For the historically interested reader: throughout the book Klaus Mann reports on political events, like World War 1, the inflation crisis, the Great Depression, the rise of fascism, America in the 20s and 30s, etc. It is very enlightening to view these infamous events through the eyes of an eye witness - one who could write eloquently about it.

In all, I found the book rather dull and long-winding. Certain parts are very breath-taking, while huge parts of the book are just boring descriptions of meetings with famous people, writing articles, etc. In the end, I can't really recommend reading The Turning Point.
