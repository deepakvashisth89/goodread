4.5 stars.I can't in good conscience review this book without first stating some very important qualifications (the actual review starts in the next paragraph). This book is NOT meant to provide therapeutic-type "understanding" to the acutely suicidal. If you are severely depressed or seriously suicidal, DO NOT read this book. If you know someone who is depressed or suicidal, DO NOT give them this book to read (though it may very well be helpful for you to read as a friend or caregiver). The frankness of its description of the suicidal mind is more likely to make depressive symptoms worse than otherwise, and while it doesn't romanticize suicide or provide a how-to manual in any sort of way, it would definitely put ideas into a suicidal person's mind that they would be better off not thinking about now. Those who have suffered depression, other mental illnesses, or suicidal episodes in the past may find a lot of value here (the author herself has bipolar disorder and has previously been suicidal, including attempting suicide). But they should exercise caution in reading it. Don't stick with it if it's making you feel worse.My interest in suicide is both academic (I study psychology, and while it's not my research area, I find mental illness interesting) and personal (a good friend of mine killed himself when I was in college). Still, I was hesitant to commit to reading a whole long book about the subject. But Jamison's book came highly recommended, and I had also been impressed by the scholarly articles of hers I'd read.NIGHT FALLS FAST is excellently written, but certainly not for the faint of heart. Jamison doesn't shy away at all from describing the despair felt by the suicidal person or the tragedy of suicide, and sometimes (though not overly often) includes grisly details. As a result, some sections, especially those containing personal stories, are harrowing and very difficult to read. The book's unflinching portrayal of the darkness of suicide is even more impressive given Jamison's own psychiatric history (she has bipolar disorder and has made at least one serious--near-fatal--suicide attempt). I have immense respect for her for turning her own personal struggles into an area of research and discussing them so matter-of-factly, but I imagine this probably made parts of writing this book extremely difficult. She is obviously very passionate about the subject, and about suicide prevention in particular. Her outrage that suicide is not more in the public consciousness and that more is not being done to prevent it (among other things, she takes issue with gun availability, some aspects of the psychiatric hospitalization process, media glorification of suicide, and "suicide awareness" programs that downplay the link between suicide and mental illness) is evident throughout the book.The book's account of the then-current (it was published in 1999) science of suicide is impressively balanced and thorough, drawing from research on biological psychology, psychopharmacology, neuroscience, clinical psychiatry and psychology, and public health. The tone is not dry, but the reliance on statistics (while simultaneously not explaining important statistical concepts that come up multiple times) may be confusing for lay readers. I have also heard from some non-scientist readers that they found it frustrating that the book presents so many unanswered questions and unresolved debates, seeming not to want to "take a side." As a psychologist, I understand that this is because many (if not most) of the important scientific questions about the diagnosis and treatment of mental illness and care of the suicidal are still unanswered and thus accurately reflects the state of the science (and to a certain extent the sceience of medicine as a whole) but I recognize that non-scientists might find it off-putting.Reading from a "someone I know committed suicide" perspective, while the chapter directly addressing issues faced by those left behind by suicide is relatively short, there is a fair amount of helpful (it helped me, anyway) information and insight in here, if one can stomach the subject matter as a whole (which is not a given--I know I wouldn't have been able to even a year or so ago). The book addresses many of the worries and "myths" about suicide that seem to most plague those left behind, such as that a single precipitating event caused the suicide or a single action on someone's part could have prevented it (at least in the long term), that most people who commit suicide are angry at loved ones or friends, that someone who's life is "going so well" wouldn't commit suicide, and similar, and backs them up with data and statistics. Some may find this information comforting. Personally, I even found learning that most people who commit suicide don't leave notes to be helpful. What the book makes clear again and again is that people who commit suicide don't address (at least not in the same way) the "rational" considerations about life prospects, affect on loved ones, and so on that so preoccupy those they leave behind. This is precisely because it is in the nature of their mental illness that they cannot do so. Though some may find it difficult to hear, I suspect others will find somewhat of a relief in this book's repeated because the emotional and mental state caused by their illness means they are incapable of doing so. Given that our society and the media emphasize individual events when discussing precipitants of suicide and thus the public perception leans this way, I think the emphasis on the sheer weight of "he / she was very sick" against any other considerations is helpful.
