This is a hard slug of a read. Osborn crams a lot of words in those 271 pages of his.Robert's début concert at the Wigmore Hall and the few hours following it serve as a coda to the whole book but most of it really is taken up by flashbacks from the lifes of a small cast of characters (Robert, his parents, his best friend, Flavia, his lover, Johnny, and an acquaintance, Daniel) and their thoughts.We follow those, and some of their actions, as they discuss desire and love with themselves but not with each other. In the end, despite, I think, an attempt at a positive and hopeful ending, and in contradiction to the title of the book, the characters are unable to truly "touch" or to communicate effectively. They altimately remain separate, even when they seem in fact to be yearning for the same thing.Osborn, being a pianist himself, has opted to structure his book like a symphony, dividing it in four movements and interweaving the thoughts of the characters like musical sentences and motifs, making them take over from each other, come to the fore or using as counterpoints to each other, while telling a very loose and rather thin story.There is one single short quote at the back of the book, by Iris Murdoch, who deems it "a very promising first novel", but, to me, this is more a case of a writer showing and thinking he is making himself look very clever indeed.While one's inner dialogue can sometimes take a more whimsical turn, it's fair to say that things remains generally pretty humdrum. Here, it is as if the characters have not lived before and therefore suddenly get all sorts of extraordinary thoughts as they cram themselves together into their heads in one go. A lot of those thoughts are paradoxes, and remarks and circumstances not grounded in reality. It seems, they are often devoid of internal sense or logic, for ever jumping from one fantastical notion to its opposite from one moment to the next, like weathervanes in a storm. It is as if Osborn can't make his points by using ordinary circumstances and has to make use of improbable ones instead.I had an issue with the clumsy treatment of homosexuality (a central element of the book): the cod psychology (possessive mother, absent father, etc), the variying levels of internalised homophobia of all the gay characters could easily have been omitted, or at least be dealt with more sensibly.All this hightened internal life we are subjected gets old very fast. It contributes to making the book feel veryl unreal,. Although perhaps this is another attempt to model it on a piece of music: it imitates life and emotions but it really is not it.It is perhaps a blessing that Osborn has not found it within himself to write another novel.
