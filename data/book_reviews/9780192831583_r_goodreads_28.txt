There were twelve books in the series, but only four and a section of the fifth book survive.

The good news is that Tacitus (c. 60 AD--125) was an engaging writer, and one feels his personality through the writing. He's opinionated and exercises a lot of critical judgment. His observations are confident, and he doesn't get bogged down in tangents. He may not always be right (I'm still learning about other sources of this time), but he is always entertaining.

He was also an insider: a Roman Senator with access to the official Roman records, and he likely personally knew VIPs from that era, those with firsthand memories of the chaos of the civil wars and Batavian and Germanic uprisings. There are a lot of footnotes in this edition, but the translation in the Penguin Classics Edition (by Kenneth Welesley) is one I found readable and engaging.

We get an insight into three of the "short timer" emperors of the year we now denote as 69 AD (Galba, Otho, and Vitellius). One of my favorite observations concerns Galba, a general who only lasted a few months but was overthrown and assassinated. Tacitus tells us, "He seemed too great to be a subject so long as he was subject, and all would have agreed that he was equal to the imperial office if he had never held it."

In this history, assassinations and executions are all too often employed. It's a bloody time and not one where mercy could be expected if one picked the wrong side. Given the myriad military leaders who challenged any new Roman emperor, each with a powerful number of hardened Roman legions behind them, it really is sobering to see how weak and chaotic a vast empire becomes when the established power at the top is disrupted.
