John Rawls’ A Theory of Justice is still treated as one of the defining works of political philosophy in the 20th century. It’s often praised for offering a “more humane” form of liberalism that tries to square individual liberty with fairness, while avoiding the extremes of both state socialism and laissez-faire capitalism. Rawls wants to show that a just society can be built on liberal foundations, and that it’s possible to create rules of fairness without getting stuck in ideological battles.Rawls’ famous thought experiment centres his theory: the original position. From behind a veil of ignorance, people design the rules of society without knowing their own position in it, like what race, gender, class, abilities, or talents they’ll have. The idea is that by removing personal bias, you’d create a system that’s fair to everyone because you wouldn’t gamble on ending up privileged. From this position, Rawls argues, people would agree to two key principles.It’s also worth saying that Rawls is almost always read alongside Robert Nozick, whose Anarchy, State, and Utopia was written partly in response to this book. They’re often framed as two sides of the liberal coin, Rawls representing the redistributive welfare state, Nozick defending the minimal, watchman state. And in political theory courses, they’re still treated as the great liberal debate of the late 20th century. I read them together too, and I can see why they’re paired. Rawls is clearly the more cautious, orderly thinker; his justice is designed to smooth conflict, preserve stability, avoid mess. Nozick, by contrast, is sharper, more provocative, his world is colder, but at least he doesn’t pretend it’s fair. Both are ultimately committed to liberalism’s core assumptions, though Rawls tries harder to mask its contradictions under layers of procedure and fairness talk. From today’s vantage point, both feel dated, but for different reasons: Nozick’s failures played out most visibly in crypto and deregulation, while Rawls’ limits show up in every welfare state that still can’t or won’t touch the deeper structures of inequality.Back to the book.First, the liberty principle: everyone should have equal basic rights and freedoms.Second, the difference principle: social and economic inequalities are only just if they benefit the least advantaged members of society.In practice, this leads Rawls to endorse a liberal welfare state. Not revolutionary redistribution, but a system of regulated capitalism with protections for rights, opportunities, and a social safety net. It’s a framework designed to allow inequalities, as long as those at the bottom are still materially better off than they would be in a more equal but less productive system. Essentially: you can be rich, but only if the poor gain from your wealth in some way.So far, so classic liberalism, but Rawls’ version does, at least, attempt to answer the moral justification for inequality, rather than just taking markets as given. I think there’s some value in that, even if it’s very little. Rawls doesn’t hide behind “efficiency” or meritocracy; he explicitly argues that inequalities can only be defended if they meet strict conditions of fairness. And his insistence that people’s natural talents are morally arbitrary, that you can’t claim personal virtue for being born smart, able-bodied, or talented, is one of the more honest acknowledgements of structural luck in liberal theory. He tries to replace the idea of desert with fairness, and in some respects, that’s an improvement over what came before him.But this is also where the book shows its limits. Rawls’ whole project is an attempt to stay “neutral” between different conceptions of the good life, by offering a procedural framework that doesn’t pick sides. The problem is that neutrality is never really neutral. The subject Rawls imagines behind the veil of ignorance is still a very specific kind of person, someone who can afford to think abstractly about fairness, who assumes they’ll be part of a functioning state, who trusts in legal protections. For people whose everyday lives are shaped by state violence, systemic exclusion, or colonial legacies, the idea that you can bracket out identity and power feels not just unrealistic, but insulting.Rawls also treats the welfare state as the endpoint of justice, as if once you’ve got some redistribution, basic rights, and fair opportunities, the work is done. There’s no serious engagement with histories of dispossession, land theft, or racial capitalism. There’s no place in his framework for people outside the formal economy, or outside normative family structures, or outside the borders of the liberal state. His difference principle might soften the edges of inequality, but it doesn’t challenge the foundations of wealth or the power structures that keep certain people at the margins.In that sense, Rawls’ justice feels more like a ceiling than a floor. It sets the upper limit for what’s considered acceptable, but it leaves little room for more radical demands, for forms of justice that go beyond redistribution into reparations, abolition, or systemic transformation. It’s a theory built for a world where the worst you can imagine is being poor within a functioning liberal democracy, not being stateless, criminalised, or erased altogether. From that position, consensus and stability look like virtues. From the margins, they look like containment.If this is as far as liberalism can stretch, no wonder people are looking elsewhere.
