According to my research, when Sinclair Lewis published Kingsblood Royal in 1947, his primary intention was to challenge his predominantly white readers' complacency regarding racism and white privilege. The novel aimed to force this audience to confront the harsh realities of segregation and white supremacist culture. African-American readers, too, considered this book to be profoundly perceptive in that it was aimed at white readers who, typically, were unaware of or indifferent to the kind of systemic racism that the protagonist, Neil Kingsblood, discovers upon learning he has African-American ancestry. It is noteworthy that while white critics in 1947 often found the book's look at racial prejudice contrived, it was highly regarded by African-American publications like Ebony.While I consider this information to be relevant to an understanding of the book itself, and I must state as a prefix that I realize this book was written with good intentions, in my opinion Kingsblood Royal is prima facie evidence of just how stale matters dealing with race-relations can become when viewed across the span of multiple generations. I believe this has to do with the fact that this book was primarily aimed at the middle-class, liberal-leaning citizens of a mostly white American reading public. How, you may want to ask, does this book measure up in terms of changes in racial progress or in terms of the evolving political status of African-Americans since its publication? More specifically, we may be inclined to question further, did the fact that Lewis' novel was primarily aimed at a middle class and liberal-leaning audience affect how the anti-racist tactics of Martin Luther King, Jr were perceived by the power-structure of white America in the 1960s?After further research into these relevant questions, I have come away with a rather shocking viewpoint: it appears that the historical truth of the matter is, contrary to the current reverence for him, MLK was in fact widely disliked by the majority of white Americans during his life, with 63% viewing him unfavorably in 1966. For many white Americans, including northern liberals, MLK was viewed as a 'troublemaker' and that his program of nonviolent direct actions, like boycotts and marches, were seen as 'instigating unnecessary trouble' and 'causing more harm than good'. In addition, media narratives often mirrored these views, going so far as to publicly question if King was a legitimate leader or not. Indeed, the dominant social science approaches of the time often blamed Black people for racial inequality rather than the white establishment.While acclaimed by some segments of the public, many white readers in the late 1940s viewed the Kingsblood Royal's depiction of racial prejudice as 'wildly implausible' and/or 'unbelievable'. The limits of Lewis' critique of the establishment of white power must be taken into account in that his book must ultimately be seen as a 'tolerance novel' that, while progressive, still relied on some of the stereotypes it sought to expose, which is an opinion that James Baldwin was among the first to annunciate. In my opinion, the response to the book aligned with the white power structure in the 1950s and 60s which, particularly in the South, viewed King’s tactics not through the lens of a 1940s novel, but as a dangerous threat to their social order.It should be made clear that this novel operated in the realm of intellectual exploration for its readers, but was specifically limited in that it was itself based in the realm of theory. This stands in marked contrast to King’s actions which created tangible tension, as noted in his Letter from a Birmingham Jail, in that they directly challenged the comfort zone of white moderates. And moreover, while Sinclair Lewis was content to expose northern, suburban racism, King faced the violent, overt racism of the Deep South, which was rarely swayed by liberal literature. Therefore, although Kingsblood Royal tried to make anti-racism palatable for the white middle class, ultimately the white power structure in the 1960s rejected the disruption of the status quo that King’s tactics required.However, I think MLK would have viewed this book with favor and would have approved of Lewis' political projection of racial equality by targeting comfortable, middle-class white Americans and specifically challenging them to confront their own racial prejudices and attendant white privilege. Perhaps I started my reading of this book with a general expectation of more from this author's work than I could possibly have withdrawn, an expectation which, considering the social context of the times in which he wrote, was only partially fulfilled. Three stars.
