Peter's book has become something of a classic overview of the history of the various inquisitions of medieval and early modern Europe and of the myth and idea of "The Inquisition". There have perhaps been more comprehensive analyses of the origins and nature of these inquisitions, but Peters' book gives an excellent overview of both, particularly their roots in Roman law.But the value of this book lies in his detailed analysis of how the mythic version of "The Inquisition" (highlighted in italics throughout the book) arose, evolved and has been influential on western thought, politics and art. The mythic form of "The Inquisition" bears only a tangential relationship to anything that actually existed, but was developed and harnessed first by Protestant propagandists, then by anti-Spanish polemic, then by Enlightenmentphilosophesand finally by anyone who wanted to use a watchword for "violent and ignorant oppression". The way in which the totalitarian regimes of the twentieth century have somewhat reduced the polemical power of "The Inquisition" made for an interesting coda at the end of the book.
