I'm surprised at the high ratings this book received from other reviewers. I've enjoyed other Inspector Rutledge books, but I found it very difficult to get into this one, and then by the time I was enjoying the buildup to the book's ending, it came to a ridiculously abrupt conclusion, leaving me wondering, And then what?I did like the fact that this book further explored the relationship between Rutledge and the ghost of the soldier he had to execute during the war and who now resides in Rutledge's mind. While the earlier books left you to wonder if Hamish was merely some subconscious part of Rutledge's brain; in this story, the ill-fated corporal becomes a real presence with an apparent ability to think independently of Rutledge.Rutledge is originally sent to investigate the disappearance of the daughter of one of the wealthiest women in England. Said daughter hasn't been seen since 1916 in the middle of the war. Now there's the possibility that some bones found on a hillside in Scotland are hers. Meanwhile, a young woman in a small Scottish town has told everyone she is a war widow with a young son, but a series of poison pen letters turn the town against her and reveal a series of lies. Are the two stories linked? Who stands to profit from these revelations?Todd once again looks at the effects of war on both those who served and those who minded the home front, and in that he (they? it really is a mother and son team of writers) is superb. And yet, there was something about it that just didn't grab me the way others have. One thing I think I've discovered is that I like reading these books better than listening to them. I can't find fault with Gillies narration, but I've just enjoyed the physical books better.
