Like poetry, Hemingway’s prose is best enjoyed when read aloud.His sentences are short and with recognizable words and just pretend like you’re narrating an audiobook. Have some fun with it and this collection of short stories, first published in 1927, when the author was 28, will turn some pages.Like much of his work, these can be read as the surface story but much of the most noteworthy aspects of his writing are between the lines, in what is not stated. A focused Hemingway reader will ask why a character is doing something, what are the parties talking about? And why are they on that subject? Hemingway asks much of his reader, but these are well worth the added effort required.Fourteen short works, each a glimpse into this complicated man’s mind, but written simply in his inimitable style. Common themes include isolation, alienation, loss, morality, courage and living one’s life without condition. We also get a look at Hemingway’s world, populated with boxers, bullfighters and wounded Veterans; strong and independent women and damaged people making the most of what little they have left. This also includes some excellent sports writing and the writer confirms his ability in this genre.“Hills Like Elephants” is one of his more recognizable stories and one of his best, masterfully demonstrating his ability to describe a scene within a scene, to shed light on complex interior monologues while only stating a simple dialogue. Also, this includes one of the best lines in his canon.“Fifty Grand” shows us an archetypal Hemingway protagonist: damaged, compromised, but surviving. I compared this to Jack London’s “A Piece of Steak” although Hemingway is more darkly optimistic and pragmatic.“Ten Indians” shows us a tragedy in the periphery, we almost miss it if we’re not paying close attention and this is the source of my favorite line in all of his work: “he was awake a long time before he remembered that his heart was broken”.My favorite is “The Undefeated” and we follow Manuel Garcia as he enters the arena one more time, to face the bull, and the crowd, and most importantly, to face his fears and doubts and to be the man he knows that he can still be.
