This is epic history covering the creation of the Krupp company in the Ruhr industrial area of Germany, in the city of Essen. Until the war in 1870 between France and Germany the Krupp company was also making steel products like railroad ties and wheels for the railroad cars (this was at a time of tremendous railroad expansion in both Europe and North America) and also cutlery. After the war of 1870 they became more and more famous for munitions – particularly steel cannons which proved far superior to the French cannons made of bronze during the 1870 war.

So, in the late 19th century Krupp became a well-known firm for manufacturing quality munitions. It had customers world-wide in China, Japan, and Turkey – and even to some of Germany’s future adversaries like Russia. In Germany Krupp was vaulted to fame and henceforth the Krupp family was treated like royalty. They had a regular audience with Kaiser Wilhelm I and II – and then with the various leaders of the Weimar Republic – and finally with Adolf Hitler. There were portraits of all these dignitaries in the immense Krupp Estate of Villa Hugel, but Adolf’s was removed shortly after the ending of the Second World War.

The book focuses on the four primary owners of the Krupp company beginning with Alfred who took over the company at the age of 14 in 1826. Until 1968 the company was privately owned by the Krupp family (all the shares were owned by the family, i.e. it was only in 1968 that the company was listed in the stock market).

All these owners were eccentric and weird – and the author provides us with many details. Alfred was given to writing all his daily deeds and thoughts and forced his son (Friedrich) to memorize them as though they were sacred texts. He left a tremendous pile of documents of future use to historians (like the author). Alfred was instrumental in making the Krupp family and company famous due to the superior steel cannons he made. It made Germany a military power to be reckoned with in Europe. It was also during his tenure that workers at Krupp were treated as valued employees. Essen, where the main Krupp plants were located, became a company town – with company subsidized housing, schools, and hospitals. Being employed by Krupp guaranteed you a life-long job (provided you towed the line and didn’t stray into socialism and unionism). The workers became dedicated to their company and to Germany – this devotion grew in intensity during the First World War. The Krupp company came to see itself as an instrumental part of the German Army and war effort, of German nationalism and pride.

It was also during the First World War that Krupp used Belgian conscripts to work in their factories – this set a dangerous precedent.

After the First World War, Krupp like much of the rest of Germany, saw itself as being humiliated and betrayed. It was out for revenge and started a secret re-armament program. The author provides a startling description of the brief French occupation of the Ruhr in 1923 and the ultra-nationalism this provoked of the Krupp workers. Six employees were killed during the French occupation – and they became martyrs and were honored annually in bombastic nationalistic rites.

With the advent of Hitler, the masquerade of minimizing re-armament was over. There is an interesting quotation from the author.

In 1943 Alfried Krupp took over from his father, Gustav who was suffering more and more from dementia. Alfried started to use slave labour (referred to as Stucke – livestock). Thousands were used in Krupp factories spread across the Nazi Empire. They were ill-fed, ill-clothed, and many were beaten. There was also child labour. It should also be emphasized that, for example in the town of Essen, the slaves had to walk openly and daily through the streets to and from work guarded by Krupp employees using whips.

For this Alfried Krupp was sentenced at a Nuremburg trial (not “the Nuremberg Trial of Goring, Keitel, Von Ribbentrop…) to 12 years imprisonment. He was lucky to get away with this; and luckier still to have his sentence commuted in 1951 after-which Alfried returned to the helm at Krupp. The author explains that part of the reason for this was the intensifying Cold War and the Allies wanted Germany on their side. Not all of this was received well by other countries.
