Rather embarrassingly I bought this book by mistake thinking it was a Patricia Highsmith novel. Funny how the brain does that, with its short-cuts that trick you into seeing what you expect to see, rather than what’s actually there. I saw the name Patricia and forgot to check the surname, d’oh!At the back of my mind I’d vaguely pigeonholed Patricia Wentworth as one of the Agatha Christie set - cosy crime on the village green. So as usual when I launch full of preconceived prejudice into a novel, I was chastened and most pleasantly surprised.For a start, it’s not actually set in a quaint village with bicycling curates and fruit-bottling spinsters. Rather more prosaically it’s set in a fictional London suburb (Grove Hill) with an arterial main road, heavy traffic and a tube station.There’s a cast of intriguing and complex characters quite unlike Christie’s two-dimensional stereotypes (ouch). The main characters are:- Winifred Graham, a selfish, resentful and entirely self-centred young widow who’s strangled at midnight in the summerhouse (the gazebo of the title).- Althea Graham, her bullied 25-year old daughter who passive-aggressively seems to enjoy the sacrifice and humiliation involved in submitting to her mother.- Nicholas Carey, the chap Althea gave up five years ago to devote herself to her stunningly ungrateful and demanding mother. He’s determined, even “wild”, and doesn’t “really care a damn” (p84) about what others may think.- Miss Maud Silver, with her “unflagging interest in people” (p59), who just happens to be visiting an old friend in Grove Hill, which leads to her growing friendship with Althea and Nicholas, and her determination to absolve them from suspicion of murder. Trim Miss Silver’s acclaimed approach to detection is her steady application of “principle, reason and common sense” (p160).- Inspector Frank Abbott of Scotland Yard - blond, slim and classy (in fact, his family are gentry), so not your stereotypical police detective.Then there are the other folk, just as sharply drawn and all too human, including:- Bert Martin, a director of Martin & Steadman, the local estate agents, who’s handling the sale of the Graham’s house.- Fred Worple, Bert Martin’s shady step-brother. He’s fast and flashy, filthy-rich from some dodgy enterprise and mysteriously determined to get his hands on the Graham’s house.- Sinister Sid Blount and his colourless submissive wife, Milly, who similarly seem set on acquiring the Graham’s house, The Lodge, at any cost.- Ella Harrison with “touched-up hair, bright make-up and extravagantly cut suit” (p35). She’s bold and bored and always on the look-out for a bit of fun with any available male.- Ella’s hen-pecked husband, poor Jack Harrison, a “quietly depressed little man” (p21) who rather regrets having rescued brassy Ella from a chorus-line down at Brighton.- The three Miss Pimms (Mabel, Lily and Nettie) who are tireless in their nosey-parker-ing and gossiping (“Neither a friend nor a shop assistant can talk or listen to three ladies at once, and all the Miss Pimms were talkers” p7).- Obese, easy-going Louisa Justice with her red-haired daughter Sophy, once a best friend of Althea but now living abroad with her husband and “tribe of children” (p31).- Miss Madison who prides herself on the nice respectability of her paying guests.Period detail in the novel that I especially enjoyed included:- Matching embroidery silks at the draper’s (p10).- Myra Hutchinson, an old acquaintance of Althea’s, who’s “vivid as a poster in brown corduroy slacks and an orange cardigan above which her hair glowed like a newly minted penny” (p13).- Fashionable cocktail parties where “you were very lucky indeed if you got a chair, and it was quite impossible to converse, the competition in voices being such as to produce a roar resembling that of the passing of a procession of tanks” (p58).- Having to make phone calls “from the call-box at the corner” (p84) if you didn’t want people listening in to you on the single telephone at home.- Local news spreading not on social media but from doorstep to doorstep by gossiping cleaning ladies and milkmen (p107).- District nurse Agnes Cotton’s cheery mantelpiece featuring “a vase with ‘A present from Margate’ on it full of bronze and yellow chrysanthemums” (p116).- Eiderdowns on beds in a world before duvets (p221).- Miss Silver knitting a twin set in “deep smoky violet” as a Christmas present for her niece Ethel Burkett (p248).Things that made me smile included sentences like these:- “Her little bird like face had an effect of pecking curiosity” (p9).- “Mrs Graham wore her invalidism in a very finished and elegant manner, from her beautifully arranged hair to the grey suede shoes which matched her dress” (p25).- “Mrs Justice was one of those large rolling women who went her way like a benevolent juggernaut, flattening people with kindness, enthusiasm and good advice” (p31).- “John Burford was one of those long pale lads for all the world like a piece of string dipped in tallow” (p114).And then there were sentences that made me sad with their poignant wisdom:- “If you made yourself think about what you were doing, not just with a surface attention but as if each thing really mattered, it did help you to get through the day” (p10).- “Civilization has not destroyed the primitive emotions, but it insists that they should function in private” (p12).- “I cannot undertake to prove either innocence or guilt. I can only serve the ends of justice and say, what I most earnestly believe, that in the end mercy and justice are the same” (p101).One final whimsical thought on the novel …At one point in the story brassy Ella Harrison ponders the “outlandish” word “gazebo”, suggesting to Nicholas Carey that saying this instead of “summerhouse” is just the kind of fancy pretentiousness that Winifred Graham would go in for. Nicholas explains that it’s actually a centuries-old architectural term (p124).I wonder what they’d make of the fact that sixty-odd years later gazebo is the word of choice for Tesco’s cheap and cheerful patio awning, a bargain at twenty quid, perfect for rainy bank holidays …
