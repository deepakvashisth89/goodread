I figured I was in for a ride with this one. Something about the oddly deflective reviews on the cover and the packaging of it. Sadly, not a bad sci fi ride in the really good sense of it. More the vaguely disquieting ride of Dan Brown attempting sci fi on a really bad day.The book opens tediously. The characters are flat, a la Three Body Problem. What is at stake? I don't know. I keep trying to latch onto anything and it doesn't hold. 1/3 of the way through I re-read the first three chapters looking for any hint that this is satire. Anything? Anything? Such is my dedication to getting anything to latch onto as a point to anything. Why do the characters even care? I can't even tell that. Alas. We push on.So we land on this planet where the 20th century earth is perfectly replicated. And, thanks for transporting me to the alien experience of... looking for parking in the rain near a concert hall. 300 years of social and technological advancement and... everything is exactly the same. There are hints this is intentional, True China or something, but if that's the case, it should all be alien to the MC. It should be throwback land far more completely for him to navigate. That culture should be infecting far more.Say the line, Bart! Say it, say it ..... "don't look at the Dragon's Eye"That's it, that's the entirety of the worldbuilding that isn't an exact copy of the geography, politics, and relationship of China to Hong Kong, including a stand-in for Guangdong. There is no reason this is a science fiction story. The inciting incident has to do with... sovereign debt. There's no way for the reader to anchor themselves to this place to enter the story exactly because it is exactly like the one we know, including the technology (buses and trains run on internal combustion engines) but it isn't that place. Forget it's that place, but keep reading. No no, forget it's that place. Don't look at the eye!Anyway, here we are in the first three chapters where everything should be getting established and.... we are whisked from one place to another without any sense of what the MC imagines the bigger picture is. Nothing. Black hole. Things happen, sort of. But most important though the reader is successfully "kept in the dark" as a source of drama. Settle in, that's not going to change.What does the MC THINK is going to happen? Can we know that? Nope. Apparently the drama is so fragile it would be drained of life to follow along with the point of any events. The events are going to unfold and you are gonna watch em.So they unfold all right. We are treated to an embassy full of "agents" who... sort of have to pretend they aren't... but never take that seriously really, committing all kinds of security breaches. It's not an embassy, right?, if it's not functioning at least in part as a diplomatic outpost. No? The author seems to think an embassy is primarily a spook holding pen...with all the leCarre style leadership cynicism infecting everything. Oh, and of course we open with a few moments of Wesley Crusher-ism for our MC. Fan service for bad book readers.While we're on the topic of agents, etc, the author, despite being an editor, never learned to avoid the slender yellow fruit writing tick. It's like water torture by the end. Who are we talking about? Why is this role important this paragraph to avoid using a name as opposed to the paragraph before? Hard enough reading this without getting jarred out of it like that just often enough to forget to expect it.I have to say, though, the hollow sphere-like emptiness of the MC is surprisingly complex thing. We are following him closely, but, why is anything happening? Why is he doing anything? What does he think is going to happen? Why is he able to ignore the cold murderous violence attending events? Why isn't the fact of everyone on staff being ordered to exterminate some other elses (?) and some people refusing... or something, why isn't THAT the story? People having qualms and standing for something. Why isn't... No, just full steam ahead on what appears to be a little side quest. Not burying the lede there at all. Is he re-wired? Is he a machine? This isn't literature. Why is there such a disconnect between normal social realities and this MC going about his moment to moment? It's freaky disconnected in a way that only a few really bad books pull off. So kudos there.Another gem this book pulls off, an MC who gets grumpy about not getting to bed when crap is completely shattering in the air off the fan. Grumpy about late transport, grumpy about not getting a blankee when the hospital is overrun. About all kinds of completely inappropriate to the situation things. This kind of thing cannot be written intentionally. It's to be treasured. The Gary Stu who is accidentally incredibly unlikeable for petty reasons. MWAH. Chef's kiss.This is sci fi for no reason and the MC gets away with a disguise that shouldn't fool someone with 20th century technology. Just a bit written into a corner there. It's Dan Brown all right, where there are interstellar spaceships but everything is always the 1980s.Once things are moving along a-pace, and the coincidences to keep things smoothed over are piling up, we only learn anything at all about the world through the author justifying events in the prose. And what details get dribbled out open up so many questions. A rigid command economy in the future, but... with untraceable cash as the currency of choice?In conclusion, everyone in this book is cynical to the point of evil, except the MC who is barely there at all. This is a subgenre I'm calling Temu Bladerunner as written by Dan Brown.
