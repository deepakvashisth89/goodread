Although in her first book, Morals and Sexuality in Oceania, Margaret Mead refuted the idea of a specific Western adolescence, the most important idea that would permeate her entire career remains the following. Everything is shaped by society, including gender differentiation.
It was in New Guinea that she found the answer she had not dared to hope for. Following this research, the anthropologist wrote "Sex and Temperament in Three Primitive Societies."
This extensive fieldwork within three different communities enabled her to refine the thesis she had already outlined following her stay in Samoa: societies consistently create differences between the sexes. Today, we would say that being a man or being a woman is a "social construct."
As a result, Mead falls into the category of thinkers for whom the relativity of cultures is crucial: men and women play at being men and women, but they are not intrinsically different. The verb "to play" would later gain a fundamental place in Judith Butler's thought.
Therefore, if the differences between the sexes are biological, the differences in "gender"—although she never called it that—would be social.
