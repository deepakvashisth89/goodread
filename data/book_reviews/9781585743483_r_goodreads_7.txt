Family lore is that my grandfather, an engineer, worked on the Thresher in some capacity and was devastated by the sinking. I don't know exactly when or how he was involved, but I do know he carried it with him for years.Polmar provides a fairly solid accounting of the submarine's last days and the potential causes of the disaster -- cautioning that there's no hard evidence to confirm what went wrong, only theories of varying levels of plausibility.In the strict, fact-based sense this is a solid intro to the story, but I do wish he had done more to focus on the human story behind it. Polmar mentions many times, in the abstract, how tightly knit these sailor families were, how devastating the loss of 129 men *must* have been. But I feel he could have done more here -- profile two or three families, give us some of the names of these sailors and what their deaths meant. Polmar takes, perhaps, too much of a military historian's approach -- so focused on the technical details of how a submarine operates and attempting to determine the flaws that caused the disaster he broadly overlooks the potential to ground the story in the people left behind.
