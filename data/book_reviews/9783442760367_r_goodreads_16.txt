In Paradise there are no stories, because there are no journeys. It's loss and regret and misery and yearning that drive the story forward, along its twisted road.

A masterful, accomplished piece of writing from Atwood that showcases her literary adroitness, her ability to operate across genres, her wonderfully empathetic sensibility, and her political consciousness.

Masquerading as a between-the-wars family saga at first glance, this is a narrative which becomes increasingly complex and layered as stories pile up upon stories: Iris' tale of her present as she's reaching the end of her long life, her written narrative of her family history that spans the twentieth century, the extracts from the cult novel, 'The Blind Assassin', which tells of the troubled affair between a pair of unnamed lovers, and the pulp novel-within-a-novel that one lover tells to the other, partly to keep anxious emotions at bay.

As we would expect from Atwood, these stories reflect upon and refract each other, not least in thematics of blindness and the broad concept of assassination, often figurative. But this is no cold novel of ideas: Iris is flesh and blood both in her cantankerous, vulnerable present as she approaches the end of her life and in her younger self. Her sister Laura is more elusive but deliberately so, I think - partly a reflection of the way Iris herself doesn't quite understand her, however close, in some ways, they may be.

Twentieth century politics are deftly woven into the book from the impact of the Depression on a small Canadian town, the struggle between communist sympathisers and more conservative capitalist elements, through to a subtle, understated indictment of a patriarchal regime which keeps young women repressed, contained and, ideally, helpless. Images of incarceration and claustrophobia abound, along with the limited, interspersed freedoms that our pair of lovers only sometimes experience albeit in small, shady, rooms and, more potently, via the imagination.

That rare animal, a book which is both eminently accessible and full of story while also being literary enough to keep your mind as well as your emotions engaged - and with a sense of humour, too (Iris and Laura's tutors!). I confess, I wept a little at the end.
