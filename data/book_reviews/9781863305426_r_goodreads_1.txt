Ok so it appears I am the only reviewer and the only rater here who liked this.Before, I had only read Masson's fantasy works, I didn't know she does realism too- and I loved the way she wove historical material through her made-up story with a lot of psychological material without making the mistake most psychological writers make, of having people too obviously and crudely disordered. Much of the disorder in this book, was ordinary believable human suffering but each turn of the screw drew a little more out.The story is about a Julius Newton historian who writes acclaimed books about composers. His latest book is about Ravel and has some new material that a fan of his, Charles Pym has brought to him. Julius' nephew and his family, as well as his spinster sister get brought into the drama around his publication. The first twist is fairly predictable (see the title of the book) but this is actually only the beginning of a confusing mess of writers, journalists, old flames, and a missing person. The answers lie in history but part of the puzzle is to untangle whose history is most pertinent here.The book is set in Australia, although which part is left curiously vague. Unlike overseas locations such as "Paris", Australia whenever it is mentioned is only generically "Australia" no place names apart from the fictional suburb, Dinwood. I found this puzzling and since I always feel excited to read a recognisably Australian setting, I would have preferred some local specificity.One thing I was surprised to find written in a way I liked were the romantic and married relationships in the book. Even though Masson privileged heterosexuality in the way we view (through Roxane's eyes) Alex's relationship with Isabelle and also his parents ideal-seeming relationship there are sly little winks in the narrative that undercut this, as well as queer, polyamorous (in various ways) and asexual characters and heterosexuality that does not work. So on the surface, the relationships in her book that work are "normal" but they exist on the margins of this teeming mess of human variation. Which is how I remember aspects of some of her other books too. So that I really loved Alex and Isabelle's relationship (which is rare for me...but then the author also knew when to understate it).Julius is enigmatic and complex, and as the reader I kept changing my mind about whether I liked him or not. I did not find him as easy to forgive as some other characters in the book did. There were many betrayals in the book, but some easier to forgive than others. Julius seemed to suffer no remorse whatsoever and may have been a narcissist, but most of the characters were deeply flawed and there was a lot of mental illness in the book, caused by varying degrees of unhealthy childhoods. Characters who in some way were mad or in the wrong could also be full of warmth and relationality (nearly all of them) so that it's not a stigmatising book.I was very engaged in the story of Ravel that was sort of behind the story of the book and liked that a writer had written about a writer who had written about composers, the multiple layers of creativity and creative personalities in the book was part of it's charm. I am not sure why no-one else liked it, to me it was one of the better books I have read this year and a very worthwhile read!
