Thomas Hardy could have opened THE RETURN OF THE NATIVE with a spare but clear descriptive sentence such as,

“Saturday evening darkness on a somber Egdon Heath seemed to be arriving prematurely and yet the road, in contrast, remained clearly visible.”

But Hardy, shoulder to shoulder in solidarity with his like-minded Victorian colleagues such as Charles Dickens, Elizabeth Gaskell, George Gissing and the Brontë sisters, was never satisfied with mere tight-fisted single sentences when lush, mellifluous (or perhaps even lugubrious) entire chapters (earning income by the word, of course) would serve!

If the masterful genius of these 19th century wordsmiths fills you with a sense of worshipful awe, (and, to be sure, it's an acquired taste) then deserves a place on your shelves and your reading lists. However … be warned. The plot is outrageously overdone and melodramatic to an extreme that simply would not be tolerated were it written today.

Hardy presents the lives of four distraught and confounded Wessex natives – Eustacia Vye, Damon Wildeve, Thomasina Yeobright, and Clym Yeobright – as a commentary on marriage and adultery, moral dilemmas and decisions, human weakness and a struggle against destiny. Which of the four is the most intensely narcissistic, selfish and unthinking is a matter of personal choice for the reader. None of the four offers anything that a reader might admire or seek to emulate. The sole outsider, a slightly more focused and credible Diggory Venn, is so honest, so fair-minded, so compassionate and so charitable as to beggar the imagination and stretch the same modern reader’s credibility to the breaking point.

In the hands of a writer like Shakespeare, for example, the plot, which Hardy treats completely seriously as a heart-breaking romantic tragedy, would never have been more than a farcical romantic comedy.
