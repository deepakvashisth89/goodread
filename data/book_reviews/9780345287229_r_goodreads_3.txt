Topper is a classic 1926 comic novel about a mild-mannered bank officer with a mid-life crisis that includes a high-spirited bevy of ghosts.


Topper is in a rut, a deep rut, a Mariana’s Trench of a rut. He is doing the same things every day, day in and day out. He walks to his suburban train station, takes the train to NYC, does the same tasks every day at the bank where he works, and comes home and faces the same routines at home every night.

Each day, Mrs. Topper prepares a meal that she moans she can’t eat because of her dyspepsia (chronic indigestion). After dinner, he goes out for a walk by himself. Then the couple sit in their respective plush armchairs where he reads the articles from the evening newspapers that interest him, and then Mrs. Topper reads articles he is NOT interested in out loud to him. Topper just can’t take it anymore.

Topper’s breaking point came when he discovered a that had gotten tangled in some string in his garden. He took the bird in his hand, removed the strings that bound it, and noticed how frantic the bird was to be free and fly away. Topper realized that he wanted to be a sparrow (Cosmo has rather low expectations in life). Like the sparrow, he wants to soar. He wants to fly.

After this avian revelation, Topper goes wild and buys a beautiful, powerful car he has seen in a used car lot. It was a car that anyone would die to own—in fact, two people already have.


Mrs. Topper apparently does not have a first name, but she makes up for it by having dyspepsia and a near-infinite supply of self-sympathy. She wallows in it like it is a mud bath and has no desire to lose the dyspepsia. She is certain that, in terms of martyrdom, it makes her the equivalent of Joan of Arc. Mrs. Topper needs her dyspepsia. She is “tender about it” like it was her baby and seems certain that Cosmo and she will stay together for the sake of the dyspepsia.

This causes Topper goes off in a Huff—well, actually in his 1926 Roadster—and to drive angrily around the countryside. Eventually, without realizing it, he accidentally passes by the spot where the original owners of his roadster—Marion and George Kerby—were killed when they crashed it into a tree only 3 months earlier. Cosmo is greatly surprised and frightened when the Kerby’s ghosts suddenly appear in his peppy jalopy.


Before their unfortunate deaths, the Kerby’s were the town’s most notorious hedonists. Anything-for-a-good-time Charlies. Party. Party. Party. And being dead wasn’t about to keep them from living life to the fullest.

They were able convince Cosmo that they were friendly specters and then drove him into town where they could all get some liquor. If there is one thing you can learn about ghosts in this book, it is that they have some serious drinking problems. They need to attend some AA (Apparition Alcoholics) meetings. The two of them get Cosmo all liquored up and get him into a fight with three (count 'em) 3 local rowdies, which gets him beat up and arrested. He kind of liked it. Adventure at last! He wants some more.
