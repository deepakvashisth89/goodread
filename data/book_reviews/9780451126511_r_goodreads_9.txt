Because I read the Preface to this novel, which was written by the author more than ten years after the book was first published, I was prepared to think that Terms of Endearment would be mostly about Emma, the daughter of Aurora Greenway. This was the third book in his series about Emma, after all, and in the Preface, McMurtry makes a big deal about loving this character. But in Terms of Endearment, Emma’s story played a background role. Book I is titled “Emma’s Mother, 1962” and runs for 360 pages. Book II, titled “Mrs. Greenway’s Daughter, 1971-1976,” took up only a mere 50 pages at the end. Having previously seen the film, in which Emma and her husband Flap have very prominent roles, I was doubly surprised.

So the book was really about Aurora, and what a character she is! She wants things her way and will hang up on people who call if they do not please her sensibilities. The terms of endearment of this novel are not the sweet words that people use to call their loved ones, such as “honey” or “sweetie” or even “darling.” They are the terms of a relationship that they are willing to accept. Aurora has many suitors, but under what conditions would she allow them into her life? What are the hard lines and what can be negotiated?

Aurora is formidable. “She was adept at small silences, and knew it. Silences were the equivalent, in her repertoire, of the Chinese water torture – they fell, second by second , upon the most sensitive nerves of whoever had been so foolish as to occasion them.“

Aurora is a widow in her middle years, though, and she is lonely for all the attention she receives. Her daughter’s pregnancy prompts her to realize that her years are passing by, and it saddens her. “It was a thoughtless state, a formless sadness; she could not even say whether what she missed was someone to want, or someone to want her, but the ache behind her breast bone was so pronounced that she occasionally hit herself, hoping that it would make it loosen and go away. But the feeling that made the ache was too strong; her little blows didn’t affect it. It was her old off-centeredness or uncenteredness, a sense that something was stopping, but hadn’t ought to stop.”

Aurura feels that she wasn’t “falling behind, slipping into some sort of widow’s stupor,” but instead was moving “beyond reach,” and it was “killing” her energy, her hopefullness in the future. While there could always be miracles, “there was no counting on them.” She realizes that she may have to take risks and bend a little here and there. This story is about Aurora’s journey, her quest to find a new way of being herself, and this quest is what made the indomitable Aurora so endearing to me.

There were other reasons to love this book. I will only touch on one. If you were alive in the early 1960s, the products mentioned in the following quote will be familiar and evoke a nostalgia in scent and touch, but it is the end of this sentence that struck me. “ Brylcream and Vitalis gleamed on the heads of those men who bothered to take their Stetsons off, and the women’s hair was mostly upward coiffed, as if God had dressed it himself by standing over them with a comb in one omnipotent hand and a powerful vacuum cleaner in the other.”

If you saw the movie, you know the ending is sad. What you may not know is that the movie script varied somewhat from the book. There are other important characters. A minor point - the Jack Nicholson character is quite different and not an astronaut. But the differences did not bother me. Both book and movie can be appreciated on their own as two independent works of art, like Aurora’s two paintings, the Renoir and the Klee. As always, my advice is to read the book first.

I loved this book. 5 stars, gratefully given for the experience of reading this novel.
