Essential reading for anyone fed up with the state of things as they are. This Ken Knabb translation is much better than the Fredy Perlman one with the famous cover.

Summary of the first three chapters for my own records:



“In societies dominated by modern conditions of production, life is presented as an immense accumulation of . Everything that was directly lived has receded into a representation.” The spectacle’s scope is total. It’s colonized all of life; the unity of a life “directly lived” has faded to a mythic memory. The spectacle isn’t merely the “mass media”—this is only a “limited” conception of the spectacle, its “most glaring superficial manifestation.” The corruption of mass communication doesn’t lie in the media , but in the way they reflect the whole of society. The spectacle is the materialization of the ideology () of economic production, having extended its mastery beyond production into consumption and finally into consciousness. The spectacle is the materialization of commodity relations, the ultimate instrument of hegemony. It veils the inequality of class power, extending to the masses only a “concrete inversion of life.” “It is the sun that never sets over the empire of modern passivity. It covers the entire surface of the globe, endlessly basking in its own glory.” It’s manifest in every sphere of life—from politics and sports to the arts and sciences. This very write-up is a manifestation of the spectacle—it’s perfectly Protean in its capacity for concealing itself and cloaking itself under every imaginable form, and defies every effort to elude it.

The spectacle represents a further stage in the development of alienation. The era of the degradation of into has passed. What’s taken its place is the degradation of into The spectacle freezes the “fluidity” of life and shatters it, and then reassembles the fragments as a world above the world—“a ”—that can only be . Everything missing in life is to be found in the spectacle. As to this Debord invokes the celebrity: “As specialists of , stars serve as superficial objects that people can identify with in order to compensate for the fragmented productive specializations that they actually live. The function of these celebrities is to act out various lifestyles or sociopolitical viewpoints in a . They embody the inaccessible results of by dramatizing the by-products of that labor which are magically projected above it as its ultimate goals: and …” The spectacle is both the God-builder and the atom-splitter. It reduces multiplicity to unity and dissolves unity into multiplicity. It converts the many into the one and splits the one into the many. The spectacle is the “common language” of the atomization it engenders. “Spectators are linked solely by their one-way relationship to the very center that keeps them isolated from each other. The spectacle thus reunites the separated, but it reunites them .” The spectacle is “the opposite of dialogue”—“a never-ending monologue of self-praise”; that is, its arguments require no answer and don’t allow one. Thus the spectacle remains protected by a syntax which deprives the spectator of the right to speak. This silence is both the spectacle’s prerequisite and its goal.

The spectacle lifts sight—“the most abstract and easily deceived sense”—to the place of preeminence once occupied by touch. However, the spectacle emerges in the way that images become so free that they escape our own control, and turn our encounter with them into a monologue that we find ourselves unable to interrupt. From this the spectacle is evidently the heir to religion. “Spectacular technology has not dispersed the religious mists into which human beings had projected their own alienated powers, it has merely brought those mists down to earth, to the point that even the most mundane aspects of life have become impenetrable and unbreathable. The illusory paradise that represented a total denial of earthly life is no longer projected into the heavens, it is embedded in earthly life itself. The spectacle is the technological version of the exiling of human powers into a ‘world beyond’; the culmination of humanity’s separation.” Under the reign of the spectacle, as under that of religion, “the individual’s gestures are no longer his own; they are the gestures of someone else who represents them to him. The spectator does not feel at home anywhere, because the spectacle is everywhere.”

During the Cold War when Debord was writing, there were two apparently warring spectacles—really one spectacle with different scepters. On the Eastern side was the “concentrated spectacle,” its ideology condensed around a dictatorial personality. On the Western side was the “diffuse spectacle,” the Americanization of the world, driven by “freedom of choice” in a glittering carnival of consumer products. Its motto: “Jump on the bandwagon, or be crushed under its wheels.” As in society, on the world stage the spectacle grounds its hegemony in an (engineered) split. A real conflict between the individual who wants to live and “the society of the spectacle, where the commodity contemplates itself in a world of its own making,” is veiled by conflicts between ideologies, “racisms,” that are in fact mutually reinforcing. However, these “sham” conflicts aren’t mere phantoms; they reflect the uneven, contradictory development of world capitalism. Even before the fall of the Berlin Wall, it’s evident to Debord that the more flexible, diffuse spectacle will prevail. Under the diffuse spectacle, each commodity promises the heaven of the commodity-form itself, and at the inevitable moment of disillusion another commodity appears, promising the same thing. “Each individual commodity fights for itself. It avoids acknowledging the others and strives to impose itself everywhere as if it were the only one in existence. The spectacle is the epic poem of this struggle, a struggle that no fall of Troy can bring to an end. The spectacle does not sing of men and their arms, but of commodities and their passions. In this blind struggle each commodity, by pursuing its own passion, unconsciously generates something beyond itself…” Like a warring monarch, the commodity-form doesn’t enter into the thick of the fray. It sends forth each manifestation of itself to “[fall] in battle” and goes on to victory in its “absolute realization.”

57: “The society that bears the spectacle does not dominate underdeveloped regions solely by its economic hegemony. It also dominates them . Even where the material base is still absent, modern society has already used the spectacle to invade the social surface of every continent. It sets the stage for the formation of indigenous ruling classes and frames their agendas. Just as it presents pseudogoods to be coveted, it offers false models of revolution to local revolutionaries. The bureaucratic regimes in power in certain industrialized countries have their own particular type of spectacle, but it is an integral part of the total spectacle, serving as its pseudo-opposition and actual support. Even if local manifestations of the spectacle include certain totalitarian specializations of social communication and control, from the standpoint of the overall functioning of the system those specializations are simply playing their allotted role within a .”

44: “The spectacle is a permanent opium war designed to force people to equate goods with commodities and to equate satisfaction with a survival that expands according to its own laws. Consumable survival must constantly expand because it never ceases to . If augmented survival never comes to a resolution, if there is no point where it might stop expanding, this is because it is itself stuck in the realm of privation. It may gild poverty, but it cannot transcend it.”
