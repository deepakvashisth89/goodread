As a staunch advocate for plant reverence, I have to agree on many of Schweitzer's broader claims. I do love his detachment of ethics from divine command. He instead grounds ethics in life, in the "will-to-live". It's almost Nietzschean, if, you know, Nietzsche was a good Christian who felt bad for hitting birds with rocks. As he so astutely puts it,
"Society is something temporal and ephemeral; man, however, is always man." (16).
"Remain men in possession of your own souls! Do not become human things which have offered hospitality to souls which conform to the will of the masses and beat in time with it." (20)

I also quite liked his take on warfare. His entire framework is built on reverence for life, and nothing could be more irreverent than carpet bombing children for cheaper oil.
"In some cities now air raid practices are held, with sirens shrieking and all lights out. People shove something over their heads which makes them look like beasts, and rush into cellars, while flying through the air appears the superman, possessing endless power for destruction." (27)
The horrors of Schweitzer's age are the horrors of our own, technologically amplified. This text was a result of his witnessing the devastation of WWI. And when threats of atomic bombs became a reality, he applied the same sentiments he put forth here, 40 years later. Schweitzer realized nothing could save us but reverence for life. Not knowledge, not dominance, not even respect for life, but an absolute, totalizing reverence for it. Reverence derived not from providential incentives, or some sort of Boethian summum bonum, but from a pure solidarity with life, from the cellular level up. For Schweitzer, modern warfare constituted the preeminent threat of aerial attacks. For us, it is the hellacious omnipotence of drones and atomic bombs.

Much has changed, and yet nothing has changed at all. Thus we are forced to reckon with a question that is just as urgent as it was a century ago:

"Shall we make or suffer history?"(35)
