A friend handed me this book, and I read it for no better reason than that it was suddenly there.There are interesting ideas in it — fear as an obstacle, discipline as a form of attention, the possibility that ordinary perception is only one arrangement of the world. But the longer I spent with Don Juan, the less I saw a teacher and the more I saw a man with an extraordinarily forceful interpretation of reality persuading someone more uncertain to inhabit it with him.The method is almost beautifully dark. First, alter Carlos’s consciousness through ritual, exhaustion and psychoactive plants. Then, when he describes what he experienced, explain to him what it “really” meant. Any doubt becomes evidence that he has not learned enough. Any contradiction can be absorbed into a system whose rules are controlled by the person teaching it. Reality does not exactly transform; it is given a new narrator.And Don Juan is a very confident narrator.At times, the book feels less like a path to knowledge than a remarkably detailed lesson in how belief can be manufactured. Give someone an experience they cannot fully interpret, provide the vocabulary afterward, repeat until your framework becomes stronger than their own — and eventually they may begin seeing what you told them was there.There is intelligence in the book, though I suspect much of it belongs to Castaneda as a writer. The four enemies of knowledge are memorable because they have the polished universality of ideas that could have wandered in from almost any philosophical tradition. What surrounds them is more dubious: an old man repeatedly insisting that his private cosmology is not merely one way of seeing, but the hidden structure of the world itself.I didn’t dislike it. I was occasionally fascinated by it. But mostly I found myself studying Don Juan rather than learning from him.Perhaps that is the more useful education here.Not how to enter another reality, but how easily someone with enough certainty can convince you that theirs is the only one.
