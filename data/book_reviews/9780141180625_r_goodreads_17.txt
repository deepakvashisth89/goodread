Oh boy. How do we talk about Charlotte Perkins Gilman? Or perhaps more accurately, how do I, a fourteen-year-old British zoomer in 2022 talk about American feminist writer Charlotte Perkins Gilman who was writing at the turn of the 20th century, without sounding like I have no idea what I'm talking about? I have spent an extended amount of time reading this book and researching Gilman's life, and still I'm not entirely sure how to go about this, but I thought I might as well try.I should start by saying that you can't review this book without reviewing Gilman as a person. It's impossible, because the didactic, political, formulaic nature of almost all these works make it abundantly clear that she was a feminist before she was a writer. She dons and sheds so many different genres, from utopian sci-fi novellas to satirical domestic dramas, faux-parables to gothic ghost stories, and none of them she executes particularly well, in her unwaveringly humorous, conversational style. The only thing I find her to excel at is psychological thrillers,The Yellow Wall-PaperandThe Rocking Chairbeing my favourite short stories, but even these exist to teach and to challenge, it seems she never wrote purely for the joy of writing.Her poetry suffers from the same issues. Drenched in iambic pentameter and heavily structured (though that could be attributed to the style of the time), it exhibits little of the love of language that makes poetry so good, it's bland and completely typical. However, I did enjoy her allegories and extended metaphors, and sometimes there'd be a gorgeous moment, like this fromSeekingDied the music and the laughterFlames and roses dying afterAnd the curtain I was holding fell to ashes in my handMostly though, her lyricism is much of a muchness.So when I found myself rating each story, I found myself mostly rating Gilman's politics, her message, and a lot of it was good. I loved her depiction of female friendships and camaraderie in stories such asMrs. Beazley's Deeds, where a kind boarder helps her landlady escape a terrible marriage, andTurned, where a woman named Mrs Marroner helps her maid get back on her feet after she was impregnated by Mrs Marroner's husband. Almost all of Gilman's stories touched on the theme of motherhood, putting a different spin on the patriarchal ideology that a woman's primary purpose is to procreate, by portraying it as an incredible, sometimes magical thing that women are capable of, on top of having a career and doing good for the community.The Rocking ChairandOld Watercomment beautifully on ownership, and the fact that women don't owe men anything.However, a lot of her messages did detract from the experience of reading. By the time I got toDr. Clair's PlaceI was thinking that if I had to read about women's irrigation systems one more time I was going to scream. There is a constant focus on education, that everything a child does has to be educational, which I suppose is understandable seeing as Charlotte Perkins Gilman didn't get a full education and probably wanted every future generation of girls to have one, but it still got a bit annoying. And although she really tried to show not tell, it doesn’t quite work; often she uses metaphors and fable-like story structures to try and show you something, but you usually end up being told it anyway, it's so heavy-handed. But these are the least of her evils.There is something dark lurking in these stories, particularlyHerland, where she talks at length about what would make the perfect utopia. For clarity, the premise of this novella is that three men stumble upon a utopian society populated exclusively by women who had evolved to reproduce asexually. The following quote comes from a point in the text where one of the men asks a Herland woman about the worst qualities in their society."When we began—even with the start of one particularly noble mother—we inherited the characteristics of a long race-record behind her. And they cropped out from time to time—alarmingly. But it is—yes, quite six hundred years since we have had what you call a 'criminal,'"We have, of course, made it our first business to train out, to breed out, when possible, the lowest types.""Breed out?" I asked. "How could you—with parthenogenesis?""If the girl showing the bad qualities had still the power to appreciate social duty, we appealed to her, by that, to renounce motherhood. Some of the few worst types were, fortunately, unable to reproduce."Charlotte Perkins Gilman was not only a feminist activist, but an open eugenicist. In her utopia, populated by a bunch of women who are seemingly exactly the same, all "bad qualities" are bred out. What are these "bad qualities"? When you look at how the inhabitants of Herland are described as being "of Aryan stock" and the (presumably non-white) indigenous people of the rainforest area are constantly called "savages", it begins to seem like these "bad qualities" might be simply people of colour. In fact, a lot of Gilman's beliefs on eugenics stemmed from this idea of "racial purity", and she was in general, a massive racist. In 1908, she published an essay which basically outlined her plan to enslave all Black people (she called it "enlistment") so that white people didn't need to have kitchens in their homes. She believed in equality for white women only, saying that Black Americans were "inferior" and below "a certain grade of citizenship".This is why it is so hard to review Charlotte Perkins Gilman's stories. She was a revolutionary feminist, and a lot of these stories contain morals that a lot of readers today would agree with, but are these messages still empowering once you realise she intended them only for white women. If you look atThe Vintage, a story about a man with syphilis who doesn't tell his wife, ending in her and two of their children dying and his one surviving son being a "cripple", is the main lesson to disclose if you have a STD, or to not have children if you have a "bad quality"? And because her writing is so didactic, can we ever separate the art from the artist? Some could argue that Gilman was a product of her time, and needs to be judged as a historical figure above anything else, but although she made a splash at the time, she was mostly forgotten by the 1940s, and only rediscovered in the 1970s.The Yellow Wall-Paper, her most famous work, was dubbed a classic about 80 years after it was published, so a lot of the historical significance we bestow upon her is retroactive.So where does that leave us? I'm not sure. I thinkThe Yellow Wall-Paperis still an excellent story and worth seeking out, and though a lot of these stories still resonate, much of what Charlotte Perkins Gilman wrote and believed is best left in the dust.
