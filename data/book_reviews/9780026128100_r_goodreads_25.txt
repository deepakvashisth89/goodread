Definitely one of the best books I have read this year, and I recommend it wholeheartedly. Of course, it would be better to read “Inside the Third Reich” first, but I should admit that “Шпандау: Тайный дневник” left a much brighter impression on me than “Inside the Third Reich.” I am not sure whether I would be so impressed if I have not read “Inside the Third Reich” first and would not be able to see the difference between the two books and think about this difference.

This is another of the two large books that Albert Speer secretly wrote while serving his 20-year-long sentence in the Spandau prison. was his primary task then, and he concentrated all his efforts on describing Nazi Germany and his personal role there for posterity as fully and objectively as possible, so he published it right after his release from prison. At the same time, he had enough time and mental energy to reflect about the same things from a more subjective point of view, to try to analyze his personal internal attitude to Hitler and Nazi policies then and now, and he started to make some random notes about all this separately, not planning to include it in (because it was a collection of self-reflections and intimate observations of a different format, less useful for historians studying Nazism overall but very important for himself and for everyone interested in his personality). Also, he added various remarks about everyday life in the Spandau prison and his conversations with the other inmates and personnel of the prison, and all this together eventually made another book, quite large and incredibly interesting, in my opinion. He published it in 1975 under the title ( in German), and in 1976, it was translated and published in English as

(I planned to read it in English translation, but it would not be very soon, and as I found the Russian translation in an audiobook, I decided to take it because of my high interest in the subject right now. Just a matter of convenience and speed. You may read the original text in German or the English translation if you find it more appropriate for you, of course.)

So, this book is first of all a real diary which starts at the time when Speer received his sentence after the Nuremberg Trial and ends with his release from the Spandau prison exactly 20 years later. It is a mosaic of various observations, memories, and reflections that Speer collected day after day during those 20 years. The notes are presented “as is,” i.e., in a partly random order (chronologically when they were related to his everyday life in prison and absolutely randomly when they were about what he remembered or tried to analyze about himself or the Nazis overall). However, the reader may separate several different subjects in all this, such as:
1) Spandau prison: how it was organized and functioned;
2) Life of the 7 inmates of the prison and relationships/conversations between them.
3) Speer’s memories and reflections about Hitler, Nazism, and himself.

Honestly, I found all the subjects fascinating in their own way, and their mosaic overall created one of the most interesting books I have read recently.

Shortly about the first two subjects:

Previously, I had no idea that the top Nazi officials convicted during the very first (and main) Nuremberg Trial were all kept in the same prison and were the ONLY INMATES there all those years. Yeah, the whole prison for just 7 people, and after the last two ones who received the longest (20 years) sentences were released (Albert Speer and Baldur von Schirach), only one inmate was left there, Rudolf Hess, who received a life sentence and lived in the prison all alone until the end of his life (he committed suicide in 1987 when he was 93 years old). So, the prison contained only those 7 prisoners at the beginning, and after 1966, only one prisoner (for another 20+ years). After Rudolf Hess finally died, the prison was intentionally demolished and replaced by a shopping centre (“to prevent it from becoming a neo-Nazi shrine”). All those years, when the prison functioned, it had a very special regime there, absolutely unique, with a strictly regulated order of equal contribution of all occupying powers (American, French, British, and Soviet). I think I will write about it a separate post, because all this is very interesting, and if Speer decided to publish a book about all these things only, it would still be a bestseller in its own right. I also could not imagine previously that all those post-Nuremberg inmates in fact were able to communicate freely with each other (they all had separate cells, of course, where they were locked at night, but during the day, they could walk around, talk to each other, work in the garden, eat together, and overall live like in a sanatorium, honestly )). Again, Speer did an invaluable job collecting various observations about the lives of those top Nazis in the prison, relationships between them, conversations they had, etc., and this itself could have made another separate book.

However, the most interesting and important part of the book is Speer’s memories and reflections about Nazism overall, Hitler in particular, and his (Speer’s) complex relationships with them. These random notes look like a very honest and often painful attempt to reconsider his life and give a frank account before history and humanity about his guilt (to a much greater extent than in ). Of course, I tried to be sceptical, because, again, Gitta Sereny shows that he still wasn’t ABSOLUTELY sincere even there, but the whole impression of an intelligent and genuinely remorseful person is very strong. (Honestly, I fell in love with him a little there, because I was able to recognize a fellow autistic soul there; an intelligent and self-sufficient person who was spending these 20 years as productively and efficiently as possible in his situation. And his invention of “walking around the world” all these years, in order to stay physically fit! That’s what I would do, definitely!) Also, his observations about Hitler and Nazism here are more personal and random than in but they are incredibly valuable as part of the true portrait of this extraordinary personality and his immense influence on the minds of other people, including Speer himself.
