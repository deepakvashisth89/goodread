If we toss forth the word "Inquisition," what would the mind come up with? The Roman Catholic Church? Spain? Torture? Witchcraft? Maybe Ferdinand and Isabella. Galileo. Goya. Dostoyevsky. Because nations, religions and sciences have evolved over the centuries, and Freud spoke, and Jung wrote, and the idea of community is even today taking on new meaning, the associative context of the word "Inquisition" has grown a little loose. We're vague about it, largely because we've come to view it in the abstract. This is good news for historians, says Professor Peters. It heralds an opening to finally frame up a piece of the past that's been shrouded in myth, empowered to symbol, and just generally misunderstood.Peters is not going to tell us there was no Inquisition. Nor is he here to tell us it wasn't that bad. His aim is to draw attention to the origin of the inquisitorial process, which was a part of the legal system of the Roman Empire. It wasn't until that empire and its emperors converted to Christianity in the fourth century that an inquisition became the go-to procedure for dealing with heretics. Church and State began to develop side by side - attracted, repelled and impacted by one another - and nowhere was this more in evidence than in the evolution of jurisprudence; both secular and ecclesiastic. Language conveys it. Inquisition. Inquest. Inquiry. Not all medieval trials were faith-based; not all torture was ordered by a prelate. Religious inquisitions, as they were held from the fourth century forward, and in terms of their level of cruelty, persecution, punishment and death, were very much reflective of the secular methodologies in use at the time.Peters, who is nothing if not ambitious, goes on to track the shift of an inquisition into The Spanish Inquisition (and the Portuguese, the Roman, the Venetian, et.al.), which was a culmination so powerful and pronounced (fired in no small part by the heretical possibilities of the newly-discovered printing press) that it transformed the real procedure of investigating religious heresy into a symbolic obstacle to freedom - not only of belief but of thought and expression, progress and perfection, innovation and fulfillment; of living the life of a human being. Here come Galileo, Descartes, Locke, Montesquieu, Voltaire, Hugo, Poe, Goya, Darwin, Orwell, Arthur Miller. All enter the tangle of the real and the symbolic, and this tangle holds for centuries because the symbol is such a fertile one. And as substantial in its way as the resounding clap of the quietly-whispered "No."If your interests run to this sort of study, Professor Peters has done a yeoman's job with it. Here's a book fit to fire a few new reading projects.
