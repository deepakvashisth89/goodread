Elizabeth Bowen has always been a master of writing about the hypocrisies that upper-class Irish and British families often displayed towards individuals and societies that they deemed inferior.

Specializing in drawing room comedy of manners, and melodramatic storylines that mirror “Downton Abbey” (and no, I’ve not watched a single episode) with rich dowagers, ruthless social-climbers, innocent children, orphaned characters.

The book is about orphaned Portia Quayne, 16, wayward and lost, who moves in with her upper-crust brother Thomas and his prim and proper wife Anna, who feel uncomfortable that they have taken on the care of a girl raised in a bohemian upbringing.

Portia falls for Eddie, a handsome cad who was a former lover of Anna’s, and her innocence about love and its follies soon scares him away; for he is overwhelmed with her overly innocent views on life, from her inexperiences at being so young.

Ms. Bowen is very good at writing about young, rejected girls who are often feared upon because of their ideals not matching their rich, conservative elders; and in turn, the ideals of old and new world often tend to clash. Emotions are always big, operatic and yet intimate.

The central character of Anna is richly textured; shrewish and childlike all at once, with a nasty temper to boot; reminiscent of melodramatic performances rendered by Bette Davis, Deborah Kerr and Greer Garson during the 1940s; or with Olivia Colman, Frances McDormand for today's audiences.

It is juicy reading for those who crave a great melodrama.
