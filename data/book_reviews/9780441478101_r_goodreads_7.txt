The Left Hand of Darkness by Ursula K. Le Guin has a voyeuristic quality, as if a description to a studious observation. I could not help thinking that I was reading a National Geographic article about a reporter visiting Winter, or Gethen as its inhabitants know it.

Many readers cannot help but comment upon the Gethenians physiological androgyny, and this is certainly a central theme of the story, but there is so much more to fascinate the reader. Le Guin has demonstrated again how she can create a science fiction fantasy novel that is both entertaining and enlightening, using the fantasy as a vehicle to explore social and psychological themes, and to state observations about our culture as metaphor.

Ray Bradbury noted that to distinguish between science fiction and fantasy, fantasy is the larger genre, a mere impressionistic lens through which we can better view our world. Likewise, Philip K. Dick (Le Guin’s high school mate, though they were not then known to each other) uses his abstractions, not as a Kafka-esque absurdist portrait, but rather as shifting hyperbole to better highlight and caricaturize the real world.

In this sense, Le Guin uses TLHOD to speak to us just under the surface about a great many subjects: sexuality, social mores, violence, politics, psychology, religion, and anthropology. The final scenes where the two journey across a vast wasteland of ice took this experience for the reader to another level.

A thoroughly excellent book.

*** 2025 reread -

Ursula K. LeGuin’s 1969 novel that explores gender fluidity, survival themes, and ideology was a joy to read and continues to be a timeless visit with our cool friend.

This a part of her Hainish cycle, a collection of stand alone novels that exists in the same universe. And it’s a very interesting universe. The way our author describes it, humanity had been a far flung galactic empire ages ago, with colonies on all sorts of hospitable planets. Then something happened and Hain neglected her children for ages and ages, and when they were able to send out envoys again, the various islands of mankind spread out amongst the great oceans of space had changed to adopt to the needs of their planet.

A Hainish envoy arrives on Gethen, a planet also called Winter. He finds anachronistic forms of government and a people adapted to make the most of their hard scrabble existence. Most notable was that the populace are ambisexual, having evolved to best make use of the opportunities for survival. In a cycle that made me devilishly consider a comparison to Piers Anthony’s satirical and humorous novel A Spell For Chameleon. I may have just lost some feminists there, but it was a very obscure, left field comparison because I have a twisted sense of humor. While Ursula’s wonderful novel has some lighter moments, this was not funny and we get to know the Gethenians and their unusual sexual evolution.

Essentially, and I hope I’m telling this right, a Gethenian is androgynous and for the most part asexual, concentrating as they must on survival in the harsh climate. Once a month they are able to procreate and will become either male or female, depending on the proximity of suitable partners, so that one can get pregnant and keep the race going.

The envoy also encounters archaic governmental structures and Le Guin spends time fooling around with what this means to due process, freedom of speech, etc.

A wonderful book, a fine entry in her Hainish series and this may be a good introduction to her brilliant work for new readers. Highly, highly recommended.
