This was a book club selection, and I probably would not have chosen it myself. The daughter of two famous anthropologists, Margaret Mead and Gregory Bateson, had a very unconventional upbringing in a series of extended families. Repressing any anger she might have felt when her parents were away, she seems on the whole to have thrived from her experiences. She became an anthropologist herself, carving out her identity in a different area, Middle Eastern linguistics, while benefiting from conversations and ideas from her (divorced) parents. I liked least the parts about child-rearing, most the relevations about her parents' methods.My favorite suggestion, in terms of fairness and impracticality, was that we seek common communication by translating into two languages only: one widespread language (presumably English in its various forms) and one relatively obscure one (not Chinese or Spanish, for example), on the grounds that ALL human beings struggle to learn it, on an equal basis, and not waste time on an artifical language like Esperanto or on multiple languages (the UN model and now, the EU's). This was Gregory's idea, I believe. But would anyone bother to learn the second language?
