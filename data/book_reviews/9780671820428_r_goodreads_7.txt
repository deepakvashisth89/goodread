Edith Howland has part of it hanging on her wall at the height of McCarthyism, and all Americans who aren't part of the pedo protection cult would do well to remember it during this fucked-up time.Edith's Diaryopens in 1954 and spans twenty years, although I think Highsmith handles the timeline a little awkwardly, especially given a book like Richard Yates'sThe Easter Parade, which was published a year before this one and flawlessly spans decades. Here ten years pass in the first fifty rather tiresome pages, and Highsmith often signposts the years by having Edith remember events we read about half a dozen chapters earlier, or never encountered at all. She finds her rhythm, however, and as the book progressed Edith grew on me to such an extent that by the end I hated every single one of the condescending and self-righteous men interfering in her life. Journalism, sculpting, alcoholism, and mid-century suburbia—checks the title page—nope, it still isn't a Yates novel. The humor is dark and pretty quirky, and it's the first book I've read in which a guy's come socks are a plot point. Where, I wonder, had Highsmith stumbled on this detail?Not a thriller—not in the traditional sense—which I knew going into it. Prominent political events mainly denote the passage of time and contribute to Edith's mental state, which is said to be deteriorating partly because what she says makes those around her uncomfortable. Sometimes rightly so—such as during her deeply racist rant. But how many Americans are presently said to be overreacting for recognizing that the US is in the midst of a fascist takeover? Just wondering. It can and is happening here. The book ends just after Nixon's resignation—we'll never be so lucky—and had Edith been living in 2025, she would undoubtedly have wanted to shove fountain pens in her ear holes every time the orange pedo opened his Bubba-worshipping mouth—luckily I own no fountain pens. Highsmith's politics, especially later in life, were rather contradictory, but still she manages to hit the nail on the head regarding the pathetic state of America—then and in the present day:In New York people say politics don't interest them. 'What can I do about it anyway?' This is the attitude government powers in America want to foster and do. News is brief, filtered and slanted. The Guatemalan 'uprising' would have been far more interesting if social conditions there had been described and if United Fruit Company's activities had been exposed - by radio and TV. Discussion clubs should be set up all over America to talk about forces behind things. We have been brainwashed for decades (since 1917) to hate Communism. Reader's Digest has never failed to print one article per issue about the inefficiency of anything socialized, such as medicine. From the American news media we have snippets without scenery, character or background. How could it be 'interesting'?And as regards the Nixon/Trump playbook:All they had been talking about seemed so unimportant compared to Nixon's save-my-skin tactics now, the fact that a powerful country had no leadership now. Nixon was promising a total pull-out from Viet Nam, announced figures proudly - 'Another five hundred home this week!' - because the country was full of people like the Johnsons who wanted their sons home, of course, but Nixon made no comment on the philosophy or strategy of being in Viet Nam in the first place or of pulling out now. Now that he was scared about his office, he was going to China to fill the TV screens with pictures of himself on the Great Wall, no doubt, and using chopsticks. Shades of George Orwell and 1984.
