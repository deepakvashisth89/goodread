When I set out to read Virgil's Aeneid a few months ago, I decided on the Alan Mandelbaum translation from 1970, and ordered a paper copy. The paper copy took a long time to arrive so in the meantime, I started a free ebook edition translated by John Dryden, c1710. I also downloaded a parallel text with Virgil's original Latin on one side of the page and a translation by J.W. Mackail on the other (I learned Latin at school).
In spite of having the ebooks, I was impatient to get my paper version. When it finally arrived, the packaging bruised and battered, it turned out that the book gods had played a trick on me. The book I received was not the Mandelbaum translation but a 1981 edition translated by Robert Fitzgerald. It was a lovely Everyman hardback so I wasn't unhappy. But I didn't give up on my original choice and downloaded an ebook of Mandelbaum's .

If you're wondering how the rest of Aeneas's fleet survived Juno's fierce storm, well, he needed the help of his goddess mother, Venus (his father Anchises was human). Like the Greek heroes, Achilles and Odysseus, Trojan Aeneas has some superhuman powers but he is mortal and needs help from the Gods from time to time. So instead of continuing on to Italian shores after Juno's storm attacked his fleet off Sicily, Aeneas turns southwards with Venus's help, and his bruised and battered ships find safe harbor at Carthage on the North African coast, then ruled by Queen Dido. Dido falls in love with Aeneas but he knows he cannot stay with her since the fates have decreed that he must take his people to Italy and found the city of Rome (Virgil was writing this saga during the time of Augustus Caesar, centuries after the time in which Aeneas's odyssey is set, so he emphasizes the glory of the future city of Rome, and by naming Aeneas 'father Aeneas' at times, he associates him with Augustus in his readers's minds. Dryden even uses the word 'August' in his translation so we can't miss the parallel: "The Trojan chief appear'd in open sight, August in visage, and serenely bright."(my other translations didn't use the word 'August' but the parallels are still clear).

I kept being drawn back to Dryden's rhyming couplets even though one of the introductions to the other translations claims he took liberties with the text. When I'd return to Mandelbaum afterwards, his more literal lines jarred a little with me. Fitzgerald was a happy medium. His lines seemed to flow better than Mandelbaum's and are thought to be more faithful than Dryden's. But still I read Mandelbaum and Dryden the most—the Fitzgerald hardback is not nearly so portable as my phone.

Once Aeneas goes ashore in Italy, the story becomes a long series of battles because the people who live there are mostly not welcoming towards Aeneas and his people, or accepting of the fact that it is his destiny to colonise their country (and some have been manipulated by Juno too). Aeneas's main enemy is another mortal demigod called Turnus (they both seek a marriage with a local princess called Lavinia, daughter of the king of Latium). The drama in this final section of the book is intense, and Virgil leaves the reader divided as to who to root for. Sometimes I was cheering Aeneas and sometimes Turnus. Here's Turnus rousing his men: "Audentis Fortuna iuvat"
Dryden translates it as: "Fortune befriends the bold."
Mandelbaum as: "for fortune helps those who dare."

The goddesses interfere in the conflict all the time, and on one occasion a simulacra of Aeneas is created to draw Turnus away from the battlefield towards safety. So we have an interesting scene with Turnus chasing after the vanishing Aeneas while the real Aeneas tries to chase after the vanishing Turnus. And Turnus being removed to safety makes his earlier words about fortune favoring the brave seem two-pronged. When he reaches safety, he is ashamed because fortune has favoured him and sent him away from the battlefield. But never fear, Turnus will get a chance to fight a last battle when Aeneas eventually tracks him down—as you might have guessed.
