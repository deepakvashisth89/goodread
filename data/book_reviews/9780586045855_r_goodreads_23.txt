"Staying On" by Paul Scott was published in 1977. It won what is still the most prestigious prize for fiction-writing in the UK - then known simply as The Booker Prize, now known as The Man Booker Prize - and the dust jacket of the edition that I have just read is replete with praise from such literary luminaries as poet Philip Larkin. I regret to say that I simply fail to see what all the fuss is about. "Staying On" is just about OK, but is really no better than that. Given its reputation, it is a deeply disappointing book.The events described in the novel occur in a place called Pankot in India. The story begins - and ends - with the death in the early 1970s by natural causes of Tusker Smalley. Tusker had been a colonel in the British army in India until he was forced by circumstances to take early retirement. Those circumstances were the gaining of independence by India from Britain in the 1940s. Instead of returning to Britain, Tusker and his wife Lucy chose to stay on in India. "Staying On" charts aspects of their life together while they were in India, a period of considerable social upheaval brought on by the collapse of the British Empire. India found it necessary to reinvent and to assert itself in the light of its newly acquired freedom from Britain. The story looks at how this transformation affected both the old ruling class, in the form of Tusker and Lucy, and those, such as their devoted servant Ibrahim, who had hitherto been used to a life of subservience. The old way of doing things was ending. The rules governing social interaction were changing. "Staying On" examines how people caught up in the transition from old to new dealt with this.Very little happens in terms of the plot in "Staying On". That in itself is not a problem. One of my favourite novelists, Barbara Pym, produced a series of excellent books in which little or nothing much happens. Her skill at characterisation, at poignant humour and at hooking the reader by the sheer verve of her prose more than made up for her lack of plotting skill. The same cannot be said of Paul Scott. His prose is frequently leaden. And he has a tendency to write very long sentences, which quickly lose the reader. Here is an example (from page 11 of the edition I have just read):"In the days before the Shiraz was built this had been the witching hour at Smith's because it was at 8 o'clock that the night train from Ranpur was scheduled to get in after its long haul up the single track into the Pankot Hills from the plains and, consequently, at that hour that staff and management had been ready for the arrival of guests who had booked and been hopeful of others who hadn't booked and all of whom began to turn up at about 8:30 in taxis, tongas, avid for breakfast during the serving of which the luggage of departing guests would be piled on the verandah so that it and they could go down to the station to catch the midday departure back to Ranpur."There are many other examples of such infelicitous writing throughout the novel. The characterisation of Tusker and Lucy (and of some of the more minor people involved in the story) is reasonably well done. But that cannot mask the fact that "Staying On" is essentially a worthy, but dull book. I was often bored with it and was on more than one occasion tempted simply to give up on it (a temptation that I managed to resist). Despite comments to the contrary by Philip Larkin, its emotional impact is, in my view, minimal. It is a very disappointing novel. 4/10.
