John Adams didn’t think too much of Thomas Paine, saying—among other things—that Paine was better at tearing things down than building them up. That feeling may have followed Paine’s Age of Reason, the next-to-last of his writings to appear in this book.

I mostly purchased this to have in my possession what Origins of the American Revolution credited with turning the colonial public against the King: Common Sense. I wanted to read it to find out just how much it might have riled people up. Ultimately, I was underwhelmed with it. Paine doesn’t attack George III like I expected him to, but rather destroys the idea of kings in general. In that, at least, it was effective. was shorter than I anticipated too, but I found myself enamored with Paine’s colorful language and his use of metaphor.

My English and Philosophy degrees let me get a lot more enjoyment out of this than most twenty-first century readers would, yet I can see why the curmudgeonly John Adams would have been less than impressed with Paine’s bluster. The American colonists did not want to separate from England because of the king, specifically. But Paine convinced them that they should. Down came the statue of King George.
