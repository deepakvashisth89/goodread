2024/79My friend J. and I plan, by the end of November or the first week in December, what to read together during the next year; this we have done since 2022. Thus, in November 2023, we choseMelmoth: The Wanderer, which we would be reading as part of the spooky season in 2024. Let me tell you that that read never happened and here is why.On January 8th, 2024, we both knew about the passing of one of our favorite booktubers: Jennifer Brooks. When he told me that she had passed away the bad news hit me like a bucket of cold water. I couldn't believe it, and even though I didn't know Jennifer personally, she had been part of my reading year all of 2023. I used to leave comments here and there on her videos, and she would reply to them; I was so used to seeing her reviews every time one of her new recommendations popped up on YouTube. After some talking with J., we decided to change two of our readings, and the new choices were not a coincidence: we wanted to remember Jennifer and remember her through our readings, books that she always praised and highly recommended. One of them wasThe Marble Faunby Nathaniel Hawthorne, which we read around August 2024, and whose review is probably up among the others—if I'm not mistaken—and the other one, the one that Jennifer always spoke highly of because of the beautiful writing style, was—wait for it—George Eliot'sRomola.Romola, unlike other Eliot's books, doesn't take place in England, but in Italy. Even more, this is not the Italy that George Eliot visited, but a period far away from the Victorian era. We are in the Italian Renaissance, around the moment when Christopher Columbus arrived in America, and among a lot of very interesting historical figures: Lorenzo de' Medici, whose demise is just the beginning of our story, Girolamo Savonarola, and Niccolò Machiavelli, who has a few 'cameos' here and there.This also feels somewhat different to most of Eliot's novels narrative-wise. I'd agree with Jennifer on how beautifully written this is (it is as though you were reading a long poem in prose), and it's not only the descriptions that are so poetic and organic, so to speak, but the whole structure of the novel reminded me of a painting from the Renaissance, perhaps Botticelli'sThe Birth of Venusor Titian'sVenus of Urbino. The author, at times, also cuts the story to 'boast' about her vast knowledge of this period, art, history, and essentially, the people who lived there. Albeit fictional,Romolakeeps some characters' fate faithful to what happened in real life. I'll venture to say, although not from beginning to end, that some of the real characters, such as Savonarola or Piero di Cosimo, have a more interesting plot than our protagonists'. A hot take, I know.As for Romola, she might not be the best Eliot's female protagonist, but she has certainly some traits that make her unique and unforgettable. Her story is worth reading, with a lot of ups and downs that come with Tito, a new visitor in town who plans to stay one way or another.In short, this novel is notMiddlemarchorDaniel Deronda, and probably not an option to kick off your Eliot journey with, however, it has quite a few thought-provoking moments, and an ending—especially when referring to one of the real characters' ending—that is chaotic, yet incredibly meaningful.I will always be grateful to Jennifer Brooks for bringing so much joy to the Booktube community and for making me readRomola, such an incredible novel. I tend to visit her channel from time to time, watching videos that I have already seen a few times before, taking notes on how to enjoy my readings a lot more, just to remember what an excellent booktuber she was—the fact is that she will never be forgotten.My rating on a scale of 1 to 5:Quality of writing[5/5]Pace[4/5]Plot development[4.5/5]Characters[4.5/5]Enjoyability[4.5/5]Insightfulness[5/5]Easy of reading[4/5]Photos/Illustrations[N/A]Total[31.5/7]=4.5rounded up
