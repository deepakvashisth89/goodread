I find it impossible to judge this book outside its time - 1992 - the Soviet Union had just disappeared, the Cold War was over, the remains of the Tsar and his family discovered - and all of a sudden things that could not be mentioned were being talked about, archives opened, it was amazing for those of us who grew up taking the status quo as something eternal. It was within this context that Radzinsky's book came out based on Russian documents and Russian recollections - not the ancient emigre literature which was all that was available and not by one of those Romanov obsessed Western writers who, if you were lucky, had a bit of French but no Russian and no archival experience.Is it any good? A million times better than rubbish like 'Nicholas & Alexandra' but while containing heaps of interesting information and speculation it is not by a historian but a playwright and should be treated with caution.The problem is that there isn't a good English language biography of Nicholas that has made use of the mass of new research that has been done by Russian historians. Radzinsky is worth reading but hr leaves a lot unexplored or unexplained.
