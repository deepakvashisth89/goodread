Another book read of the eight books published by a great author whose life was all too short. I'm now half way through that number, and so far they have all been wonderful.

This one is set with generous margins and narrow text blocks, then divided up in short thematic sections each commencing with an ornate Celtic capital. This setting beguiles the reader into believing the book will be a fairly quick and easy reading.

But I should have known better. O'Donohue writes prose like a poet - the sentences packed with meaning. You have to slow down and take time.

I always have this drive to get through books, the stacks that surround me, the ones on wishlists, the ones not yet discovered, old and new. At one level simply ticking off the task, closing the loop, moving on to the next thing, progressing. At another level, the hunger to know and explore, delve into the great mystery. At some other level a gentle addiction born of the need for meaning and/or escape / solace, safe spaces.

"The act of reading," says John O'Donohue, "becomes a wild symbiosis of the reader's longing and the wise configuration of words." (p301)

A change occurred when I started using this book devotionally, reading about three sections at a time not long after waking, and particularly when at last (having passed through parts named 'Awakening in the World: The Threshold of Belonging', 'Presence: The Flame of Longing', 'Prisons We Choose to Live In' and 'Suffering as the Dark Valley of Broken Belonging'), in Part 5 we arrived at 'Prayer: A Bridge Between Longing and Belonging'. Beautiful. Profound. Apt. Generative and moving.

Strangely, though, this is not where the book ends (resisting the urge of most Christian books to end with a warm spiritual glow), instead circling back in a sixth part on Absence, which eventually lands on the necessity (though not as a panacea or without ambiguity) of (non-fundamentalist, non-cultic) community ("a prophetic community... nourished by prayer and animated by divine longing" p372), and an apophatic possibility of presence discovered through/in absence, with longing being the force that draws us up/in/on/outward.

Actually, this circling back to absence paints an accurate existential picture. The discovery of prayer is a wonderful thing that makes a world of difference, but it won't take away your longing, or magic away the realities of life. It's a bit like Kierkegaard's dancer's leap (of faith) - an upward movement and a return to the ground, all hopefully done with grace.

O'Donohue's is a unique blend of philosophically and psychologically informed Christian-inflected (postmodern) Celtic spirituality that sometimes explores the borderlands of what might be considered orthodox. Some might read that description as pejorative - I don't mean it to be. The spaces he explores are up to the reader to consider and navigate. Perhaps I'm not 'selling' it very well. I have found vast amounts that are profoundly life-giving in his books. I have discovered an incisiveness regarding the deeper human condition, intimations of the more than human, a connection, and a way with words that I can't stop quoting in my own work.

O'Donohue writes with an extravagance and generosity of meaning, poetically woven and enchanted, continually plumbing the depths of things, while expanding the width of vision, calling us inward and outward, to bring the human condition into a burgeoning encounter with the divine.
