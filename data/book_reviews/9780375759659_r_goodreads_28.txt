I have a chronic illness—a degenerative neuromuscular disease. It affects me always, but is more debilitating during flare-up periods. These might cause me to spend more time in bed for stretches, kept company by books and mindless television.

I am in no way opposed to or ashamed of the latter. I love reality TV. But in order to avoid my brain turning completely inside out, I try to work on specific reading projects when I can. This year, I have a few going, but one I’m really enjoying my “read the chunky classics you might otherwise never get to” challenge, because it makes my involuntary downtime feel productive.

That lengthy background aside, I enjoyed this next installment in the project. Lawrence is one of those writers who seems important to “get to” and who doesn’t naturally draw my interest. So tackling his famous chunk of a novel was rewarding.

What to say about D.H. Lawrence? He writes beautifully.
His writing is lyrical and poetic, but it’s different from other lyrical and poetic writing I know. It’s larger in scale, somehow. Sweeping. Even when he writes about an individual character, Lawrence spends most of his time describing humanity as a whole.

Because of this, there isn’t a psychological closeness in his descriptions, but there is a sort of big picture, philosophical relatability. His characters wrestle with the same questions we do, and he captures that existential wrestling across many kinds of people: children, teens, young adults, parents.

Lawrence also, because he seems to think in such vast absolutes—he likes universal truth, universal experience—likes to generalize. His favorite thing to generalize about is the great divide, the yin and yang of the sexes. He really leans into and believes in a sort of predetermined, divine masculine energy and its counterpart in the feminine. And sex itself, to him, is the melding of those forces, neither complete without the other.

Now, interestingly enough, Lawrence knew my own literary hero, E.M. Forster. He tried and tried again to persuade Forster (according to biographical sources) that his homosexual feelings were a mere distraction from the greater truth and destiny that is the heterosexual sexual bond. Forster tried to explain to him that, no, thank you, men and men only for him, please, would do just fine—divinely destined or not.

I think the problem here is that Lawrence was a man of great talent and feeling who unfortunately could not understand that his feelings may be different from those of others. This seems to be a common problem with (straight) men of great talent.

I do feel that Lawrence is one of the most masterful writers of his time. I do feel that his insights on humanity and religion and nature and love and life are completely fascinating and worth reading and thinking about. I don’t think his particular way of painting his characters works for me completely.
