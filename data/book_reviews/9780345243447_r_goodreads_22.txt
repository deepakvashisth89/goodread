__________________________The Stars are Not for ManA former Nazi rocket scientist, now working for the Americans, is assembling anatomic-poweredrocket on an atoll somewhere in the South Pacific …(Note:In the 1950s, when this book was written, science-fiction writers thought everything was going to be atomic powered in the future—atomic cars, atomic ray guns, atomic-powered trains, atomic powered homes, atomic powered robots were everywhere in SF. In real life, The Ford Motor Company actually developed a design for an atomic-powered prototype:The Ford Nucleon--you can see the scale-model they developed on Google or Wikipedia. Candy companies were sellingAtomic Red HotsandAtomic Fireballs(jawbreakers). There was a topical muscle pain analgesic calledAtomic Balmthat, as I remember, hurt more than the original pain. I remember ads from power companies appearing on TV promising that electrical power will be almost free if we allow them to build atomic power plants. Ah, the good ol’ days when the atom was our friend.)Well, you’ve gotten me distracted. Don’t do it again. Now, I’ll have to start from the beginning.A former Nazi rocket scientist, now working for the Americans, is assembling an atomic-powered rocket ship named theColumbuson an atoll somewhere in the South Pacific. The ship is designed to take Americans to the moon and eventually the stars and is only a few weeks away from its launch date. But then the scientist looks up at the sky, and he sees something that makes him realize theColumbuswill never fly. The sky is filled with spaceships. Not American spaceships. Not Russian spaceships. Not Chinese. It is undocumented alien spaceships. Flying Saucers. UFOs the size of major cities.The beings in these ships are not tourists or illegal immigrants. They are here to save the human race. They say so themselves. But they refuse to let us see what they look like. What the devil are they really up to?🌟🌟🌟🌟Stars.When I first read this book, I was in high school in the 1960s. If I had been in the habit of handing out stars as book reviews back then, I would have given it five huge shiny ones. In the past 60 years, though, I have read hundreds of sci-fi books, and I now thinkThe Five Stars are Not for Childhood’s End. But is still one of my favorite science fiction novels, and well worth reading for SF fans."Two possibilities exist: Either we are alone in the Universe, or we are not.
