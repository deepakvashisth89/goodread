The more identities a man has, the more they express the person they conceal.What can be said of this compulsively readable, brilliant novel – a thoughtful Christmas present - having finally read it after being coaxed by enthusiastic friends and having both watched the 1979 series with Alec Guinness and the 2011 film twice (a tendency to obsessing one of my many weaknesses )?Likely the first espionage novel since my childhood – only vaguely remembering being engrossed in a manual of spy techniques (creating invisible ink with lemon, attaching hairs to doorposts…) -Tinker Tailor Soldier Spyhad the lustre of exploring uncharted territory - the foreknowledge of elements of the plot, the denouement and the demasqué of the mole in the British secret intelligence service – not in the least making the journey less suspenseful and enthralling.The pages are perfumed with the revolting stench of the rottenness at the core – of the secret service, and of the society it has to shield and secure. Steadily and slowly progressing through a delightfully complex maze of bewildering twists and turns, unpeeling layer upon layer of the moral murkiness through which George Smiley and his former colleagues and allies wade, the hunt for the Soviet double agent exposes multiple levels of chilling betrayal - personal, professional and of the country one is deemed to serve.(Illustration Matt Taylor on the cover of the 2018 edition)Look... we're getting to be old men, and we've spent our lives looking for the weaknesses in one another's systems.
