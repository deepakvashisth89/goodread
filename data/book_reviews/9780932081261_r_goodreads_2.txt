I have a particular interest in the satanic panic and this is an important book in understanding the movement. Whenever I read these "first-person accounts" (Michelle Remembers, Go Ask Alice, Jay's Journal, The Satan Seller), I am a little baffled that so many people bought this was really happening.The first part of this book details the authors own experience with satanism. Obviously, I can't know, my impression here is this was a knowing exaggeration at best (as opposed toMichelle Remembers(, which feels more like false memory production). This reads like incel fan-fiction. God doesn't make a girl to fall in love with him so he turns to Satan? He studies ninjutsu daily in his backyard before it is cool in America?Do I believe the author was a deeply troubled young man grasping for control and meaning in his life? Absolutely. And as I read the remainder of the book, I became more convinced of his sincerity, even though I do feel like there had to be some knowing untruths.In the second part of the book, the author goes through the ways in which occultism exists in our culture. Ultimately, his conclusion is that any form of engagement with the spiritual which is not christian, is satanism. If this is the definition, then I guess I could understand the belief that satanism is widespread. If reading your horoscope makes you a satanist, then sure, it is widespread. However, this is one of the fundamental issues with many religious beliefs...the inability to imagine a worldview different from one's own; to only be able to interpret the world through one's own belief. Most of the practices and beliefs the author explores don't even acknowledge the existence of Satan, so how can they be satanic?The final part of the book runs through the troubles of our society. The answer: christianity. While much of the analysis is shallow, I will give the author credit for more than once surprising me. For instance, he makes a point of saying boys need to learn that girls do not exist for their sexual pleasure and he calls out christians for a lack of commitment to their professed ideals, calling on them to address poverty, homelessness, and so on.I enjoy reading about this time period and trying to understand the psychology of those enmeshed in the movement. Of those, this is a more sincere and balanced version of the narrative, though still rife with fearmongering and unsubstantiated stories of satanic rituals. But for those interested in this moment in history, I do think this is an important part of the record.
