Some classic authors just slip through the cracks. And one of those for me was definitely Jack London. I had somehow walked the earth for 37 years before I ever read more than a short story from him while in a high school class. Having now read two of his most famous works, I completely understand why his works have been so enduring, despite—or perhaps because of—the fact that they are so wholly different from anything else I’ve ever read. Other than perhapsWatership Downin some ways, every other story I’ve read that is this animal-centric has been varying degrees of fantastical. BothThe Call of the WildandWhite Fangfeel anchored in reality even as they imagine inner thought lives for their protagonists.The Call of the Wildkicks off in autumn of 1897 when we meet four-year old Buck, a half St. Bernard who sees himself as king of the Judge's estate. When he's essentially kidnapped, sold by the gardener to fuel his gambling addiction, he's understandably irate. I immediately liked the voice London gave Buck. His mind felt believably canine. And his growing, burning desire to be challenged and tested wasn't a characteristic I had considered developing as a creature grew into an apex predator, but it was an interesting counterbalance against the creature comforts he had once known.It was fascinating to witness Buck's evolution from pampered pet to sled dog to leader of the pack to—finally—a wild thing. But a wild thing strengthened by the civilization that spawned it. I'm not quite sure why this has become so widely accepted as a children's classic. Yes, the central character is a dog, but the writing and themes are deeply developed and mature.White Fang, on the other hand, takes a much more roundabout journey to introducing our titular protagonist. First we have to get through Part 1, which was super stressful. The way London kept increasing the tension was really effective. It’s as the instigator of this tension that we meet White Fang’s half canine, half lupine mother.I'm fascinated by London's imagined insight into the interior life of a wolf cub. I was especially taken with his portrayal of the cub's first experiences with the world outside the cave in which he was born. And I was devastated by the hard track White Fang’s life took, though it did ultimately lead him to better things. Again, I’m not sure how this became to be considered a children’s classic, as the violence White Fang suffers and dishes out is pretty visceral. But the culmination of his story, the love and home he finds, was absolutely beautiful.I was thoroughly entranced by both stories. And I really respect the way in which they mirror one another in regards to their perspective characters’ beginning and journey and final destination. It’s easy to see why London’s writing has endured, and has served as inspiration for countless other authors. While I hate that I didn’t read these works sooner, I’m glad to have them now living within my imagination.
