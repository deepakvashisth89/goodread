”… So inconspicuous an observer … could not explain his power of bewitching large audiences. The Senator was vulgar, almost illiterate, a public liar easily detected, and in his 'ideas' almost idiotic, while his celebrated piety was that of a traveling salesman for church furniture …”

”He would whirl arms, bang tables, glare from mad eyes … and in between tricks would coldly and almost contemptuously jab his crowds with figures and facts—figures and facts that were inescapable even when, as often happened, they were entirely incorrect.”



Let's just say that I'd feel almost positive that a certain president had read this novel and used it as a playbook, except that it includes some big words and I'm pretty sure said president can't read beyond a second grade level. Hell, that third quote just about uses the phrase “make America great again.”

Anyway, is a cautionary tale about the rise of fascism in the United States. It was written almost 100 years ago, but it's eerily relevant to the present day. The first half in particular is 😳😳😳. I mean, it's about a guy who wins the presidency, takes control over all three branches of the government, creates his own private army whose members abuse (and kill) citizens in the streets, and persecutes his political enemies using the judicial system. He promises Americans money that never appears, strips rights from minorities and women, and controls the media. He accepts bribes for political favors, has committed fraud prior to taking office, and believes in the “superiority of anyone who has a million dollars.” He describes anyone who doesn't support him as an “Antibuzz," throws undesirables into concentration camps, and wants to take over Canada and Mexico (and I do seem to recall Greenland being mentioned at one point 😳). He has no loyalty to those who are loyal to him, starts a war for no reason other than imperialism, and there's another smarter guy behind his "throne" pulling all the strings. So … yeah.

The second half of the book gets very dystopian, and I truly hope that Sinclair Lewis was not an actual soothsayer because sweet baby kittens on a cracker.

There are points where I felt that I was way too dumb for this novel — there are a ton of references to literature and historical events and famous people that I just didn't recognize — and the conclusion was a little too open-ended for my liking. Overall, though, it was an enlightening (and admittedly somewhat depressing) read that cautions against complacency in the face of authoritarianism.



4.1 stars, rounded down.
