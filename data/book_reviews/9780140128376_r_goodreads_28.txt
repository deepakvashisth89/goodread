The Bloody Chamber (1979) is a collection of short fiction by English writer Angela Carter. I had heard nothing but good things about this collection and when emmie read the first sentence of the first story in one of her YouTube videos, I knew that I had to get my hands on this book.

"I remember how, that night, I lay awake in the wagon-lit in a tender, delicious ecstasy of excitement, my burning cheek pressed against the impeccable linen of the pillow and the pounding of my heart mimicking that of the great pistons ceaselessly thrusting the train that bore me through the night, away from Paris, away from girlhood, away from the white, enclosed quietude of my mother's apartment, into the unguessable country of marriage."

I mean, how gorgeous is this passage? I didn't know much about the content of these stories but that didn't stop me in the past. I bought Du Maurier's solely because of it's first sentence () and it became one of my all-time favorite books. Unfortunately, I cannot say the same for Carter.

Part of my problems with are "me problems", like my dislike for fairytales and retellings as a whole, others are more "book problems", like the blatant racism, heteronormativity and lack of originality. Out of the ten stories featured in the collection I only somewhat enjoyed two, the rest I found absolutely horrible.

(3 stars)
The first story is a retelling of Bluebeard – which became one of my favorite fairytales after I read the second book in the series by Cornelia Funke, in which Fuchs is abducted by a Bluebeard and saved by Jacob.

Carter sets the story in the present. A young girl marries a rich Marquis who had three earlier wives. She moves into the Marquis' castle, loses her virginity on their wedding night and finds the Marquis' sadistic stash of pornography. The Marquis leaves on a business trip, entrusts his keys to the heroine, only forbidding her from one room. The heroine uses the forbidden key, which leads to a torture chamber containing the bodies of the Marquis' three previous wives. The Marquis learns what the heroine did and prepares to behead her. Just as he swings his sword the heroine's mother appears and shoots the Marquis. The heroine inherits the Marquis' fortune and she, her mother, and the piano tuner live happily together.

This was probably my favorite story in the collection. Despite it's clichés, laughable dialogue ("Have the nasty pictures scared Baby? Baby mustn’t play with grownups’ toys until she’s learned how to handle them, must she?" – bestie babes, I snorted) and the deus ex mother ("the maternal telepathy" BAHAHA), it was actually an engaging story. Like I said, I like the original Bluebeard tale and despite its ending, Carter didn't really change any of the dynamics/tropes. It's not the most original take but it's very well written. But beware of racist language ("the y*llow outlaws of Indo-China").

(1 star)
The second story is where everything went downhill. Early on, I started to realise that Carter's takes on these fairytales is very similar. Her heroines are all the same (young, beautiful, virginal, WHITE, innocent etc.) and most of the stories play out in the same way (heroine gets taken by a beast/creature/man and has to overcome or endure abuse).

"The Courtship of Mr Lyon" is one of two Beauty and the Beast retellings in this collection. Why there's two, I do not know. It is definitely the weaker one. In it, Beauty's father seeks refuge from a snowstorm at an empty mansion. On his way out he takes a white rose and then the lion-like Beast appears. The Beast makes Beauty come to dinner and asks her to stay with him, promising that her father's fortunes will be restored. Beauty agrees but when her father grows rich she leaves, promising to return before winter ends. Beauty forgets her promise and only returns when the Beast is dying. She finds him in his bed and kisses his hands, and he turns into a man.

Plot-wise this story didn't do anything for me. It was boring, predictable and didn't add anything to its source material. There is no true subversion, so I had to ask myself why Carter chose to retell in the first place? This is also the first story in which I noted how Carter enforces the racist undertones/tropes/depictions of these European fairytales. Her heroines are white, in a way that is totally shoved in your face, whereas the aggressors are beastly and foreign.

Some descriptions from the story: "This lovely girl, whose skin possesses that same, inner light so you would have thought she, too, was made all of snow…" or "How strange he was. She found his bewildering difference from herself almost intolerable; its presence choked her. […] And such a one she felt herself to be, Miss Lamb, spotless, sacrificial." I found it absolutely grating to read, over and over again, these tales of woe of these innocent white women who are sexually intrigued but ultimately harmed by these beastly/huge/overly masculine creatures (characteristics that are often used to portray Black men). Carter doesn't challenge the racism of the source material, instead she uses it to interrogate the feelings and inner world of white women. Meh. Boring. Unoriginal. And potentially harmful.


Another Beauty and the Beast retelling. Overall, I liked this one more because it was more subversive. The story has another intriguing opening line: "My father lost me to The Beast at cards." In the story, a Russian man gambles away his daughter to a mysterious nobleman called The Beast. The Beast's valet takes the heroine to a mansion, where The Beast wants to see her naked. The heroine refuses and is put in a room with an automaton maid. The Beast then takes the heroine on a horse ride, where he disrobes and reveals himself as a tiger. The heroine takes off her own clothes in response. (Again, whiteness is shoved into your face: "I showed his grave silence my white skin, my red nipples…") Later the heroine goes to the tiger's room, where he licks her and her skin comes off as she transforms into a tiger.

Personally, I really enjoyed the ending. The "Beastification" of Belle is an interesting trope, and one I would've loved to explore more deeply. It's unfortunate that the story cuts off at the most interesting part.

Another thing I didn't enjoy is the unwieldy incorporation of feminism into these stories ("I was a young girl, a virgin, therefore men denied me rationality just as they denied it to all those who were not exactly like themselves, in all their unreason."), it actually made me roll my eyes. Why go on these tangents and explain your feminism? Why not incorporate it and show it through the action of the characters? Show, not tell. It's the simplest trick in the literary book. But why does Carter not get it? Also beware of racist language, a poem (?) is quoted in this story that speaks of the "base I*dian".


MY MOST HATED STORY. THIS WAS SO FUCKING INFURIATING. I don't even wanna talk about. This is Carter's attempt at a humorous story and I wanted to rip the pages of this story from my copy of the book. Figaro is the most irritating narrator ever. I hated him so fucking much.


In this story, the heroine wanders into the woods and is seduced by the Erl-King, a mysterious figure who lives in harmony with nature and has many birds in cages (yes, the bird cage metaphor is absolutely OVERDONE; Charlotte did it and it should've died with her, if you ask me). The heroine learns that the caged birds were once girls, and she strangles the Erl-King and sets the birds free.

Again, could've had great potential but ultimately fell flat because the story is too short and the ideas in it not really original.


One star is maybe a bit harsh for this story but it's literally only 2 pages long, so it's not even a real story at all. This story is based on a short tale about a wife telling her absent husband that she was impregnated by a snowflake, and then the husband later killing the child and saying that it "melted". Carter tries to subvert the story by having the wife being the jealous one of the child and the Count not just murdering, but also raping the girl.

Maybe there's something there but this story leaves a bitter taste in my mouth. Women in Carter's fiction, just like in fairytales, are either "undamaged", "spotless" heroines or nasty old hags. For a writer who claimed feminism for herself, it's weird that she doesn't challenge these gender norms in her fiction at all?? It's also irritating how heteronormative every single story is. Where are the gay heroines? It's all so fallen out of time.


This is the other story of the collection that I actually enjoyed. Female vampires are just so fucking cool. (I'm gonna read Butler's soon and I am beyond excited.) I also think that the subversion of the original vampire trope works really well. In the story, Carter reverses the gender roles as the heroine is now the one with all the power – men are objects for her, literally food for her desires. Sexuality is associated with violence as the Countess kills young men in her bedroom. Probably my favorite quote of the entire collection: It's also brilliant how she tells these men "You will feel no pain, my darling.", because that's usually a phrase we associate with men saying that to girls/women whose virginity they will "take".

One thing I didn't enjoy about the story is its ending. One day, a young soldier is lured into her mansion, he kisses her wound. She then becomes mortal and dies. Ugh. What is it with powerful women being doomed to be stripped off their power and die?


A Little Red Riding Hood retelling. A child travels through the forest to visit her grandmother. She is attacked by a wolf and cuts off its hand. When she reaches her grandmother's house she finds that her grandmother is missing a hand, so the neighbors kill the grandmother. I have no thoughts.


Another Little Red Riding Hood retelling. Again, why not settle for one? I don't get it. A child goes to visit her grandmother and meets a handsome huntsman on the way. The huntsman gets to the grandmother's house first, transforms into a wolf, and eats the grandmother. The child arrives and seduces the wolf before he can eat her. ("The girl burst out laughing; she knew she was nobody's meat.")

Had this story been earlier on in the collection I probably would've upped my rating to three stars. It's one of the more interesting takes that Carter has regarding the source material. But this far back in the collection I was already so disenchanted and frustrated, I couldn't read the stories without prejudice. I just wanted the collection to be done. :(


Again, I only read it to be finally done with this shitshow of a book. I didn't really comprehend it. If I ever reread this collection (which is unlikely to be fair :D) I will definitely start from the back and read the stories in reverse order to give the last ones a fair chance.

In "Wolf-Alice", a girl raised by wolves. Some nuns take her in but then give her to a werewolf Duke. The Duke is wounded by a bullet and then Wolf-Alice licks his wound, transforming him into a full human. The story contains my least favorite quote of the entire collection, so that's probably a fitting ending for this review: "it seemed to her that a wolf who, perhaps, was fond of her, as wolves were, and who lived, perhaps, in the moon? must have nibbled her cunt while she was sleeping, ..." – BESTIE BABES, I AM DONE.
