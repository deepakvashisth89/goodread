"In the process that carries knowledge from the laboratory to the market, there is not enough fear. And in the history of that process, there has not been enough accounting."This book is extremely well-written, which should be no surprise given Mr. Berry's talent for thorough, considerate, reflective thought. There are whole premises of the book that I certainly agree with — the seizure of science by industry and "The Economy," the privatization of research, the failing application of supposed universalities to local contexts, and the danger of over-compartmentalization and siloing of disciplines. (DM me for my soapbox on how the prioritization of STEM over the arts and humanities has led to ... *waves hands in the air gesturing the general state of the world*) BUT, Mr. Berry misfires in three critical areas:1. The entire book is a strawman, and a flimsy one at that. It is a critique of Science, but quite exclusively only Edward O. Wilson's vision of Science in "Consilience." This would be fine if the book were more specifically described as a book review or a response. Instead, Berry stages war with a dangerously monolithic, uncritical, purely positivistic version of science. Entirely absent are paradigms of post-positivism, constructivism, interpretivism, and pragmatism, each of which addresses the unknown and culturally specific that Wilson (and consequently Berry too!) ignores and which were well established by the time both books were written. It should not be lost on the reader that this is one individual (white man's) critique of one individual (white man's) vision of science. Missing are queer, feminist, and indigenous critiques, which by this point were rampant within academic circles but disappointingly disregarded by both Wilson AND Berry. Berry also suffers from working within the binary fields of "the arts" and "the sciences." In his critique of Wilson, there is no room for historians of science, the sociology of science, or for that matter any of the social sciences writ large, which frequently mediate science, religion, and art.2. Ultimately Mr. Berry's predictions about the totalitarian alliance between Science, Industry, and Government were devastatingly wrong. I do not think that it is happenstance that this book was published in 2000, when science was pressing against religion in politics at a possibly unprecedented rate. BUT this was also the year that the populace (or rather the Supreme Court) chose religion/Bush over science/Gore. Religion did not give way to science as Berry predicted but instead the opposite. And now the planet is burning. For the last 25 years, we have seen a targeted devaluation of any scientific expertise, intellectualism, or general education (scientific or otherwise) that challenges the Tea Party's and now MAGA's Religion–Industry–Government alliance. And today, we are consequently witnessing an unprecedented defunding of nearly all modes of learning.3. Berry suggests that Science needs to adjust its focus from discovery to settlement — deriving adaptation and conservation from familiarity: an admirable vision. But this shift cannot be wholesale; otherwise, it suggests that we have nothing to learn from diference — a dangerous thought that only fuels the flames of anti-DEI sentiment. Discovery is as essential as settlement to our mutual survival on this rock.This was a frustrating read.
