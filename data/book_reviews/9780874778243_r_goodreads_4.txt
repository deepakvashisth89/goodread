It’s often interesting to read some treatise on contemporary events or analysis decades after its publication. What appears unique sometimes may turn out to be common; what seems inevitable turns out to be “evitable”. You’ll also see recurring patterns or strengthening trend. As we stand on the threshold of AI revolution, “the end of work” is an apt title for a book today. Perhaps looking back a little (this current book was written about 30 years ago, when the concern was about the impact of automation), we can learn something? Here are a few example lessons. (New numbers are from ChatGPT, so take with grain of salt.)

Productivity: In the book, Rifkin mentions that to harvest an acre of wheat land took 20+hrs in 1880 and 6.1 in 1936 (a roughly 3.5x improvement in 56 years.) Continue the same speed of improvement to today, it would be another 7x. Reality? The actual improvement was 24x (it takes <15min today). This one is easy: productivity will keep on increasing. If anything, the rate of improvement will increase.

Unemployment: Rikfin listed unemployment rate rising by decades staring from 1950s 4.5%, 4.8%, 6.2%, 7.3%, before moderating a bit to 6.6% in the first 3 years of 1990. This is part of the argument for the displacement of employment by automation. Latest result? 4.1% (Oct 2024). Back in 1996, non-farm payroll was 117M out of a population of 269M (or 43.5%). Today, it’s 159M out of 337M (47.2%). So no, automation’s displacement is not as dreadful. It also creates opportunities.

Wealth concentration: Rifkin mentions that networth of 834K richest families total $5.6T, more than the $4.8T from bottom 90%. This is not easy to compare. But assuming a family of 3.2 person, then that roughly translates to top 1% population. Today, the same top 1% and bottom 90% would own $60T and $46.3T respectively. So yes the rich got richer. But the bottom 90% (which admittedly is too broad) also got a lot richer (inflation from 1995 to today is roughly 2x). So the average Joe is 5x richer in 30 years than the average Joe back then, inflation adjusted. But the same Joe is likely a lot more better off than his younger self. Incidentally, Bill Gates was the richest person back then with $18.5B. His networth is $161B today. (He’s not the richest person anymore. That’ll be Elon Musk, worth $300+B.) Adjusted for inflation, Gates is 4.4x richer. None of this is rigorous measurement. But the lesson appears to be, the rich and the average will continue to get richer.

So if this time it’s not different, then 30 years from now, productivity on many things will be through the roof; we will likely still have a job; and we’ll be another 5x richer on average.
