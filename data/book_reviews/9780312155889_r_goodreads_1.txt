SNOBBERY WITH VIOLINSWhen novels were actually novel, they were about wild and weird places and mad adventures (The Mysteries of Udolpho, Frankenstein); then they calmed down and were about keen observations of social relations with especial focus on the marriage market (Jane Austen, Middlemarch); then they turned towards minute psychological case studies (Ulysses, The Man with No Qualities, Proust). This particular novel is one of those, there are a million of them. It was a whimsical choice.We have a sister and a brother whose partners die. The brother is gay, I wasn’t expecting that, and his grief for the death of his partner is treated in the same way as that for the sister’s husband, profoundly seriously. So far so good.But these people are upper middle-class and swim like fish in a sea of utter snobbery and judgy condescension – everyone is sneering at everyone else, disdain and cheeseparing class distinctions soak the atmosphere they breathe like an invisible poison gas. They have a bad word for everybodyIt’s no easy assignment with these ghastly colonial women whining so loudly through their teethOld women leering crazily from clownish painted faces; old women smiling and nodding with cracked shoes and skirts done up with safety pinsThere was a good chance that Tom might meet people there who would satisfy his notions of Bohemia, while yet being a little less washed-up and half-baked than the usual crowd he mixed with.Weren’t they rich trade? That sort of people always have to pretend to more than they’ve known in life. They don’t feel happy unless they’re the trout among minnows.He knew that the Rogersons thought him off-beat, unrealistic, smug; he thought them smug and unrealistic and sincere, and evaded them more than the colonels and the stockbrokers whose unrealistic, sincere aggression touched nothing in him.Our Mrs Eliot and all her frenemies spray all this contempt for everyone not exactly like themselves in all directions and you couldn’t find a better explanation than this careful novel as to why the 1960s were such a brilliant and necessary explosion – anything to put an end to these insufferable 1950s people and their monopoly of expression. Give somebody else a go! Open a few windows!No surprise, this book gets bogged down in pages of soul-searching and almost Jamesian examinations of the ninety different ways well-meaning people can slightly misunderstand each other and how much you should throw your life away helping lame ducks and boring stuff about what plants are grown in nurseries (the brother runs a nursery) and how it’s just not possible to carry a weak member of your string quartet when trying to play Bartok so the second violin has to go and all sorts of really feeble stuff.I think only five things happen in these 350 pages. Not enough for me. I want things to happen.
