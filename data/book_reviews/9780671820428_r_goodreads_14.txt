Great book—very keen and insightful.This book, which forms a nice counterpoint to Updike’s over-enthusiastic “Couples” serves as a depressing catalogue of the underside of the sexual revolution. Although most reviewers note the careful cataloging of Edith’s slide into crazy, I would like point out that it is not at all clear who is crazy here.In my reading, Edith appears for the most part to be fairly balanced, and the actions of the people around her are unreasonable and horrible. Her ex-husband is gutless and heartless, her son is cold and sociopathic, and her government (i.e., America fighting in Vietnam) is filled with fear and paranoia masquerading as principle. Through this sh*t storm she soldiers on, keeping house, nursing a relative of her ex-husband, holding a job and saying good bye to beloved family members.In this view, Edith could be considered the most upstanding person in the novel, for enduring events which would have thrown a normal person into an angry rage, and yet keeping her cool. Perhaps it is because she never slips into that rage that other readers consider her crazy, or perhaps they are simply accepting the view of the friends and neighbors of Edith within the novel, who, as time goes on, keep hinting that she needs medical attention (when she does eventually start to show anger about the government and her situation).In this reading, Hightower is challenging us as to who is the crazy one: Edith, or the people around her who calmly tolerate the load of BS and selfishness that life in this suburb constantly flings in her way.
