Overall, I enjoyed this book. I'd say I'd rate it in between 3 (liked it) and 4 (really liked it). I particularly enjoyed the bits about remote island dwellers Steve and Suzanne Lawson's connection to the land, and their amazing activism to protect the rainforests of BC and the species that live in them and in the surrounding waters. I was in awe of their fierce commitment and was fascinated by their story.

Trapper Murray Monk's story challenged me to think about the fur industry in a new way (and I say this as a vegetarian, mostly vegan of nearly 20 years). Living in Northwestern Ontario, even as someone who loves animals and does not want to see them suffer, you do have a different relationship with activities such as hunting. I have never been against hunting because I know too many people who use it responsibly and ethically to feed their families, and it's more ethical by far than factory farming. But trapping seemed frivolous and wasteful. I am now not sure that is the case.

Wilkins has a pretty good knack for capturing a person's character, Murry Monk's especially, even though at times I didn't feel this work totally cohered as a book and the writing was overall a bit uneven.

I've never read much travel writing before and it was interesting to experience types of adventures I'm not at all interested in experiencing first hand, such as travelling on a cargo ship on the Great Lakes (even reading about being in open water makes me a bit sea sick) or climbing mountains (yup, afraid of heights too).

Wilkins also managed to capture some of the love I share for Thunder Bay, our sadly beleaguered community (or best kept secret in Ontario, depending on who you ask). He included some history about the city I didn't know much about. His anecdote about the elderly man who showed him around the East End and then sang to him in Ukrainian was very touching.

Although I appreciated Wilkins' efforts to include several Indigenous voices in the story, including (sadly, almost as a footnote - too throwaway, I felt) a bit from Freda McDonald, a respected Elder in Northwestern Ontario who died recently but was living at the time of this book. I felt, however, that he overly conflated the relationship white folks of settler origin have with the land with the relationship that Indigenous peoples have with the land. I kept thinking back to and thinking the book needed to be re-written with an understanding of settler colonialism in mind (I grant that this term has only gone into circulation recently, but the idea of Indigenous and settler relationships to the land being different and an awareness of colonial history existed in 2002). The anecdote about how he entered an area where Metis people were squatting (by the grain elevators, I believe) that had a sign asking white people to keep out heightened my feeling that he didn't really get it, despite his efforts. I keep thinking as well of the anecdote about Bruce Hyer that Wilkins threw in (Hyer became an MP after the time of this book) - that Hyer rode the railway until he saw a place he felt he wanted to make his home, and then he settled there. This entitlement to the land - the idea that you can just have some instant connection and ownership without regard for who the traditional stewards of that land may be - is at the heart of settler colonialism. And to treat that flippant sort of connection alongside the connection that Steve Lawson feels as an Indigenous man - who recognizes that as an Anishinaabe person he is also a visitor to the territory where he lives on the west coast, and has worked to build relationships there - just misses a level of nuance that would have made this book so much better. Not that Wilkins had to hammer this point home in an academic way, but the differences in relationship to land that different people have could have been signposted more throughout, rather than mashing these stories together in a way that came across as disingenuously unifying.

Before I conclude, one more prop for Wilkins not related to the content of this book: I met Wilkins some 15-ish years ago in my senior high school creative writing class. Some of us read our work out loud and he was very thoughtful and generous in his responses. In anticipation of meeting a "real author" I had bought this book on sale in hardback for him to sign but hadn't read it (not that I told him that) before he signed it with a truly lovely personalized inscription about how he liked my work and unique voice and to keep writing. That was a kindness I really needed at that time in my life, so I treasured the book, even though I've only gotten around to reading it now!
