Pride Will Have a FallThere is some temptation to make the whole of Dickens’s mammoth of a novel,Dombey and Son, boil down to the above saw – all the more so since Dickens, according to Forster, meant to focus on Pride the same way he had done inMartin Chuzzlewit, its predecessor, with regard to another deadly sin, Greed. However, since a saw – as Ambrose Bierce suggested – has been given its name because “it makes its way into a wooden head”, we might flatter Dickens, and ourselves, by assuming that the novel is something more but a mightily inflated Sunday schoolbook text in that it not only intends to criticize individual vice but also social ills that help it sprawl.In this respect,Dombey and Sonwould be the first example of a new quality in Dickens, who no longer limited himself to being a minstrel of mere morality tales in which good and evil are rooted within individual characters and are just a question of their inherent moral qualities, but began to take a more pessimistic outlook on society as the source of injustice, moral decay and personal misery. One first step towards this new outlook had already been taken in his Christmas taleThe Chimes(1844), although its result was somewhat garbled – not so much to its contemporaries but in retrospect – by the narrow confines of the tale and Dickens’s attempt to put too much into too little space (and to coat it over with yucky sentimentality), but withDombey and Son, a novel, Dickens had the opportunity to develop his criticism on a large scale. One might argue that there was already social criticism inOliver Twist, what with Dickens’s jabs at the Poor Law and the workhouse system, but that early novel seems more of a simple morality tale to me since Oliver, its protagonist, for all he sees and endures and for all the corrupting influences he grows up under, never leaves the Path of Virtue by an inch.Dombey and Sonalso heralds a new epoch in Dickens’s literary career in that the author now carefully planned his novels in advance and discarded the picaresque and episodic structure altogether. It centres on Mr. Paul Dombey, an overseas merchant, and his miserable, loveless family life. Dombey’s wife dies at the beginning of the novel, giving birth to a son, who is the focus of Dombey’s ambitions and expectations in that he is expected to carry on the family business and to live up to the name of Dombey like his father did. With a view to future glory, Dombey is impatient for Little Paul to finally grow up – only to find his ambitions nipped in the bud when the little boy dies. Instead of trying to seek solace in the love of his daughter Florence, whom he utterly neglects, he begins to quarrel with fate, which has bereft him of his son and left to him a daughter he has no use for. His estrangement from Florence increases, whereas Florence, a true Dickensian female, still overflows with self-forbearing love and tenderness, and Dombey more and more falls under the spell of his conniving and underhanded employee James Carker. He also decides to get married again, and his choice falls on Edith Granger, a widow, whose pride and reserve equal his own aloofness. The marriage is not a happy one, and Edith finally runs away with Carker, who – feeling himself constantly slighted by his master – has been waiting to deal him this blow to his self-respect and who also has done his utmost to ruin the firm. The company eventually going bankrupt – last not least due to Mr. Dombey’s proud failure to admit its endangered and weakened state to himself and to act accordingly –, the merchant, embittered, withdraws into himself, only to be finally redeemed by Florence’s unwavering love.One might see this plot development as a treatment of an individual’s overbearing pride and arrogance but the way Dickens presents Mr. Dombey also makes it possible to regard him as a typical representative of Victorian manners and values so that the novel may be said to criticize predominant codes of social behaviour of its time. It is true that we have no Court of Chancery, no Circumlocution Office and no Podsnappery inDombey and Sonbut nevertheless there is the railway, the harbinger of change, which destroys time-honoured neighbourhoods and coerces old trades to redefine themselves.In its subplots there are other instances of social criticism: Dr. Blimber’s school may not be as horrid and sadistic as Mr. Squeers’s in that the Doctor seems to mean well, and yet it is a place where young men’s minds are wrecked and stifled. Cousin Feenix may be an honest man, and yet he turns a blind eye to how his relative, Edith, is married to the rich businessman because her mother hopes to strike a good bargain and because it is the way of the world. Rob the Grinder’s unbearable hypocrisy is marked out as a product of the sort of school he attended. And Mr. Morfin, another minor character who makes but few appearances in the novel, points out very well how injustice and social wrongs can thrive when he says:”I am sure […] I have good reason to believe that a jog-trot life, the same from day to day, would reconcile one to anything. One don't see anything, one don't hear anything, one don't know anything; that's the fact. We go on taking everything for granted, and so we go on, until whatever we do, good, bad, or indifferent, we do from habit. Habit is all I shall have to report, when I am called upon to plead to my conscience, on my death-bed. ‘Habit,’ says I; ‘I was deaf, dumb, blind, and paralytic, to a million things, from habit.’ ‘Very business-like indeed, Mr What's-your-name,’ says Conscience, ‘but it won't do here!’”Mr. Morfin’s indictment of habit, human sloth and indifference, clearly shows that Dickens no longer sees the Quilps, the Jonases and Pecksniffs, the Sikeses as the origins of evil but rather society’s inclination to put up with them, which is probably why there are no dyed-in-the-wools villains inDombey and Sonanymore; even Mr. Carker, who could qualify as one with his Iago-like double-facedness, tries to justify his course of action by referring to the treatment he received at his employer’s hands, a very threadbare excuse but still a sign of Dickens’s growing awareness that evil deeds often need some feeding-ground.Despite its new quality,Dombey and Sonsuffers from typically Dickensian shortcomings such as its goody-two-shoes female protagonist – Florence’s readiness to suffer almost seems masochistic – and some contrived happy endings, and both these kinds of shortcomings may be seen as resulting from Dickens’s readiness to give in to his readers’ demands – unlike Hardy did, who finally stopped writing novels altogether. Luckily, there are the typical Dickensian treats as well in that we find a wealth of unforgettable minor characters – such as the mean-spirited Major Bagstock, the generous Captain Cuttle, the naïve Mr. Toots, the intrepid Susan Nipper, the good-natured Miss Tox, or the witchlike Mrs. Pipchin – and numerous instances of Dickens’s overwhelming prose, like Little Paul’s deathbed scene, which has a feverish quality about it.
