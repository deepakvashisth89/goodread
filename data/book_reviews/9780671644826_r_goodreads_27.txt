TL;DR The premiere work on Ed Gein. I recommend for any true crime fan, but not for the squeamish.I am the local serial killer expert of my community. Do I have any objective evidence of this claim? No. However, I have declared myself the expert, and no one has refuted it thus far. Specifically, Ed Gein has always been a fascinating person to me - his upbringing, mental condition, crimes, trial, and legacy were unprecedented. So when I found a book on Ed, I decided to pic it up and give it a shot.Ed Gein, for those who do not know, is a necrophile and (suspected) serial killer from Plainfield, Wisconsin, USA. He has 2 confirmed murders with at minimum 1 more suspected murder, (this third murder would classify him as a serial killer). He also robbed multiple graves and made various utensils, furniture, and clothing from the remains of the dead. His impact on the cultural zeitgeist is enormous - Gein was the inspiration for Norman Bates in the movie Psycho, as well as Leatherface in the Texas Chainsaw Massacre and Buffalo Bill in the movie Silence of the Lambs (although Buffalo Bill is more so an amalgamation of several killers). Schechter goes throughout the entire story of Ed Gein's life, from birth to death, with a highlight on both his potential and realized crimes. Schechter goes throughout the entire story of Ed Gein's life, from birth to death, with a highlight on both his potential and confirmed crimes.I have always found Ed Gein's story to be muddled and prone to the bias of the person telling it. Some portray him as a mentally ill hermit that should be pitied for his deplorable upbringing - a product of his circumstances, essentially. Others Portray Gein as a master of manipulation, a villain who conned not only his community but those who evaluated him. They claim he got away with murder. From the opinion of this expert, the truth seems to be somewhere down the middle, and I think Harold Schechter does a good job of portraying this. He adds the often-overlooked details of Eddie's life post-capture and impact he made on the community of Plainfield, which I found to be refreshing. Schechter explores Eddie's mental illness in pretty solid depth, as well as the controversy around it. I think he generally leans a little on the sensational side, but his research is sound, and he generally takes a pretty centrist perspective on Gein. If you are interested in Ed Gein, I would say this is the best source of information that I have found on him.
