This is a case where the whole is greater than the sum of its parts. A far as short stories go, these are pretty good. Petry puts the reader in the picture with sensory details, and the stories flowed well. But most of them have a "punch line" kind of ending, which is not my preference for this kind of writing.

However, taken as a whole, I was impressed (and even moved) by the breadth of the range of Black experience contained in the stories. The first three stories are linked, and detail the life of a middle class family which is the only Black population in a suburban New York State town. Other stories depict domestic service, factory work, high school teaching, a junk dealer in Harlem and more.

The central characters in the stories are also diverse in personality, and even in skin color (black, brown, reddish brown, even white). Each story is about how the characters feel and the different ways in which they experience racial prejudice. Even the white character deals with the mirror image of this in his futile efforts to be accepted by a Black woman and her family, though the young daughter maintains a friendship with him.

Note that the stories in the second half of the book can be emotionally challenging for a reader. There is a case of spousal abuse, a situation where high school boys abuse a female classmate, the avoidable death of a racist white woman, a race riot and more. The "n-word" appears frequently.

In the end, I found this reading experience moving and compelling, even if there was nothing really new to me. But the stories as a whole focus on the emotional burden constituted by being Black in this country. No character in the stories escapes this burden, but each one has an individual way of dealing with it. To me, reading "Miss Muriel" was feeling experience rather than an intellectual one, and Petry has created a book I find memorable.
