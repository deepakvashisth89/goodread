The second part of The Death of the Heart, titled (not without irony) “The Flesh,” opens with the first intimations of spring, early in March, when “the trees are not yet budding - but the senses receive an intimation, an intimation so fine, yet striking in so directly, that this appears a movement in one's own spirit. This exalts whatever feeling is in the heart."

“No moment in human experience approaches in its intensity this experience of the solitary earth's. The later phases of spring, when her foot is in at the door, are met with a conventional gaiety. But her first unavowed presence is disconcerting; silences fall in company - the wish to be either alone or with a lover is avowed by some look or spontaneous movement - the window being thrown open, the glance away up the street. In cities the traffic lightens and quickens; even buildings take such a feeling of depth that the streets might be rides cut through a wood. What is happening is only acknowledged between strangers, by looks, or between lovers. Unwritten poetry twists the hearts of people in their thirties. To the person out walking that first evening of spring, nothing appears inanimate, nothing not sentient: darkening chimneys, viaducts, villas, glass-and-steel factories, chain stores seem to strike as deep as natural rocks, seem not only to exist but to dream.”

The brilliance of this passage lurks in that ever-so-quotable line: “Unwritten poetry twists the hearts of people in their thirties.” Because the protagonist of , Portia, is sixteen. And so we know immediately, coming upon this line, buried in a paragraph supposedly about how people in general experience the coming of spring, that we are not reading about people in general, but about Anna. Anna, who is “already half way through a woman’s checked, puzzled life.”

Such subtlety characterizes every page of this extraordinary novel. The care with which Elizabeth Bowen draws her characters and their worlds allows us to inhabit a perspective that is neither exactly in nor out of her characters’ minds. We see Anna and Portia from a distance, a stranger crossing a park or peering out of a lit window; we see the shadows they cast upon each other’s hearts; we see the world impressed upon them, and their selves impressed upon their worlds—and we are left with a paradox: that the human heart is unknowable and also utterly familiar, that to fall in love is at once totally singular and inescapably universal, that to half-understand is the only genre of understanding available to us.
