Stephen King is one of the great fiction writers of our age and probably the greatest horror/thriller writer of all time. He makes writing look easy, especially when he seems to pump out a bestseller every year. Fortunately for us, King has also provided the tools that he thinks writers need to pursue the craft. However, writing may not be for everyone. King claims that his prowess was part naturally ordained and part extensive personal training. This underpins his two-part thesis of good writing:
The first is that good writing consists of mastering the fundamentals (vocabulary, grammar, the elements of style) and then filling the third level of your toolbox with the right instruments. The second is that while it is impossible to make a competent writer out of a bad writer, and while it is equally impossible to make a great writer out of a good one, it possible, with lots of hard work, dedication, and timely help, to make a good writer out of a merely competent one.
Of course, this is controversial, but I think it is fair. The craft of writing is like many other skills: some are more predisposed than others, and some are willing to work extensively to not just be naturally good, but to be great. Athletes come to mind as a good comparator. Often, you can tell at quite a young age whether they have the capability to go pro.

Alongside writing every day for a couple of hours, King states that “reading is the creative centre of a writer’s life.” This seems rather obvious, but it is surprising how often wannabe writers of various kinds do not read that much. I have noticed the importance of reading myself as I have started writing more extensively over the last half-year. I won't say that I am a good writer just yet, but I am ok. I think this is because I have read hundreds of books, which has enabled me to accrue a repertoire of writing styles and information that I can easily draw upon when I need it. This is something others simply can not do if they have not read a lot.

One critique I have of this book is that it is written with a fiction writer in mind. For those who write non-fiction, like me, some sections are merely interesting rather than instructive. That said, there are sections that remain highly pertinent to all writers. The section on revising and editing one’s work was very good. One lesson that will stick with me, and has stuck with King since he was told it as a young writer, is that your first proper draft is what you have initially written minus 10 per cent of the word count. If you apply this rule, you will find that a lot of the fluff is cut out, and the piece you are writing becomes much sharper.

This book is not all writing theory; it is also part memoir. King outlines the key moments of his life that either directly or tangentially impacted his journey to becoming one of the world’s most famous authors. This provides practical grounding for his writing theory by demonstrating that he was skilled at writing from a young age and toiled away for decades in his spare time before making it big. At the end of the book, he also recounts the time he was almost killed when a car hit him. It was his love for writing that helped him recover. Despite its macabre origins, it was a nice ending. It reminded me of a Nietzsche quote made famous by Viktor Frankl in : “He who has a why can bear almost any how.”
