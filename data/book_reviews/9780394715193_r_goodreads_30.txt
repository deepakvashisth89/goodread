For the first few pages, I worried that Hyde had built his book on the shakiest ground.

1. We call an artist's abilities "a gift."

2. Witnessing a beautiful artwork feels like "a gift."

3. Therefore, these "gifts" must be conceptually linked. Artists have gifts, and they give us gifts in turn. Gifts everywhere.

I wasn't sold. The book's title -- indeed, its whole premise -- seemed like a dubious pun. A loose, metaphorical connection. And neither "gift" seemed terribly similar to a literal gift, like a birthday gift or a wedding gift.

But I was wrong. Lewis unites these ideas by unpacking the anthropology of "gift cultures," which follow these rules:

1. In a gift culture, To keep a gift indefinitely is to commit a kind of theft. You must pass it on (or pass on a similar gift.)

2. In a gift culture, For example, if one clan is given cattle as part of a wedding ritual, they should eat the cattle. To keep the animals alive, milk them, and profit off of the milk would be a kind of theft, a deep violation.

3. These two tenets may seem to contradict each other, but they don't, because in a gift culture, For example, the bones of consumed animals are typically offered as gifts to the earth or to the gods. Thus, what might seem like a one-way path to us is in fact a kind of cycle: "these gifts came from the gods, and we give them back to the gods."

4. In a gift culture, An exchange of favors between two parties does not count. For Hyde's purposes, birthday gifts aren't really gifts. They must belong to a web of gifting.

5. In a gift culture, At one point Hyde busts out a dizzying diagram for one culture's ritual of wedding gifts. The aunts of the bride must give fish to the sisters of the groom, who in turn give grain to the cousins of the bride... and so on. The choreography is elaborate.

6. In a gift culture, This is a deeply supernatural idea. Very different from, say, the market-based idea of "earning interest."

7. In a gift culture, The word "economy" is of course ill-used here, because it carries a lot of marketplace baggage. But the point is that gifts are not a weird interruption to the normal pattern of consumption (as in the case of birthday gifts). They the pattern.

Put it all together, and the artist in a gift culture has a clear purpose: to accept the gift of divine inspiration, and then pass it onward as the gift of your work, which is no longer "yours," but now moves through the world in lovely and mysterious cycles.

The artist in a market culture, meanwhile, is caught in a familiar hustle. First, turn your thoughts and feelings and skills into commodities; second, advertise these creations in the marketplace alongside "competitor" products; and finally, hoard the spoils (if there are any).

In so many words: sell your soul.

To Hyde's credit, he's not totally sentimental about gift cultures. He understands the appeal of market economies. (I, for one, enjoy my comfy shoes and my well-insulated home.) But by explaining the unfamiliar workings of a gift economy, Hyde sets up a vivid contrast, casting some of the marketplace's shortcomings in a new light.

All in all: a good and worthy book.

On a final note: I love the some of the reviews on here. "Hyde is offering an alternative to the poisonous logic of the market. But I have trouble understanding it. Two stars."

What better way to prove Hyde's point than by insisting that a book can be quantified on a five-star scale?
