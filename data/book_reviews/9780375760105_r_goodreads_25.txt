Moll Flanders (not her real name) wants you to believe she regrets every shilling she ever stole. But the reader will shortly realize that her "confession" is being made for appearance's sake. Moll accumulates husbands, abandons children with barely a thought, and recounts her best pickpocketing scores with relish. She understands that a woman without money in late seventeenth-century England is a woman without a self, and she sets about acquiring both with a methodical shrewdness. She exhibits the patience of someone working a long con. The moralizing passages feel pasted on, which I suspect they were.Defoe doesn't seem to have decided whether he was writing a cautionary tale or a how-to guide, and the friction between the two is what gives the book its strange power. Moll's repentance does not feel authentic, and I don't think it's supposed to. Defoe was a Dissenter who knew exactly what real spiritual rebirth looked like, and what he gives us is the counterfeit version, possibly on purpose. For a book published in 1722, it surprised me how modern the book feels (quaint prose aside), and it is a propulsive read. It’s arguably an early feminist novel, which I quite enjoyed.
