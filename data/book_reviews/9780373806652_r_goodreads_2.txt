I swear I meant this summary to be only three lines -- she's disdainful of him, he has a secret identity, everything works out perfectly in the end -- but I can't not be verbose about the details.

Spoiler-filled summary:

The heroine, president of a data processing firm, plans to take revenge on her (married) ex-fiancé by allowing him to think she's meeting him for a romantic assignation in a resort town in the Rockies, when she actually plans to laugh in his face and reject him. (The ex had dumped her after her father's funeral when he realized that the firm she was inheriting was in deep financial trouble. The heroine has since turned the business around.)

While waiting for the ex to arrive, she meets the owner of a local art gallery. She's attracted (and bored) enough to hang out with him, even though she's openly disdainful of the hero's lack of entrepreneurial drive. He fishes when he wants, skis when he can, throws pottery as hobby, and only opens the gallery for business when he's in the mood. While the hero is clearly living a millennial's #bestlife, this vintage heroine's ideal man would be a more ethical .

The H tries to seduce her out of meeting the ex. He tries to debate her out of meeting the ex. Eventually -- on her own, citing her concerns about potential impact on the poor, unsuspecting wife -- the heroine calls off the assignation and tells the irate ex over the phone not to show up because she had only been planning vengeance. On her way out of town, she drops by the hero's home to find him haggard (he had been up all night fretting about her decision). They end up having sex and the poor guy drops off to sleep immediately afterward, giving the heroine the opportunity to sneak off because she thinks they're "from two different worlds."

Back in the bustling business metropolis of Denver, the heroine is shocked when her ex calls her office to notify her that he is in the process of arranging a hostile takeover of her firm and he had asked to meet her in the mountains only to see if he could seduce her into merging amicably. (Historical note: leveraged buyouts and corporate takeovers got a lot of media attention in the 1980s. See .)

The heroine is panicking because she has no idea how to fight off a buyout, but first she has to host a party in her home that evening because hosting parties is what JAK heroines do. (Why is "hostess with the mostest" not part of my standard JAK checklist?)

Of course, the hero crashes the party. Wearing a tailored business suit and knowing all about the ex's nefarious plans, he offers to help the heroine fight the takeover. When she condescendingly asks what a part-time gallery owner/fisherman/ski bum/potter could do to help, he intimidates her by threatening to tell her company's executive staff she was meeting the opposition on the sly...if she doesn't agree to continue their affair. (It's worth noting this blackmail threat is only a demonstration. He doesn't actually blackmail her. Once she acknowledges that he can be ruthless, he pulls back into nice-guy mode.)

Reeling, she accepts his offer to help and takes him to an emergency management meeting the next day. When she announces that their firm is the target of a hostile takeover, everyone glares accusingly at the composed hero. Then they're surprised when the heroine announces the H will be helping them to stave off the attack.



It turns out the hero is a noted corporate raider in the Rocky Mountain empire who suddenly sold off his interests and retired 18 months earlier, just when the heroine (who had only recently returned to Colorado from Texas) was busy grieving her father's death, recovering from her broken engagement and getting started as CEO of the firm. Surprise! Not only is he of her world, he was so successful at it, he could own her world.

After this revelation, the protagonists get down to business saving the firm. Mr Corporate Raider pulls a psychological maneuver where he takes the heroine out to dinner at the same restaurant her ex and his wife are dining at and announces they're engaged. This is intended to let the OM know the hero can't be bought or bribed. Later that night, he seduces the heroine into agreeing to make the engagement real.

Two days later, the ex's firm calls off their takeover bid. Basically, the solution was for the heroine's firm to threaten to leverage their pristine and underused credit to buy their own shares back, making the takeover a more expensive proposition for the aggressive ex. The less-desirable option floated was possibly finding a third company for a friendly merger. Both of these options probably felt really educational to 14-year-old me, but four decades later they look like obvious choices the heroine should have been able to reach on her own. Her lack of competence in this area undermines the progressive nature of having a heroine who is a top-level business executive.

Now that the data processing firm is safe, the H & h talk about their relationship. She thinks she has simply been a catalyst to bring him out of the mountains and back into the cutthroat business world he once enjoyed, and that he'll discard her now she's no longer serving that purpose. He thinks she's worried he'll now use her firm as the cornerstone to rebuild the business empire he discarded. They clear the air with mutual trust and kisses, and HEA

The most surprising element of this story is that the heroine's snooty attitude never gets thrown back in her face, the way it would in a vintage HP. The hero doesn't show up to taunt her about her misconceptions while squiring some OW around to ritzy nightclubs; he shows up to prove he can be the kind of man the heroine claims she wants. Fortunately, that lifestyle turns out to be something he also wants. He only gave it up 18 months ago because he thought his ambition was empty and destructive. With the heroine as the center of his universe, he's no longer concerned he'll fall into that trap.

Although isn't JAK's first book for Silhouette (that's ), it is the debut book for the Desire imprint, and I can see the editors were interested in keeping the narrative tight and the storyline as progressive as possible. This effort is more polished than Krentz's books for MacFadden and Dell/Candlelight.
