Premise: The world is more or less as we know it, except now there's an influx of weird magical energy and people from an unknown dimension that we just call Shadow as a catch all. So there's dark elves, dragons, arcane magic, possibly new gods -- only most people can't really see this. But a few special humans can and also some of the shadowkind who come through are good, so good people and shadowkind have to fight back against the bad people and shadowkind.Or something, because this book has absolute certainty about some of what it wants to do, like: all the chapter headings are in a spraypaint graffiti style, all the numbers are outlined in dripping spraypaint dots, etc. -- the book absolutely knows it wants to be "urban" in that very limited aesthetic sense.But I'd really love to ask the designers to give a one-line description of what the PCs are supposed to do here. Like in D&D, the core activity is "kill things and take their stuff"; in Call of Cthulhu, it's "investigate the sanity-shredding monsters until you're dead or mad." What are the PCs supposed to do here and why? The ad-copy on the back says "sling fireballs and dodge bullets," and while that's a good summation of the tropes here (magic, but in the modern day!), it doesn't really tell me anything about the theme, motif, meaning, etc.Now some of this could go back to this being itself a little generic, and there's some GM advice about deciding on the scope of the world. Do you want to tell a story in your hometown or maybe in a major city or maybe on a global stage? You and your players can decide. Or maybe even a better example of how this book thinks of itself as a toolbox: in this world, there's magic, but not psionics, and yet, there's several pages about psionic rules and organizations, because they know someone will pick this up and say "oh, but I wanted to play a telepath!" And, like, I'm all for giving players options -- well, theoretically I'm all for it, because I think designers need to recognize how options are also traps and diversions. If you have an idea for a game, tell me that idea, and if it doesn't fit, I can change it; but if you don't give me your idea to start with, what are we even talking about?So yeah, I disliked this campaign idea when it was outlined in the core D20 Modern book because it seemed like a rewarmed meal of D&D; and for the first few chapters, reading about character options, I was actually reconsidering that. Maybe it would be fun to be a dark elf driver for a heist with a magic car. (Though that said, I was left scratching my head over some of these options, like why do we need at least two different magic-tech people?)But then... nothing in particular rises up from out of this chaos. Here's an organization devoted to an evil god of death -- should we fight that? (Also, what does it mean that these deities from Greyhawk are coming into this world? What happens if any modern-day religion gets a whiff of this real power coming from some supposed god?) Here's a good god, here's a crime syndicate, here's an evil corporation, here's a circus of halflings. But, like, what do we do?I've talked before about how rules help channel play by rewarding certain actions (or even just having rules about certain actions), so even a generic game has some themes in it; and yet a truly successful game has a convergence of theme in the text and in the game system. And I can't even say if this is successful because the text is so loose.OK, but that said: is there something positive here worth learning or taking? Well, I like some of the monsters, like the living dumpster and the art for it, that are just slight twists from our world. And I think there might be something interesting about a campaign that isn't out-and-out fantasy (though I might prefer something like "you have to clean up this mess before the normies figure it out").
