I began this book with genuine enthusiasm. The early pages drew me into their world, and I was fascinated by the sisters’ early years. There is something compelling about watching a life unfold through someone’s own voice—especially when those words were never meant to be shared publicly. I have a real fondness for letters, which offer an intimacy and honesty you rarely find elsewhere.

I also want to acknowledge the incredible amount of work Barker has invested. Her research is extensive and unmistakably shaped by her background as a historian. While admirable, this level of detail results in a dense and rather exhaustive reading experience. The chronological order makes sense, but without titles, subheadings, or thematic markers, the book often feels like a continuous stream of letters with little narrative shaping.

I reached 200/500 pages before I realised my momentum had stalled. The letters were becoming repetitive, and the experience increasingly heavy. Even before the family’s tragedies appear on the page, there is a sadness whenever the sisters mention their futures—the ages they expect to reach, and the plans they assume they will have time for. And I know the most emotional, pivotal letters must be buried somewhere later in the book, but since the story has already gotten quite heavy, I think I need to return to this book another time. Despite my criticisms, I do agree with many notes of the highly rated reviews.

Ultimately, the lack of structure is making the book more exhausting than engaging. Barker’s occasional explanatory notes help, but they can’t offset the flatness created by the span of 30 years of unbroken correspondence. Picking this up is starting to feel like a chore, even though I can see the links to their published works, and appreciate the immense historical value of the material.

[There are also 30+ pages of photographs and illustrations at the end of my digital copy, which I’m keen to flip through, too, so I hope someday I can finish this book.]

On a personal note, having read as much as I did, I’m starting to realise I identify most with Emily.
