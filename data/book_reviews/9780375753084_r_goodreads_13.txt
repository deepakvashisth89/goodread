Life at the Top: Iranian Version

For an inside look at the Iranian elite before Ayatollah Khomeini crashed the gates and dynamited the house, you can’t do better than this. (Two other books I’ve reviewed are “Daughter of Persia” by a woman who must have been related to the present author, and “Under Five Shahs” by a straight-arrow general from a very elite family. The former Empress also put out a book.) This author came from one of the highest level families from the Qajar Dynasty times (which came to an end in 1925). The erstwhile prince was born with a silver spoon in his mouth in 1917. For me, such people are neither glamorous or admirable. It all depends on what they do in life.

Manucher’s father had many wives and many children, not to mention vast properties in several parts of Iran. At the age of nine, Manucher was sent to study in England and he remained there for 14 years, returning only at the age of 23. He learned perfect English and absorbed a lot of English culture, but he faced a lot of prejudice. The book informs readers about his career in the oil industry, working in the Iranian government oil bureaucracy, eventually rising to the top post. Along the way he made many friends (and probably many enemies too). He knew or was related to almost everyone in the halls of power including the Shah. This shows us that there was not much room at the top in Iran—the ruling circle was extremely small with a vast body of the masses below. The central character of the political crisis of 1952-53, Prime Minister Mossadegh, was a close relative as were many other important political and financial figures.

The author was no doubt something of a playboy and ladies’ man, marrying and divorcing three foreign wives, admiring many others along the way. He led a charmed life, living in luxury, always finding support from some privileged character at the right moment. He knew everyone who counted. Does this appeal to you? He certainly saw and was able to relate how the British exploited Iran, in many ways more than in their own colonies. The deals they made for oil were outrageously one-sided, but British power enforced them. Close to the center of power for years, Manucher became ambassador to Venezuela in the 1970s, after a distinguished career despite having to duck and dodge with the changes of politics in Iran. When the Shah fled and the government fell amidst chaos and bloodshed, Manucher himself barely managed to escape to Europe and thence to Venezuela, where he had friends.

I felt that this was an expansive man, hotheaded but ultimately tolerant, the eternal “man about town” who was not too fond of details. The book is full of small exaggerations which made me feel that there could have been bigger ones too. [*p.358 Iran as a country of a million square miles—actually 636,000. *pp.352-53 a gross misrepresentation of the first British-Afghan war 1838-42. *p.397 exaggerated comments about Haile Selassie of Ethiopia, etc. etc.] On the other hand, you will not find much criticism of the ubiquitous secret police SAVAK and only scattered references to the widespread poverty that lay all around him. He did note that as oil brought more wealth to Iran, people poured into Tehran from the countryside. They often did not know how to use a telephone, a fridge, or drive a vehicle. Many were illiterate. Such was the gap between the European-educated top families and most of the rest.
This is a very revealing book about Iran up to 1980. It shows the inner workings of the oil industry and its operations in Iran and a vast amount of personal experience with people in the halls of power. The 480 pages may be too much for some people, but with some extrapolation you can get the feel of what Iran must have been like in those days from the pen of one who played his privileged part.
