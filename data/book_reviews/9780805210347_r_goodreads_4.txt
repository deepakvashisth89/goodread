This book provides a helpful short introduction to the Zohar by one of the great luminaries of the twentieth century, Gershom Scholem, as well as a short miscellany of some of its content.It serves its purpose well, but I have to say I do not particularly resonate with the material. The Zohar lies at the intersection between mysticism (Plotinus, Pseudo-Dionysius, Nagarjuna) and esotericism (Sethian Gnosticism, Hermes Terismigistus), and the former interests me, while the latter, by and large, does not.One can think, as a point of reference for its effect, of the Book of Revelation, which appears on the surface to contain some kind of code or structure, but on closer analysis, no real underlying sense can be found, even if individual moments are striking or uncanny. And like Revelation, the Zohar is populated with material that was streamed directly from the unconscious. As a wise psychologist friend of mine once observed, at some point one learns that material is unconscious for a reason.I have yet to see the esoteric text where I did not come away with the impression that it was the author themselves who was, first and foremost, confused, and a bit lost in the projection of their own unconscious. That seems to me to be part of its method, evoking a great inner bewilderment, and then wandering through its labyrinth, looking for moments of epiphany. I prefer an approach that retains a more level head.
