Exhaustive in both style and scope,City of Godis a book that really takes some reading. Not only is it long, but Augustine attempts to leave no stone unturned in his examination of the various topics he touches upon.City of Godis often pitched as Augustine’s answer to the claim that the growing belief in Christianity was responsible for the decline of the Western Roman Empire and the sack of the city of Rome. The logic being that as more people came to believe in Christianity, less people worshipped the old pagan gods. As a result, the pagan gods ceased to protect the city.In reality,City of Godis about much more than that. It does begin with this topic, and continues discussing it for the first ten books, but thereafter it moves onto pastures new. The extensive takedowns of Varro, and the measured affection for Porphyry are certainly interesting in their own way (Augustine considered Platonism to be the closest to the truth of any of the pagan schools of philosophy), but the text really comes into its own when the topic moves onto Biblical exegesis.It’s fair to say that not all of Augustine’s ideas are accepted by modern Christians, but Augustine’s analyses - while not resolving all issues - set the terms of the debate for centuries to come.Some parts of City of God are certainly harder work than others. For example, the extensive explanations of how various prophecies in the Old Testament are precursors to the coming of Jesus - this section was sometimes dull, but not because the topic was dull - it’s just that the arguments have become very commonly repeated. You may even have heard a number of the same arguments from a pulpit. When City of God is dull, it’s more likely to be because you are reading something you've already heard before. That is the extent of Augustine's influence - even today, people are quoting some of his theories without realising it. For an audience new to these ideas, these sections would likely have been fascinating.The common thread through this whole volume is the idea of the City of God (founded by the line of Seth) and the City of Man (founded by the line of Cain). This idea weaves itself in and out of very disparate topics, but always returns as a leitmotif to tie everything together thematically. Those residing in the City of God love God above all, even above their own self. In the City of Man, the people love their own self above all, even above God. Only God knows who belongs to which city, and the movement between them is a spiritual rather than a physical process.
