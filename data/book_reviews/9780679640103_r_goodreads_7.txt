Henry Brooks Adams was the grandson and great-grandson of United States presidents, one of whom, John Adams, is among the nation’s founding fathers. Henry’s father, Charles Francis Adams, was a lawyer, diplomat and author, member of the House of Representatives, and Minister to Great Britain throughout the U.S. Civil War. The Education of Henry Adams is an autobiography containing Adams’s observations on education, history, art, politics, war, diplomacy, and religion, which culminates in an abstruse theory of history.

From my perspective, the most interesting part of this memoir are Adams’s reflections on his mid-nineteenth century Boston and Quincy Massachusetts childhood, his undergraduate years at Harvard followed by experiences as private secretary to his father during the Civil War. He witnessed first-hand the machinations of a British government precariously straddling the fence on which side to support until success on the battlefield moved the course of history in favor of the Union.

Following the Civil War, Adams spent several years as a political journalist in Washington D.C. during the Johnson and Grant administrations. Then, disillusioned by the seamy side of politics, Adams accepted the position of Assistant professor of history at Harvard and editor of the North American Review.

There is a lacuna of about twenty years in the narrative, a period that includes Adams’s marriage, and one must go outside the text of the autobiography to account for the hiatus. After almost thirteen years of marriage, Marian (Clover) Hooper Adams killed herself. According to biographical material, his wife’s suicide sent Adams into a deep depression from which he never fully recovered.
In his autobiography, Adams never mentions his wife directly, but rather obliquely by reference to the Adams Memorial in Rock Creek Cemetery that was designed by Augustus Saint-Gaudens, a noted American sculptor and a friend of Adams and his wife.

The remainder of the biography consists of Adams’s search for order and meaning in an increasingly complex and chaotic world. For me, his Dynamic Theory of History, based on the Second Law of Thermodynamics, is an intellectual rabbit hole, and I did not care to follow him. However, given his education, life experience, and intellectual gifts, I’m not surprised that he chose to travel that perplexing path to its conclusion.
