UPDATED WITH REVIEW OF VOLUME 2

Review of volume 1, i.e. the 19 stories from the 1837 edition:

★★★★☆

After reading and loving The Scarlet Letter last month I decided I should get a taste of Hawthorne's short fiction. This was his first story collection and second book, the tales "twice-told" only in the sense that they had already been published individually in periodicals prior to being collected here.

Single-author story collections often fall into one of two traps: either the selections are too similar and the experience becomes same-y, or they're too disparate and it feels disjointed. If I had to choose one or the other I'd pick the latter, and that's very much where Hawthorne falls. Actually I'd say there are three main modes you can sort these stories into, give or take a handful of edge cases. There are the classically Hawthornian spooky, symbol-laden, psychological mini-gothics, often but not always with a colonial American setting, which are generally the best and certainly what I'm most interested in:

"The Wedding-Knell"
"The Minister's Black Veil"
"The Gentle Boy"
"Wakefield"
"The Prophetic Pictures"
"The Hollow of the Three Hills"

Then there are pieces I'd put more in the category of "yarns" or fables—lighter in tone, more overtly fantastical or at least implausible in subject matter, usually with a clear moral hammered home firmly at the end. These are typically entertaining enough (give or take the occasional wince-inducing 19th-century comment on race or gender), though pretty insubstantial:

"The Gray Champion"
"The Maypole of Merry Mount"
"Mr. Higginbotham's Catastrophe"
"The Great Carbuncle"
"David Swan"
"The Vision of the Fountain"
"Fancy's Show-Box"
"Dr. Heidegger's Experiment"

And finally there are what I call the "sketches," basically just imaginative musings on contemporary New England life structured around a loose creative conceit: the daily encounters of a tollbooth operator, scenes of town life through the eyes of a young girl, a monologue from a sentient water pump who moonlights as a temperance movement propagandist. These are entirely skippable, though at least mildly informative as historical documents if you're interested in day-to-day life in the pre-Civil War U.S. (I am):

"Sunday at Home"
"Little Annie's Ramble"
"A Rill from the Town-Pump"
"Sights from a Steeple"
"The Toll-Gatherer's Day"

As you can see from this breakdown, the American gothic Hawthorne of and is not actually the predominant one here. Like his contemporary (and complimentary reviewer of this very collection) Poe, who wrote about as many satires and adventure tales as he did his more iconic horror stories, the early Hawthorne wore a number of authorial hats. It's lucky for American literature that he had the time and good sense to figure out what he was most skilled at and refine his focus.

Hawthorne's best works are the ones which feel both parable-like and inconclusive. By far the standouts for me in this volume are the rightfully-famous "Wakefield," about the existential emptiness of husbands who get off on pranking their wives, and the unnervingly ambiguous "The Minister's Black Veil." It's easy to see, in stories like these, why Melville was so in awe of Hawthorne, and what tricks he picked up from him and perfected.

★★★☆☆

It turns out I could have just ended my reading after volume 1 without missing any significant highlights. The 1841 edition of adds an additional 17 stories to the table of contents, but none of them rise to the standards of the best of the first set. You don't even really get the satisfaction of watching Hawthorne develop as a writer, since, oddly, many of the pieces here were in fact composed earlier in his career than those of the first batch. An indication, maybe, that Hawthorne or his publisher knew these weren't the cream of the crop, but decided they'd at least do to pad out a reissue.

The yarns and sketches predominate here, with some of the latter being especially weak: a transparent attempt to meet a word count with some halfhearted musings about snow (), a retrospective on the year 1839 in the guise of an encounter between the anthropomorphized Old Year and the New (complete with product placements for Hawthorne's employer, the Salem ), and so on. Thankfully even the poorest efforts tend to have at least one or two really striking passages, where the more artistic or philosophical Hawthorne breaks free from the constraints of his conceit and ensures that the reading isn't a total waste of time. (As a perennial insomniac, I will say I appreciated "The Haunted Mind," about the pleasures and pains of being awake at 2 am. "Footprints on the Seashore," documenting a solitary day at the beach, is another standout—it's Transcendentalism without the self-importance of Thoreau or Emerson.)

There are only a small handful of truly gothic tales here, the most notable being the four-part opening story "Legends of the Province-House." Each part is set in the eponymous Boston mansion (once the official residence of Massachusetts' colonial governors, now long since torn down), with a framing device about how the author comes to learn what he's relating to us. There are some good premises here (a spooky portrait presaging Wilde, an eerily symbolic masquerade ball and a second ball which serves as a smallpox super-spreader event, both anticipating and possibly inspiring Poe), though they're hindered a bit by the number of references to the administrations of specific colonial governors and by the unsubtle patriotic messaging throughout. Other gothic-inflected pieces are the atmospheric but confusing "The White Old Maid," a vignette called "Edward Fain's Rosebud" which is mostly a showcase of Hawthorne's recurring horror of aging women, and the overlong, semi-comic story "Peter Goldthwaite's Treasure," which reads almost like a spoof of "The Fall of the House of Usher" despite apparently just managing to pre-date it.

Even after 36 Hawthorne stories, very few of them really great, at least half of them totally forgettable, and none of them as good as his most famous novel, I still do find myself strangely engaged by and drawn to his short fiction, which for better or worse is so completely different from anything I see happening in that form today. I've especially found these stories excellent bedtime reading: drowse-inducing, maybe, but also good fodder for dreams.
