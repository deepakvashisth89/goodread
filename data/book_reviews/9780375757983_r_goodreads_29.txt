Interesting insight into the experience of immigrants to America in the late 1800s and early 1900s, this book details the internal conflict of its flawed but endearing main character as he navigates feelings of displacement and disillusionment.Plot: David is born in Antomir, Russia and is encouraged to become a Talmudic scholar. However, the violent death of his mother at the hands of gentiles shatters his world. He begins to lose his faith and becomes curious about America. Upon arriving, he struggles to survive, both drawn to and repulsed by the idea of achieving status and abandoning remnants of his more traditional background. He finds some fulfillment for a time with intellectual pursuits but a chance incident makes him turn toward business, moving up in the garment industry to become a somewhat ruthless businessman. Yet his life is married by loneliness, failed romantic pursuits and a career which leaves him feeling empty. By the end of the novel he acknowledges that if he could do it again, he would have stayed a scholar rather than a businessman, but even then, we never actually see him pursuing that path. What is clear is that he is deeply unhappy despite his success.The main character David is very flawed and his life is kind of a mess. He plays dirty to get ahead, he acts superior, and he chases a lot of the wrong things. But there is still this emotional honesty in his voice and a sense of humor in the telling of the story, that you can't help but care about him. Personally, I found him kind of endearing and maybe it's because we see him grow from boy to man, but we still sort of see him as a child deeply affected by his early life experiences. David really kept me hooked to this story and it held my attention way more than I expected.The interpretation of the book, most people say, is that it is a criticism of Jewish assimilation and the pursuit of wealth and capitalism—those are the reasons why David is so unhappy. That is part of it, but I don't think it's the whole story. It seems to be a very psychological book that might also be interested in more universal aspects of human nature. There is a primary conflict between David's old self and ideals and the reality of how he actually lives his life. I can see him as someone pursing wealth as a way of coping with his early traumas and channeling his energy into something else.Despite having a lingering attachment to ideals like education, morality and justice, he does not pursue them and often expresses a negative attitude toward people who do. Yet his behavior and thoughts often give away his deeper feelings. His romantic interest in various women, for example, seem to reflect an attraction to the ideals they represent - which also dooms them to fail. In Dora, he probably sees something of his mother's devotion to her children, but it's that same devotion that leads her to choose her husband and children over him. Anna's interest in radical politics, steadfast convictions and her eventual marriage to a scholar also highlight these ideals which David explicitly has abandoned. At the beginning he dreams of being a big, wealthy man and later dreams of returning to his Antomir days, which, as he himself described, were fairly miserable in many ways. I think David is unhappy because he doesn't quite understand himself, and therefore, cannot arrange his life to be consistent with his values. It doesn't seem like any one path to happiness is portrayed as the only one. It's not as simple as advocating to be socialist over capitalist, traditional over modern, religious over atheist. Rather, I think David's story is meant to show us the importance of facing our own feelings and desires and finding peace within.
