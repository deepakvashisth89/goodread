To tell the truth, I'm not entirely certain what I was expecting from this one. Woody Guthrie is a man I knew primarily as an idea, the scrawny Okie who crossed the country several times over with nothing but the shirt on his back, a guitar and a song for every conceivable occasion - especially labor rallies and strikes. I'll even admit slightly shamefully that the only song of his I genuinely knew was "This Land is Your Land" - and I didn't even know about the excised leftist verses that the whole idea really pivoted around, the one extolling the virtues of trespassing and the ending where he stumbles across a breadline and wonders if this land trulywasmade for you and me until maybe a couple years ago. On some level, I might just have been curious to see where exactly the American folk tradition of Bob Dylan and Pete Seeger sprung from.What I got from this is a biography so unsparingly honest in its warts-and-all portrayal of Guthrie that it practically reads as a hit job. Guthrie was a true eccentric - and unfortunately, he was the kind of eccentric that relied on the kindness of those around him to survive with almost no thought given to how he'd repay that kindness in turn. He comes across here as the kind of guy far more likely to entrap you in a vicious diatribe about the failures of your own personal politics when stacked up against his personal brand of communist utopia than to help out with the dishes after dinner or take care of his own kids (or, hell, even to bathe himself). In this, his strongest legacy shows itself - Woody, for better and for worse, is one of the true forefathers of the American leftist movement.In fact, the biggest takeaway I got from this book is simply this: the left really has always been like this in America. The various communists that populate this story are people who dream big, wild ideas of freedom and equality for the working poor - and frequently, they even build the mechanisms and structures that get this work done on scale to a remarkable degree. And just about every time they get going somewhere, factional infighting and rigorous purity testing of those allowed to join the ranks fuck them up at every conceivable turn - until they finally face their most formidable opponent in Joseph McCarthy and, their numbers dwindled and fractured, they fold and collapse in the face of the Red Scare. It's a cautionary tale that my contemporaries today seem to be repeating as passionately as ever, like they're carrying on the family tradition. Were Woody one of us, I'd bet good money on him being an outspoken personality on Twitter and I mean that in the most derogatory way I can.As a person, he just seems like the type of guy best enjoyed from afar, in a crowd that's on strike in the 30s. Or maybe if you're building a dam. He's inspirational as long as he isn't in smelling range. I know there's a good degree of his later behavior that you can attribute to the unfortunate onset of Huntington's - his weirdly pushy eroticism in particular - so i don't want to seem like I'm punching a sick man for showing symptoms. (by the way, it's one thing to know that he wrote explicit erotic letters to his wife but my god, I could've lived a full life without reading lengthy excerpts of Woody Guthrie narrating the act of fucking) But even as a young man, he was remarkably awful to his first wife and kids, building his legend at their expense by leaving them to languish wherever they'd settled last while he gallivanted around the country playing Voice of the People. It's hard to tell how much of his persona was genuinely authentic and how much was a deliberate put-on that saw him play-acting out the part of a semi-literate podunk in a condescending attempt to relate to the working man.Quite frankly, if the man really is deserving of his title as the Father of American Folk Music, this biography does him a remarkable disservice. And if it's accurate? Then Woody Guthrie was kind of an embarrassing manchild. We don't need more guys like this on the left today. We already have tons.The original "This Land is Your Land" is still a pretty great song, though. He gets at least one. Best example of his style of music, though, is and remains his son Arlo's "Alice's Restaurant Massacree". It's nice he lived just long enough to hear it, at least.
