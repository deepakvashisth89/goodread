“The movement of this advancing warm air was now a little southwest breeze, where previously all flow of air had been from the northeast. With this new breeze, air which was still warmer and more moist moved in from the south along the near-by section of the original front, renewing its vigor and causing a little shower. All these new and renewed activities – winds, drizzle, and shower – were now arranged in complex but orderly fashion around a single point. As from the union of two opposite germ-cells begins a life, so from the contact of northern and southern air had sprung something which before had not been. As a new life, a focus of activity, begins to develop after its kind and grow by what it feeds on, so in the air that complex of forces began to develop and grow strong. A new storm had been born…”-	George Stewart,StormWeather is one of the most important factors of human existence. The weather can determine whether crops will grow; whether a war will be won or lost; whether an economy will thrive. In many instances, the weather decides whether people will live or die. On a more personal level, the weather dictates our comfort, the choices we make for recreation, and our health.Yet for all its preeminence, weather gets something of a bad rap. “Talking about the weather” is code for a boring conversation. The use of weather in art is often derided as a pathetic fallacy. Meanwhile, in the grotesque timeline in which we live, weather – and its boss, the climate – has become just another aspect of politics, one that we will continue to argue about uselessly until flood or fire consume us all.In George Stewart’s unique, somewhat strangeStorm, the weather is given its due. The titular event at the center of this “eco-novel” is godlike in its power, its reach, and its ability to shape the affairs of mankind.***Stormis an oddity. It is divided into twelve chapters, each chapter focusing on a single day in the life of a Pacific storm – nicknamed “Maria” – that is bearing down on California. Each of these sections is, in turn, subdivided into numbered subchapters that encompass different perspectives of the storm’s evolution and impact. In some areas, the storm will bring needed rain to end a drought. In others, it will cause floods. In the mountains, there will be snow.The main character – no surprises here – is “Maria,” whose birth, travel, and eventual demise is lovingly described in great scientific detail. Entire pages are devoted to describing its path, as well as the way it interacts with other systems.Beyond the storm itself, Stewart has included a long list of supporting actors, some of them humans, some of them animals, and one of them a cedar tree with a backstory starting in 1579.With regard to humans, we meet the superintendent of roads for Donner Pass, a number of snowplow drivers, a general from the First World War who is now in charge of a system of dams, and a lineman sent out to repair a downed power line. Some of the people we meet only briefly before they disappear. Others, such as the Junior Meteorologist at the Weather Bureau – it is telling that many men in the book are known by their titles only – keep reoccurring. None of the men and women involved are meant to be fully-realized, three-dimensional beings. They are symbols.Not content with the viewpoint of homo sapiens alone, Stewart takes us inside the minds of the local wildlife, including a boar and a coyote. There is also a paragraph devoted to an owl who ends up electrocuted. Like I said,Stormis a bit weird.For objects both animate and inanimate, Stewart is obsessed with extended causal chains. One small thing happens over there and – eventually – something big happens over here. He never explicitly mentions a butterfly’s wings, but he is definitely on that wavelength.***Stormwas published in 1941, with war raging in Europe and Asia, and the United States still on the outside looking in.I mention this because there is a real quaintness in Stewart’s depictions of plow drivers who live in barracks like soldiers; of weathermen plotting storm tracks on a map by hand, based on a half-dozen data points; of newspapers turning a forecast into a banner headline. This was an age in which commercial air travel was quite dangerous. An age where people worried incessantly about long distance “tolls.” An age when technologies that no longer exist in our lives were held in awe.There is a timelessness to Stewart’s message – as I’ll touch on below – that is somewhat diminished by the particularity of its setting. Still, among other things,Stormworks as a slice-of-life of a vanished time, when things were simpler, though not necessarily better.***The whole purpose of Stewart’s exercise is to get the reader thinking about the innumerable effects of a storm. Many ofStorm’s set-pieces revolve around the consequences that attend to the weather outside one’s door. Sometimes Stewart is a bit hyperbolic, with huge decisions being based on a single day’s forecast. At one point, for instance, a farmer chooses to end his own life when it seems like there is going to be no rain. Another person decides not to buy a car because it seems like it will rain.Overall, though, Stewart’s point is well taken. Indeed, when you look past the oft-bizarre presentation – the macabre,Final Destination-like deaths; the brief snatches of out-of-context dialogue; the time spent within the mind-space of a boar –Stormstarts to feel more relevant than ever.The long-term climate is changing, which also changes the short-term weather. Droughts are drier; heat is hotter; rains are rainier; and storms are stormier. Where I live, the last fortnight has been neatly divided into several days in the thirties, several days in the high eighties, and a tornado warning in between. This is what we have to look forward to, and it will cost us in terms of money, property, and lives.
