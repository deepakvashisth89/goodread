I was shocked and outraged by The Jungle, but I was probably even more shocked that I've lived my life up until this point only vaguely aware of this book. I had heard of Upton Sinclair and knew the title of his most famous work, but nobody ever told me what it was about or how truly horrifying it is.

The Jungle was heavily censored for decades because Sinclair uncovered the atrocities happening in early 20th century Packingtown, Chicago, and in other places like it. Telling the heart-wrenching story of a family of Lithuanian immigrants who come to America hoping for a better life, he exposes the tremendous poverty and disgusting living and working conditions these people lived in.

I’ve read books about tremendous poverty before— the ones that come with a grain of hope that with hard work you can prosper () and the kind that feel like you are trapped in a rigged system without hope of escaping. This is the latter. Sinclair didn't write this book to sell hope or optimism; it is a furious clarion call to change a broken society.

We see immigrants swindled by hidden clauses in contracts they can’t read, and by bosses who leech every bit of time, money and health from them. We see young children forced out of school and into work so their family doesn't starve. And, alongside this, we see a select few Americans living in the most extravagant, gratuitous luxury while those beneath them wither away.

It also paints a dire and revolting picture of the state of the food industry in the early 20th century. Some passages are hard to read without gagging.

While the book is over a hundred years old and, in many ways, the world has seen much in the way of reforms since then, I was deeply affected by Jurgis and his story. I couldn't stop talking about the book to anyone who would listen. I've been annoying about it. But is so compelling because it's both a jaw-dropping look at injustice and an emotive tale of a family striving to get by.
