I picked this up at my local Japanese bookshop since I have seen Karl Poppers work referenced by authors that I have been reading from recently like David Deutsch and Nassim Taleb. His idea of falsifiability as the criterion of demarcation is super simple yet powerful: a theory must be open to being proven wrong to count as science. What stood out is how this applies beyond physics to fields like politics and economics.His view on trial and error also resonated.Stronger theories are more exposed to criticism because they cover more ground and make bolder claims. This does not make them weaker, it makes them better. Einsteins theory replaced Newtons because it explained more, covered more variables and stood up to greater scrutiny. Now this does not mean Newtons theories are suddenly invalid but they were a stepping stone that paved the way for Einstein. Of course as a consequence of this idea it means Einsteins theories are also tentative in a sense since if a better explanatory theory comes along it replaces his theory too at it was simply the best theory for the moment.Some parts were tough to follow, especially when he referenced older thinkers. Since it is a collection of essays, the flow is not always smooth, but the key ideas come through. Some essays repeat the same ideas in different ways, but this repetition helps reinforce the concepts.Popper challenges ideas such as inductivism, empiricism etc. This book clarified what science really is: not an absolute truth or unquestionable authority, but a process of making conjectures and exposing them to criticism. It is always provisional and open to revision.He even questions the dialectical school of thought, which tries to evade criticism by absorbing it in a way that avoids genuine refutability, a subtle but important distinction from his own emphasis on open criticism.He also dives into the role of language and logic in gaining insight about the world. This part was quite fascinating having learned a new language later in life I was absorbed in the theoretical aspects of how language maps to reality.it seems to be a combination of reason, observation that results in explanatory power of theories. His engagement with figures like Bacon and Kant is sharp and thought provoking.Overall for me the key takeaway amongst many which I can’t think of at the moment is is this: to get closer to truth which is by no means manifest, we need three things:1. imagination,2. trial and error3. the gradual discovery of our prejudices through imagination, trial and error, and critical discussion.
