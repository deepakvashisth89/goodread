Book #6 (re) read for Black History Month, 2025."This life could only be my own, which in order to transform Giovanni's, must first become a part of Giovanni's room" (Baldwin 88).I first read this in 2016, one of the last of James Baldwin's novels I read. I remember friends who read it warned me it wasn't just a novel, but a supreme masterpiece of fiction.In this 2025 reread, it was even more shattering than the first time I read it. This time it as a visceral gut punch of love and rage as I wanted to love both Giovanni and David with everything I had.It is a book not for the faint of heart. Perhaps it is James Baldwin's truest masterpiece in the highest order.I unpacked themes of violent and obsessive love, ahead of its time in its depiction of polyamorous relationships both physical and emotional.David, an American expatriate finds himself in Paris torn between the icy blond Hella and the magnetic and needy Giovanni, himself a tortured Italian expatriate in Paris.It's also a novel that unpacks internal homophobia, the fear of aging, the fear of loneliness and not being seen or worth something to someone.It's a book that forces you to see a heart breaking slowly and surely, as you reach the final page.I felt on this particular reading that the novel is also about escape. The escape of having love, wanting love, needing love- all because of society's cruel heteronormative norms of the 1950s that are not so different from today. It's what connects Giovanni and David- and what will drive David apart from him.David's affair with Giovanni begins at a bar in Paris run by an older gay man named Guillaume whose family is from old French money, "entangled with French honor and French glory...a symbol of French manhood" (150). Guillaume is predatory in his desire for the younger, charismatic Italian man whose past is burdened with sorrow- like David, he too is bisexual, and left Italy after losing his baby son, "we buried it right away, then I left my village and I came to this city where surely God has punished me for all my sins" (Baldwin 140). It is also Guillaume who will cause Giovanni's eventual tragic demise, in which David is often wondering in regret.Both David and Giovanni are terrified of getting older, alone- and turning out like Guillaume, a stereotypical lonely gay man with effeminate tendencies. Nothing is wrong with this of course, but embodying someone who isn’t masculine is terrifying for both.David's story of escaping New York also begins as an attempt to get away from a father who tries to understand him after the death of his mother, and of his aunt Ellen who suspects he might be gay.Also, David recalls his first love with another Italian boy named Joey, "I realized that my heart was beating in an awful way, and that Joey was trembling against me...out of this astounding intolerable pain came great joy" (Baldwin 8).As David and Giovanni's burgeoning love for each other grips them both with an vicelike intensity that is all consuming, Baldwin's coming of age masterpiece about obsessive first love had never been so much clearer to me- and it is novel that I will continue to carry with me.It is a monologue given by David's friend Jacques, another older gay man, (and who will later become Giovanni's lover) that is the key to the novel's furious, beating heart of longing and desperate need to love- Jacques sees clearly that the young men are on to something:“Love him and let him love you. Do you think anything else under heaven really matters? And how long at the best, can it last? You can give each other something which will make both of you better forever, if you will not be ashamed" (Baldwin 57).David is definitely more afraid of what society thinks of him in comparison to the mercurial Giovanni- and it is this sadly realized internal hatred of himself is why he clings to Hella so much later, as he wants to forget the wrenching memory of how he and Giovanni have parted:"Cheri Mon tres cher. Don't leave me. Please don't leave me. I turned and held him in my arms, sobbing, it would have been said as though his heart would break" (Baldwin 140).Personally my favorite line is one said by a crucial, but small character- an American woman named Sue with whom David picks up for sex. It sums up everything that we all want out (or at least what I believe) of life- the maddening, human need to be seen and loved at all cost, "Maybe you'll be lonely again. I guess I won't mind if you come looking for me" (Baldwin 102).Putting aside the sadness of Baldwin's narrative- there are wonderful, swoony evocative moments of true romance that burst through the seams of each page that depict David and Giovanni's courtship that lead them to Giovanni's Room- a place that illustrates a "me and you against the world feeling"- as reflected by David:“What was happening to me was was not so strange as it would have comforted me to believe...voices within me boomed...he pulled me against him, putting himself into my arms as though he were giving me himself to carry" (Baldwin 62-64).Like his other novels I've read- the need to belong, ambiguous loves, and sexuality all come to play in this novel which ignites itself in erotic white heat, that continues to sear itself into this reader's memory.It belongs on a very small, select shelf for me of books that continue to haunt me that are all about heartbreak and loss.Baldwin’s novel is a romantic, sensory experience that is the godfather of other LGBTQ themed books of his era and thereafter. Without it, we wouldn’t have other masterpieces of the lonely and of solitude.There is no other book like this one that can just make you swoon over the intensity of David and Giovanni’s affair- and truly break your heart after it is all gone.Postscript: It is James Baldwin's centennial anniversary, and if you are in the New York area, be sure to see the Treasures Exhibit at the New York Public Library where it currently houses on display the haunting, first drafts of Baldwin's "Giovanni's Room". Seeing his handwriting on the typed pages chilled me, and immediately led me to rereading this.
