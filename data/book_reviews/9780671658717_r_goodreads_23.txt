TLDR: As debut collections go, this is more of a grower than a banger, as the kidz all say these daze—for those just dipping their toe in, don't let the relative weakness of the first story dissuade or dishearten you: a few stories in, you will find much to celebrate, little to weary you, and more than one glimpse at future greatness. If I must quibble, perhaps there is a greater abundance of detail in some of these (at times, for their times) provocative (but never scurrilous), always rather artful (but generally-realistically-drawn) stories than I might have wished for, but so what?

That said, I did find the opener, "Daisy's Valentine" (a thingy about a unrepetant "recreational" pill-popper leaving his equally-addicted ball-and-chain for an office crush), a bit on the hollow side, though even there I found sentences that echo the perfection of some of Gaitskill's later stories,

But for me the book really starts rolling with the next two pieces, "A Romantic Weekend", a two-POV-er that plumbs the deluded psyches of a banally evil serial sexual humiliator and his abject, bumbling would-be, erm, humilatee, there is much pathos (and some fun) to be had watching as Gaitskill, through a massive feat of imaginative empathy, gets us very close to their respective suns to singe our own wings.

Now, as I might say of numerous other stories in this collection, I'm just into that scene, man (like, , hahahahaha), but if I can dig it—and I guess I can, or did, it was only because Mary Gaitskill me dig it, the way Dostoevsky made me dig the self-imposed torments of Raskolnikov, say, or Milton his sympathetic devil...

The next two stories, "Something Nice" and "An Affair, Edited" both plumb the emotional landscapes of sexual netherworlds still further—again, without so much as a trace (as far as my own Freudian Censor allowed me to detect) of gratuitousness.

In the first of these, Gaitskill wants us to think a little more clearly (or at least fully) about those old-chestnutty "Whores With a Heart of Gold" and their "Johns Who Fall For Them". For starters, though, divide that cliché by half, then halve that again, and then yet again, such that by the time we are at the quartic root of said formula, we become interested parties (hardly innocent bystanders but not-quite-guilty voyeurs, either...strangely implicated anthropological observers, perhaps?) as our author-handholder not only Virgils us through this somewhat less circle of Hell, but stops to ponder how complicated and bedizened and yet, well, non-binary and, still, even more delusory it really all is—even, or especially when it spurs a moment of self-recognition (in the characters, I mean, but ofc...):


I won't say much about the second story's even-bigger-cad except that it is his extended memory/fantasy innerworld as this Dude navigates his sold-out way around . This story's sordidly onanistic ending is also its protagonist's barely conscious beginning, and lying-to-self middle: The image became tiny and unnaturally white, was surrounded by darkness, then faded like the picture on a turned-off TV."

Similar levels of self-deceptive self-talk permeate story #5 of 9, "Connection", as well, though in this case our lyric voice is no trashscan Sinatra but a self-congratulatory supposed do-gooding ex-friend of a woman (possibly) spied now down-and-out and very much on-the-street. Concerning this one, you really have to marvel as the do-gooding shifts ever-so-slowly towards Ungood as our hero, Susan, makes her own way through Professional Life in the Big City—less sordidly than in the preceding story, perhaps, though no less damnèdly, or at least doomèdly, for all that. Even though "Connection"'s ending is arguably less totallizingly bleak that some othe rthe others, protagonist Susan is perhaps the character I liked the very least in this collection—and that's saying something, for is decidedly a book for someone addicted to "liking" much less (one hopes) identifying with its characters...I guess I just really, really didn't find Susan's glib condescension bearable, really, when it comes down to it—though I definitely salute her author for making me feel that way.
A little bit further down the infinitely-spiralling staircase, with "Trying to Be" we get, sort of, a conflation of two worlds Gaitskill traipsed us through earlier: sex work and the just-revisited PLBC. Here (and in kind of an obverse to the John in "Something Nice", our person of concern "Stephanie" has a foot- or rather toe-hold on both worlds, since "tricking was just something she slipped into, once a year or so, when she was feeling particularly revolted by clerical work." Stephanie also fantasizes a third world of possibility for her life, imagines that she is sure that she should/can/must/might/could be a writer, but falls for an image she projects onto one nice enough John anyway, by way of escape from all three worlds perhaps.

I don't have much to say about "Other Factors", which for some reason didn't make much of an impact upon me (or at any rate I don't remember very well) except to say that it hews close to PLBC world, but shares much its time and by contrast with Lesbian Domestic Life in a way that reminded me of Dickens's Wemmick surviving the rigours of London Dog Eat Dog, Inc. by constructing a literal, defensive around his home and hearth (and heart).

But the longer 7th and (much longer) 9th stories are my absolute favourites here, and worth the price of admission alone. In the 7th, "Secretary" we see a young girl, "Debby" (here uncharacteristically delivered in first person by same) who does not particularly want either to be an adolescent, nor to grow up, but is forced to do so first by her parents, then by her employer, and finally by a muckraking journalist. But, as ever, "There had been a catastrophe hidden in the folds of my contentment" (such as it discontentedly was)...I won't spoil why, or why the journalist gets involved, though!

Finally, with much, much longer "Heaven" we get a tiny (by comparison) Buddenbrooks: the Decline of a Family—something which, though hardly formally or thematically novel, felt like it should have been extended into one—to the length of novel, I mean. I was that captivated by it.

At the story's outset, as with Faulkner's "That Evening Sun" (or Michael Cunningham's equally, miraculously devastating "White Angel"), say, we learn of the tragedy that has eviscerated them all, son Charles's death, but that is really the here, not what Aristotle might call the Efficient (preciptative) Cause. No, that thing is much more complicated and un-root-out-able, almost, though Gaitskill does most exceptional yeoman's work in her attempt to "flesh it out", revealing the mystery for what it often truly is in the process, I might add. As well as how most of us numb ourselves to that mystery over, and through, perhaps because of, time.








Here's the thing, though: these characters (these , damnit!), may talk about it occasionally, but they cannot feel it, much less live by it. At least not with much facility, or regularity—or with Other People, much less themselves. Shoulda been a full-length novel, this'un.

Still in the shoegazing, new moon mood Gaitskill has induced in me, I shall end with first her epigraph, and then one of T.S. Eliot's (a bit of Dante)!
