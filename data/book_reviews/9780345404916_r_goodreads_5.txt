Powerful and Human Ending to an Epic Trilogy

The Last Full Measure by Jeff Shaara

Jeff Shaara’s The Last Full Measure is a fitting and deeply moving conclusion to his Civil War trilogy. Covering the final two years of the war, the novel picks up after Gettysburg and carries us through to Appomattox, bringing the conflict—and the lives within it—to a powerful close.

I’ve come to really value Shaara’s approach to historical fiction, especially when paired with traditional Civil War history. For me, that combination works best. The factual backbone gives structure, but Shaara breathes life into it. He helps you feel the war—not just understand it. Reading his work alongside biographies and battle histories has given me a broader, more human understanding of the conflict.

What stands out most in this final installment is the emotional weight. Shaara doesn’t just recount events—he immerses you in them. His portrayal of General Robert E. Lee is especially compelling. You see the war through Lee’s internal struggles—his growing awareness, his burdens, and his quiet endurance. There’s a haunting quality to it, especially as the Confederacy begins to crumble.

One of the most rewarding threads for me was the continued development of Joshua Lawrence Chamberlain. Watching his growth from a thoughtful academic into a hardened and capable military commander is one of the great arcs in the trilogy. Shaara captures not just his courage, but his evolution—how experience, loss, and responsibility shape him into a leader of real substance.

What makes Chamberlain’s story even more powerful is where it leads. His selection by Ulysses S. Grant to oversee the formal surrender of the Army of Northern Virginia at Appomattox is one of those quietly profound historical moments. Chamberlain understood the significance of the occasion, and his decision to have his men salute the surrendering Confederates speaks volumes. It wasn’t about victory—it was about respect, reconciliation, and the hope of healing a fractured nation. That moment has always stayed with me, and Shaara does a beautiful job honoring it.

I also appreciated how Shaara shifts perspectives between leaders, giving us a multi-dimensional view of the war. However, I’ll admit that at times I had a little difficulty keeping track of the many battles and shifting settings. This book covers a wide stretch of campaigns, and the pace can feel dense. Still, that’s a minor critique in an otherwise rich and rewarding read.

One of the highlights for me was the afterword—an unexpected gem. Seeing how Shaara connects Ulysses S. Grant with Mark Twain later in life added a fascinating layer. It reminded me that these figures didn’t just exist in war—they carried its weight long after.

If I had one small critique, it’s that Grant’s perspective sometimes felt a bit underdeveloped compared to Lee’s. Given how central Grant is to the Union’s final push, I found myself wanting just a bit more depth there.

But overall, this is historical fiction at its best—engaging, thoughtful, and deeply human. Shaara doesn’t glorify war; he reveals its cost. Through his characters, you see the exhaustion, the sacrifice, and the quiet dignity of those who endured it.

For anyone interested in the Civil War—especially those who enjoy blending narrative storytelling with historical study—this is a must-read.

Highly recommended.!
