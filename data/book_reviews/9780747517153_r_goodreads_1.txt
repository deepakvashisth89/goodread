I was reminded of this one when it unexpectedly came up in Emma LE Rees'sThe Vagina: A Literary and Cultural History. Rees compares it favourably with Nicholson Baker'sHouse of Holes, a book published seventeen years later and to which Freely's novel does indeed bear a striking resemblance.Maureen Freely was my creative writing supervisor in my final year at uni and she was incredibly generous and encouraging, especially when it came to the vague ideas about cross-linguistic vocabulary strategies that I was thinking about then. It was something she knew plenty about – having grown up in Turkey, she is probably best known as the English translator of much ofOrhan Pamuk's work, though she's written several novels of her own. I picked this one up the afternoon before my first seminar with her, and skimmed through it Good-Will-Hunting-style before we met, in an attempt to get a handle on her approach.Silly me, it's not very representative. It is fun though – a kind of investigation of efforts to uncover a genuine female sexuality, which (unlike Baker's book) does not allow itself to become utopian. It centres on the ‘Vulcania’, a vast women-only adult playground where stressed soccer moms and executives can – for a hefty fee – have their deepest desires made flesh by an army of diligent ‘beaux’. Clients at this pleasure dome can explore such areas as the Palace of Foreplay, or have a quick ride on the Merry-Go-Vibrator, which is exactly what you're thinking. Narrative tension comes from the central character, Fiona, who discovers that the Vulcania's manager is her old boyfriend Raul, with whom she shares a history of conflict and shame over the implications of sexual fantasy for real-world gender relations.A lot of the book's impact is based on the simple idea of doing a gender role-reversal of typical sex work, something that seems a lot less challenging now than perhaps it did in the early 90s. Nevertheless the investigation of sexual fantasy is always interesting to me, and here Freely suggests that there is a hidden violence and perversity in female desire which shocks and frightens the few men who take the time to uncover it:The most distraught new boy had just finished his first three-hour stint as a beau confessor. He had been deeply disturbed by the fantasies the clients had confessed to him. Two had involved sex with animals – a goat with horns and a baby donkey. Another three had involved incest – with daughters as well as sons. And a good five had culminated in castration – by truncheon, by scissors, by teeth, by hanging, and through the agency of a hungry giraffe.Freely is mostly having fun here (as that camelopardine aside suggests), and although you can get some sexual politics out of the book if you work at it, it's basically ajeu d'esprit– though not an unenjoyable one, since she is a very readable writer. Ultimately I preferredHouse of Holeson grounds of prose flair, though it is true that Baker is much more content to stay with surface descriptions compared to Freely's interest in her characters' motivations and interior life. Still, I was pleased to be reminded of this, and happy to be sent back to my shelves for a happy half-hour of reminiscence and literary debauchery.
