This is another book that had been sitting on my Kindle for a while, and where I can’t remember why I downloaded it. Possibly it was because the title suggested it might be suitable to read now that the long nights are here for those of us who live in northern latitudes. It’s a collection of 11 short stories, most of which are set in the 19th century, or earlier. I haven’t previously read anything by Karen Blixen, and I believe this collection is considered a minor part of her oeuvre. In terms of her writing style, I was struck by her very elaborate descriptions.

The first story, turned out to contain lots of fantastical elements, and resembled an ancient myth. That was OK for me. I have been known to read ancient myths, though I probably prefer them when they are ancient. I really enjoyed the next entry, in which a young man’s destiny turns on a misunderstanding. I was though left bewildered by the third story, I simply couldn’t understand the motivation and behaviours of the main character. I related only a little better to the next,

I liked a thoughtful piece set during the Franco-German War of 1870-71, which reminded me a lot of Maupassant’s stories set during that conflict. An English student finds himself caught up with a group of French people accused of spying by the German military, and the actions of the eponymous heroine will determine their fate. The GR blurb suggests that this book was published in 1942, during which time Denmark was occupied by Nazi Germany. It seems fairly clear that The Heroine was really a story about Denmark in 1942. I also wondered whether this was partly the case with a story set in the 18th century, and which is centred around the treatment of a peasant family by a local nobleman. In my opinion, this is the best story in the collection.

Mosty of the stories in the second half of the collection concern family relationships. Both and feature children who are adopted into middle class families. Both children seem detached, even other-worldly, and to some extent this is also true of the characters (and not just the title characters) in a tale about two teenagers who live in the same house. Rosa is the biological daughter of a local parson. Peter is described as Rosa’s cousin, but there is also a suggestion that he was actually a foundling.

A number of the stories contained various pseudo-philosophical ramblings, and this was particularly noticeable in both and in Neither did much for me. set in medieval Denmark, was another story with magical/folkloric elements.

I can see why people might love this collection, but I was perplexed by many of the stories, and found I couldn’t relate to most of them.
