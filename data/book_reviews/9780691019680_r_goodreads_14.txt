Although never quite enjoying the same level of joy and popularity as his brother Party, Popper’s text remains one of those books which academics and historians love to get excited about - in short this first volume challenges what he sees as Plato’s brand of quasi-totalitarianism and to be fair he carefully picks apart Plato’s stance on many issues with a fair degree of insight and conviction.It’s quite amusing to think how persistent the myth of ancient Greece looms so heavily on the higher echelons of western thinking – a troubling amount of politicians, scholars and others would have you believe that for most of that society consisted of flouncing around in pristine white robes scoffing down grapes and olives, throwing back the wine in between heated debates and pontificating as no one had to do a day’s work. Rarely is it mentioned that the reality consisted of democracy being restricted to a tiny, privileged male elite based on slavery and brutality – and yet far too many would have us believe it was the height of civility and enlightenment.Anyway I'm not saying I agree or disagree with Popper - this was so dull that to be honest by the end of it I couldn't care less either way and eh so there we go.
