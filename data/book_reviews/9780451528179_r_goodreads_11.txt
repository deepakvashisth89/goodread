Mark Twain is a good deal of a character and an unparalleled literary showboater. He is, by far, the amusingest raconteur to ever compose a tall-tale, spinning his yarns into pure gold. But in Life on The Mississippi Twain outdoes even himself. Never was there such a silver-tongued, hyperbolist rascal with the astounding ability to make his words flow like his beloved river, endlessly on. Why describe in six words what can be described in forty-six? And even though I usually prefer quieter, concise, simple language, I really love reading Twain. His writing is euphonious, fairly singing and dancing along like a, well…, river.

I fear I’ll do myself an injury if I carry on like this, writing at Full Steam, so I’ll Ease All.



And this Slow Steaming “gives me the opportunity of introducing” the thought that maybe great writing in a book inspires great writing in the review (at least enjoying my writing, but I suspect I’m channelling Twain’s braggadocio rather than his literary excellence). “But I’m wandering from what I was intending to do, that is” reviewing Life on the Mississippi.

Twain first recounts how he learnt to be a pilot during the heyday of steamboats (c. 1857–1861). I loved this part of the book filled as it is with some of the best writing I have ever enjoyed: Beautiful, insightful, provocative and witty. Then, in the second part, Twain describes his return to the beloved river twenty one years later only to find it changed dramatically. They had tamed the wild Mississippi. Such is the explicit topic of his book, but Twain meanders, taking frequent offshoots and side channels. The journey is the thing.

Life on the Mississippi could have been a maudlin attempt to relive the ‘good ol’ days’ but instead Twain manages to immortalise the steamboat era, keeping it alive through his literary tribute. The vibrancy of his observations make it real for us and we feel as if Mark Twain is steaming down the Mississippi even now.

I can understand Twain’s elegiac motivation. I still miss, almost grieve for, the beach-side house where I grew up. I loved the majestic outlook over the harbour, the sprawling garden where I could climb and jump, chase and hide, the two-story tree-hut, and the delightful cat who would follow us out with her tail held high, over the low-tide mud flats to check the nets. But the house and garden are gone and I can only imagine walking sadly through the rooms and along the paths. I understand the great appeal of preserving my past still living in literature.

Life on the Mississippi is also a great book about teaching and learning. One of the core skills of a steamboat pilot, a necessity for safe navigation, was knowing every inch of the hazard filled Mississippi. But memorising the ever-changing, twelve hundred mile river, was a seemingly impossible lesson. As Twain describes it, it is akin to memorising War and Peace, in multiple languages, as it is being rewritten and edited for publication. Just when Twain feels triumphant and conceited, thinking he has mastered the impossible, his teacher shows him his next preposterous lesson - he has to memorise it backwards as well as forwards!

“Upon that text I desire to depart from the direct line of my subject and make a little excursion” about transformative learning. Twain lost the beauty of the Mississippi, he says, because what he learned transformed what he sees, rather than merely adding new knowledge. This is the same transformation that happens when you learn to read a script with characters different from your native language (or even when you first learn to read). Before the script makes sense you might admire the artistry and elegance of the curves and lines. But once you learn the meaning of the script you see only what it means, and never again can you appreciate it simply as lovely forms.

These passages about Twain’s transformative learning also inspired a fascinating discussion in : Like Twain, can learning too much ruin our appreciation of painting, literature, or other forms of beauty? What do you think?

And Twain also had more to say. Near the end of Life on the Mississippi he ignores his earlier conclusion about the lost grace and poetry of the Mississippi, and praises its beauty at sunrise.



And later he waxes eloquently about all the enchanting sunsets on the Mississippi, painted on the canvas of the river in “every imaginable dream of color.”

So he forever lost aesthetic appreciation of his Mississippi, except for all the times when he could still see its beauty. Twain, apparently, can believe two contradictory things at sunrise and sunset.
