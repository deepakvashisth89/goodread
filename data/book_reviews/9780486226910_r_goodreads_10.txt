I'll be honest that I've been putting off reading the rest of my Baum books because the last couple have been a bit disappointing. Was I remembering them as better than they actually were?

Baum had noted that this was his best work, and he might actually be right. I'd have to do a head-to-head comparison without childhood nostalgia in the way, since Glinda of Oz and Sky Island were always my favorites, but this is definitely equal to and perhaps better than the latter. It has a less of Baum's trademark quirkiness and subscribes more to the structure of a typical fairy tale, which makes it a very pleasant, easy read with a satisfying arc. And I have always loved fairy tales.

Some parts were a bit silly, still, and the timeline surrounding the Roly-Rogues was particularly absurd - all that happened over the course of a couple days? Plus it was never particularly clear how old King Bud and Princess Fluff were - some of the action could've easily spread out over a few years, as they grew up and became accustomed to their roles - instead of all taking place within a few short months. But a reader forgives such things in a story like this.

Queen Zixi, despite being the title character, is absolutely not the main character of this story in any way. The alternate title, "The Story of the Magic Cloak," might've been Baum's original suggestion, and I suspect his publishers would've pressed him to make this story sound more like the super-successful Oz he'd spent years trying to avoiding revisiting.

I'm not really sure the main character is, honestly, and maybe that's one of the book's flaws. It's kind of about Bud and his sister, who were orphaned by the ferryman who'd adopted them the time they were orphaned - an unnecessary and rather nice sidenote, that they were raised and loved by a father who wasn't related to them by blood.

Their "evil aunt," who takes them in and hauls them off to the city where she works as a laundrywoman, turns out to be nice enough after all, once she has enough money and pretty clothes to lift away all the day-to-day worries that'd made her cross and a bit cruel to the children. She doesn't begrudge Bud his sudden kingship - she just wants to live in the castle, too!

Similarly, all the king's advisors, while foolish and selfish in their own ways, never showed the slightest hint of being power-hungry or trying to take the kingdom for themselves. They're happy enough to dig out an old book of laws, appoint their new king, and let that child rule.

Then there's Queen Ziwi, a witch who's presented as a potential enemy of Bud's kingdom, but turns out to be quite a friend, in the end.

That's all typical Baum, with a irredeemable characters (the vicious Roly-Rogues), and a pleasant veneer of niceness spread over everything else. And of course there are fairies, Baum's favorite, who bestow a magical cloak upon the kingdom, then take it away when it starts doing more harm than good. (Although it seemed rather unfair to me to take away the dog's ability to speak - how was a foolish wish?? Also: did the aunt get to keep her wings?)

I did find it a little annoying that Bud was a pretty selfish child whose sister did most of the ruling while he spent his kingdom's money on toys and complained about his duties. He's young, yeah, and ultimately he made a selfless wish, but none of that character development really happened over the course of the story itself. That's why it would've been nice for the timeline to extend a bit...but Baum never really lets his characters grow up.

It's interesting that all of Baum's attempts to establish other worlds ultimately folded back into Oz. I don't remember Queen Zixi, but apparently she visits Ozma in another book, and the fairy forest in the beginning of this one is the same one where Santa Claus grew up in . I definitely didn't enjoy that book enough to ever reread it, but I like that I was able to recognize the setting here.

Those kinds of touches helped to make Baum into such a beloved children's author - it's nice to feel at home in another world, and to have the sense that you know your way around a place so unlike your ordinary surroundings. Baum is good at infusing words with magic and making them feel tangible and entirely approachable.
