I actually have a copy of Kafka’s complete stories, but I decided to just read his published stories for now, which align with the stories in this collection and have the same translator, so I’m going to use this as a placeholder.I think such flat, matter-of-fact prose just isn’t my—forgive the cliché—cup of tea. Kafka’s prose is almost devoid of metaphor—which makes sense, I guess, since to liken anything in that world to this world would be to throw the reader a lifeline, in the way that to liken an otherworldly creature to an octopus would be to offer an exit from the unknown and reentry into the familiar. But I was still frustrated I couldn’t find the richness of metaphor here I could in writers like Walser and Schulz. Kafka’s sentence structure is quite complex, though, and I found I would grasp at his commas, semicolons, dashes—desperate for something to hold on to. This was especially frustrating when reading the shorter stories, which read more like prosaic prose-poems than short stories, and I would forget them almost as quickly as I read them. It does worry me a little that I have so much trouble appreciating writing that’s not of the beautiful kind, but what can I do?Most of the stories in this collection aren’t as grotesque as I thought they would be. Actually, most of them aren’t grotesque at all. And as with most short story collections, it was only a roughly and intermittently blissful experience. The highs for me were In the Penal Colony and Before the Law. I’m still undecided as to whether to read the unpublished stories. If I do, it’ll probably be when I have nothing else to read—which certainly isn’t happening anytime soon. If I sound a little bitter, I guess I am. I expected Kafka to become a new favorite. But I’m glad to have finally read something of his besides The Metamorphosis.
