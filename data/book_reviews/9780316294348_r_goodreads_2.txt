This book, originally from the mid-90s, advances what Friedman claims is a fresh thesis: that over the course of the Hebrew biblical narratives, which didn't even become composed chronologically, different authors told how the gradual retreat of a present God occurred in various stories, independently compiled into the canon. In a note, however, he credits Jack Miles, whose "God: A Biography" was being assembled at the same time. And I add as an aside how I recall James Michener's entertaining epic "The Source" from a generation earlier (1965) in fictional fashion included this very insight, as gradually his characters find a cutoff from contact with divinity.

Anyhow, a generation later, we have this, and another concurrent study (now slightly updated; see my review of Daniel Matt's "God and the Big Bang"; also acknowledged by Friedman as having been conceived about the same time as his own study regarding the Kabbalistic resonance in light of cosmology), and the new narrative theology of Judaism by Rabbi Irving "Yitz" Greenberg, "The Triumph of Life" (recently reviewed too), which is how I found out about Friedman's earlier theory.

Greenberg expands the Lurianic concept of 'tzimtzum" or contraction of the divine force in creation, and applies a schema to three phases of Jewish understanding of the regressive pace of God back from humanity, the better (or worse) for us to participate as grown-ups, as it were, in repairing the world. Friedman, a leading scholar, nevertheless proceeds with commendable and extreme caution in his initial chapters, careful not to confuse his theological acumen with New Age fuzzy logic or pop-culture folderol. And he also calls out astrophysicists who lack Biblical Hebrew!

Back to Friedman, as others have critiqued, his triple header starts with the biblical accounts. Part two segues into a sympathetic depiction of tortured Nietzsche (who wasn't anti-Semitic; blame that unfair reputation on his bitter sister after his death) and brave Dostoevsky's parallel examinations of the loss of belief and demise of the paternal, caring deity of old. Finally, the Kabbalah model offers Friedman a template for how we can step up in an unprecedented opportunity to break through ignorance, harness scientific knowledge, and try to emerge as (fittingly he cites Arthur C. Clarke's story "The Sentinel" inspiring "2001: A Space Odyssey"--I thought his clever "Childhood's End" with its own take on spiritual forces entering earthly awareness might have enhanced his discussion) mature beings able to face the diminishment of God in our lives, and act as confident men and women who can therefore try to restore the lost connections with the Hebraic covenant.

To which he makes profound commentary on the Christian return of the intervention of God the Father as His Son, and how Jesus embodies what his Jewish culture denied, the incarnate and eternal among us as human. Friedman reads into the last words on the Cross of despair the departure of the divine within the Christ suffering, and how this too depicts Friedman's perception of the Almighty leaving "us" to take charge. He treats both testaments with respect and scrutiny.

While the end of the book rambles, as impatiently the author wants this imminent transformation of men and women willing to heal the planet, reduce injustice, and increase the positive messages of both scriptures as lived out, one cannot fault his heart's in the right place, and he strives mightily for his intellect to catch up. It's a rewarding if diffuse reward of one's time, and hours spent in Friedman's presence (see my review of his Exodus revisionist theme as well; his Torah commentary will also please you) result in wisdom in abundance. Not perfect, but what creation is?
