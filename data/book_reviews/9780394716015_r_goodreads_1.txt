A conciseNew York Timesreview from early 1983, one of a handful inthis column by Newgate Callendar (what a name!),had this to say:Another British entry -THE LAST HOUSEPARTY by Peter Dickinson (Pantheon, $12.95). Mr. Dickinson is one of the more original mystery story writers of the decade, and he generally manages to create something that breaks the mold. In fact, one wonders if ''The Last Houseparty'' really belongs to the mystery genre, even though a crime is committed at one point.A sort of ''Brideshead Revisited'' affair, the book hops around in time in a rather unsettling way. Its characters are very British upper class, there is a great deal of political and other kinds of talk, there is a castle and a tower clock that play a large part in the plot (but disabuse yourself of the notion that this book is in any respects a Gothic), and at the last houseparty there is a nasty crime that one finds hard to believe. Mr. Dickinson is as suave and sensitive as ever, but readers who look for the classical amenities in a mystery story should steer clear of this one.My reaction differed in some respects. The book's time shifts were probably intended to be unsettling (which may be what theTimesreviewer meant). It's as if the story were being dragged toward a discovery that it wanted to avoid. As for the crime, it was disturbing but not hard to credit. That the book played with formal elements of its genre is probably why one of my former English teachers recommended it to me, and it's part of what I enjoyed.
