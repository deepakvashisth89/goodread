As someone who has experienced "Hollywood development hell" firsthand I loved this book. Monster describes how Dunne and his wife Joan Didion spent more than six years shepherding a screenplay through dozens of drafts, multiple executives, battles between studios and labor unions, countless cross-country flights, and nearly-endless contract re-negotiations, all to make a film that had very little to do with their original idea. Dunne and Didion's original pitch focused on the tumultuous life of a pioneering news broadcaster named Jessica Savitch; the resulting film, called Up Close and Personal, is a soft-focus romance between characters played by Michelle Pfeiffer and Robert Redford. (It has nothing to do with Savitch whatsoever.) And Dunne and Didion were lucky! Their film actually got made; they got paid well. My own work in Hollywood was decades later, and hasn't made it to screen, but the process has been much the same: huge personalities, big promises, bald-faced lies, cost-cutting, re-negotiations, inexplicable logic, artlessness, and, at the center, the desperate groping around in the dark for profit. Ultimately, no one knows what the public is going to like, least of all the executives in charge. And this lack of knowledge fuels the insanity of this industry, where it might take a dozen meetings to get a single line of dialogue changed, where writers are fired only to be re-hired months later, where contracts are written with the hope that the writers will breach them, and therefore forfeit pay. The idea of the next Ben Affleck and Matt Damon writing a script between day job shifts that becomes an Oscar-winner is pure fantasy. Scripts aren't art; they're commerce by committee, constructed in the most inefficient way imaginable.
