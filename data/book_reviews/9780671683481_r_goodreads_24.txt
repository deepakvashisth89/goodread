★★½I finished this audiobook feeling incredibly unsettled, and not for the reasons you’d expect from a true crime deep dive. There is a point in the post-script where Carlton Stowers essentially suggests that no further investigation into the case is required, which felt remarkably blasé given the massive gaps in the narrative. It’s a problematic stance that mirrors the exact issues I had with the book’s "hero," Truman Simons.The story documents the 1982 triple-murder of three teenagers at Lake Waco, Texas. The scene was so gruesome that authorities initially suspended the case indefinitely after only 52 days. However, Patrol Sergeant Truman Simons remained obsessed with the carnage, plunging into a world of violence to eventually secure convictions against David Spence and three accomplices. While it’s framed as an Edgar Award-winning account of justice, the reality feels much murkier.If there was ever a textbook case for tunnel vision, Simons is it. There is a very specific reason the prosecutor never put him on the stand during the trials; his conduct was questionable at best. He should never have been permitted to interact with David Spence in jail or take statements from prisoners he was directly responsible for. The fact that so many of his own colleagues called shenanigans on his methods should have been a major red flag, yet the book glosses over this internal friction.Stowers also desperately needed a tighter edit—this felt about 300 pages longer than necessary. I found myself wanting to skip ahead during the mountainous amounts of extraneous detail that added nothing to the central mystery. I truly don’t need to know about a parole officer’s gardening habits or the various claims made by psychics. These tangents only served to highlight how little physical evidence actually existed.The book lacks any real balance, as Stowers clearly relies on the police and prosecution as his primary sources. He didn’t bother to engage with the defence lawyers or independent experts, instead choosing to glorify individuals who committed obvious misconduct. He can shout from the rooftops that these men were guilty, but when the "proof" relies almost entirely on jailhouse snitches and coerced confessions from those trying to avoid the death penalty, it’s hard to swallow.Ultimately, I’m frustrated because the motive presented feels farcical and nothing was proven beyond a reasonable doubt. After finishing, I looked up the murders and found that most of the "facts" here are heavily disputed. I have no idea who killed those teenagers in 1982, and unfortunately, because of the sloppy investigation and this one-sided narrative, I don’t think we ever will.
