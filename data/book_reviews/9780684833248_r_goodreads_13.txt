Co. Aytch (Company H) by Sam R Watkins is another one of those books that’s been collecting dust on my shelves for at least a decade—2025 is turning into the year of reading books I’ve had lying around unread for many years, and as someone with an enormous TBR, there’s always satisfaction in it! I’m a certified Civil War and Revolutionary War geek, and love primary sources and memoirs, so it’s a wonder I haven’t gotten to this one sooner. Regardless, I’m glad I did.Sam Watkins is good with a pen, and has a natural feel for a good turn of phrase. He’s also got a clever sense of humor, and when someone is writing about subject matter as absolutely brutal as the Civil War, that ability too look at everything with some humor really adds a special human element to the whole thing. Sam fought in the 1st Tennessee for virtually the entire war. By the end, he was one of about ten guys left that made it through without being badly wounded, killed, or otherwise put out of action. Pretty amazing honestly.While Sam’s service is well documented, I will say that I wouldn’t be surprised if he’s a bit of a “tall tale teller”—he kind of Forrest Gumped his way through the war, having encounters with all kinds of major figures and events throughout. Sam’s regiment fought mostly in Tennessee, Virginia, and Georgia towards the end of the war; he was not in Lee’s Army of Northern Virginia that fought many of the most well known battles, like Gettysburg, Fredericksburg, Petersburg, etc. That’s not to say Sam didn’t fight in huge, brutal and consequential battles because he did. Shiloh, Murfreesborough, Nashville, and Franklin among many others. Franklin in particular was a lesser known (outside of the CW geek world) but unbelievably brutal battle that saw the Confederates under John Bell Hood attempting a full on frontal assault on a heavily fortified Union position that turned into an absolute meat grinder. Similar to the more widely known Pickett’s Charge at Gettysburg, I can’t even express how terrifying this must have been. This was an example of old Napoleonic tactics being attempted at a time when the guns and artillery had become more efficient and deadly. The canister shot coming out of those cannons is the stuff nightmares are made of.But Watkins made it through, and doesn’t revel in blood and gore. He also tells us some of the lesser discussed (as time goes on) sides of the war: hanging out at camp, stealing the dumbest mule east of the Mississippi, and snowball fights in the winter (when some “mean fellow” enclosed a rock inside his snowball and nearly took one of his fellow Confederates out). All in all, it’s a very human account of a war that happened long enough ago, where the human element can start to be lost. When we look back on these old wars, the whole way back to the Greeks and beyond, it’s important to remember these wars were fought by people; people with individual stories and experiences, and these are the truly fascinating parts of the story. Far more than tactics and battle recaps, which Watkins doesn’t get into at all.His writing can be a bit scattered at times, but overall it’s a fascinating account of a brutal war fought amongst American towns, cities, farms and fields. Worth a read
