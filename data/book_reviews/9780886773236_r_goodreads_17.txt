The Trilogy of Morgaine and Vanye Concludes in 'Fires of Azeroth', though their quest - perhaps a literally never-ending one - does not end (especially since there is another book to be read, though my understanding is that Gate of Yvrel, Well of Shiuan, and Fires of Azeroth, form a complete story together with the fourth book being a follow-up).From the mountainous, snowy realm to a drowning planet to the forest-filled Shathan, Morgaine and Vanye have had QUITE an adventure, and I think out of it all Fires of Azeroth is my favorite. The first book was a little too 'slogging in the snow' for my full liking, and the second was mostly a big 'prologue' introducing one of the world's native (Well, somewhat native, when you consider the events of the trilogy) inhabitants followed by 'Vanye is captured and held prisoner' as the other major event of the book. In Fires of Azeroth, though it maintains the same patient, plodding pace as the others (with a great deal of the most important stuff happening off-screen, and out of Vanye's sight) Cherryh has gained enough momentum for more than two action scenes to happen. Also to my great relief (as well as to Morgaine and Vanye's relief I'm sure) Shathan isn't a miserable shit-hole full of grimdark thieves with nothing on their minds but murder and torture. As Morgaine herself says over and over, even good-intentioned people are her enemies, but by the tale's end she has that resolve put to the test.Ever difficult as Cherryh's writing sometimes is, with action scenes coming and going in the blink of an eye with no emphasis from the narrative, I still like it. Dry though it is it is convincing, and lays plenty of foundation for the mind to work upon imagining more. Stony and cold though the characters often are (so much interaction between them rises and falls on the smallest of things, like a jaded old married couple ready to find affront or read meaning into every cough and inflection), their brief moments of happiness and humanity are wonderful.Once again, as I've noted in my review of the first two books, I would have much preferred and loved it to have not had the over-arching premise explained to me by a prologue, saying "Once there were aliens who built teleportation gates through time and space, but their abuse of the gates ruined everything, so now Morgaine has to destroy all the gates so no more bad things happen". It's the sort of backstory to everything that's happening I would have preferred to figure out myself, that the 'Gates' and 'Wells' and 'fires', whatever they're called by their respective worlds, are just a network of teleportation/time-travel doors, and that Morgaine's terrifying and dreadful magic sword Changeling is just a teleportation sword itself, it's awful power, that characters speak of like it's worse than the devil, is just to stab open a portal to outer space and suck enemies through it. The mythology and vaguaries of everyone's speech elevate these sci-fi concepts to something awe-inspiring. It does, alas, form the rather pessimistic thought that the most terrible power in the universe, which humanity must be denied, as is Morgaine's sacred charge, is a bunch of teleportation doors. Are we, as a species, so irresponsible and forever beyond the hope of betterment that wondrous technologies, like the ability to travel from planet to planet in the blink of an eye, are a responsibility so dreadful to consider that we must be denied them forever, left to eke out existence as feudalistic tribes and kingdoms scattered across time and space?One day I look forward to reading the final chapter of the Morgaine Cycle, "Exile's Gate", but for now I'm happy to take a rest here, on what felt like an optimistic (comparatively) and happy end.
