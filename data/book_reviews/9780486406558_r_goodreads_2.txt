The action of this more than 400-page novel takes place before and shortly after the First World War, in a small town in Minnesota called Gopher Prairie, where one might think Sinclair Lewis has drawn heavily on his native background.
For his people and Dr. William Kennicott, Gopher Prairie is the most beautiful city in the world when he describes it to Carol Milford, whom he met in St. Paul, Illinois, and whom he dreams of marrying. Deep America. A whole, deep city, cheerfully colored, carefully maintained, and populated by a small, lively, cultivated, and warm earth.
When Carol, now Mrs. Kennicott, arrives in Gopher Prairie after their honeymoon in Colorado, she strives to see the city as her husband has presented it to her—and as he sees it.
But Carol is born an anti-conformist and romantic, too, and imagines that good intentions, new ideas, a little youth, and immense goodwill will overcome the a priori. The idioms and laziness of this small town, where sidewalks go up the "high street," are still in wood, as in the time of the pioneers. Despite its horror, Gopher Prairie was ready to sympathize sincerely with its inhabitants. It will soon become clear that all this is more difficult than expected; good intentions are not enough when they clash with conformity and well-thought-out plans.
Sinclair Lewis draws a portrait of a rigid and stuck American society in a tongue-in-cheek way that allows the reader to take a critical distance, sometimes too much so. He was born there, lived there, and then, somewhere, he is attached. But no proverb says, "Who likes to chastise well?"
Here are the pillars of the significant relationship between money and social success already held by Americans of the time: worship of a single Bible and only a church, fear, and contempt for the old continent. The pioneers came from the terrible empire of the "what do we say" (not specific, that one, it is true, small US cities) and a macho and patriarchal world vision to which Carol will finally, though reluctantly, submit.
It should be noted that, from the beginning, Lewis posits as a principle that Carol's sex is an additional burden in the fight she intends to lead.
