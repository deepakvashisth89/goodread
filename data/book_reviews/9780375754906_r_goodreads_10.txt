31 Jan 2026: The story is set in Bursley, Staffordshire in the north of England 1840-1905 and follows the lives of sisters Constance and Sophia Baines. Arnold Bennett does a wonderful job of describing the area and setting the scene. One example is "the villages were full of women who did nothing but fight against dirt and hunger, and repair the effects of friction on clothes,” and I chuckled at “It was not easy to right a capsized crinoline.” I think he seemed to understand a woman’s lot in life and poke fun at finicky fashions that were difficult to wear and somewhat ridiculous.

I also appreciated his insights into how people communicate, especially non-verbally, such as: “Their glances met for an instant in the transient gaze which expresses the tenderness of people who feel more than they kiss.” Later on in the story he tells us that the sisters communicate better with each other through non-verbal communication.

At ages sixteen and fifteen respectively, Constance and Sophia are about to leave school. Sophia wants to be a teacher. Mrs. Baines (her mother) has other ideas.

There is much talk of Mrs. Baines’ ringlets. My favorite quote is, “her temper flashed out and you could see ringlets vibrating under the provocation of Sophia’s sauciness.”

Mrs. Baines is described as dealing with Sophia with “god-like calm.” Constance refers to her sister as a “stormy creature.”

Mr. Baines is bed bound after a stroke. Mother and daughters nurse him for fourteen years until he dies by falling out of bed during a moment where Sophia left him briefly.

I won’t describe all the intervening years, however I will say that the sisters end up being separated for about thirty years and we learn about their separate fates.

I noticed that a lot of attention is given to the dogs in the story. One example is the announcement, ‘Here’s a man come to see you about a dog.’ This got a wry chuckle out of me as this idiom is usually used to cover up the true purpose of someone’s visit, however in this case there truly is a dog involved!

Continuing on the topic of dogs, I loved that Constance couldn’t resist getting another dog, ‘a young wire-haired fox-terrier’ as a replacement when her elderly dog passed away, even though she had sworn off having “a young dog again because, […] they ate the stuffing out of the furniture.”

When Sophia falls sick as a mature woman and manager of a boarding-house in Paris, France the doctor tells her “speaking with a charming exaggerated purity of the vowels.”

‘You have the mucous fever. I have had it myself. You will be forced to take baths; very frequently. I must ask you to reconcile yourself to that, to be good.’ I was bemused to think that she may dislike taking baths and even rebel against them!

After a more than thirty-year gap, Sophia, now aged fifty, is now back in touch with her sister Constance via letter. She exaggerates Constance’s illness (a bout of sciatica) to depart from her friends and acquaintances in France and returns to England.

After much anticipation and some trepidation on Constance’s part, the sisters are reunited at the family home in Bursley. “As Constance shut and bolted the front door the sisters looked at each other, Sophia faintly smiling. It seemed to them that they understood each other better when they did not speak.”

The sisters must learn to live together again. They each have a dog; Constance has the wire-haired terrier named Spot and Sophia has Fosette, which is described as having a ‘Lion cut’ or ‘Continental Cut’ whereby there is a lot of hair on the front half, the back half is shaved and the hair at the tail tip and ankles is rounded. Additionally, Fosette was decorated with a pink ribbon round her neck. “The animal had absolutely the air of a decked trollop.” These dogs must learn to live together also.

Sophia regrets her hasty departure from Paris. “Ten steps, and her career was finished, closed. Astonishing, with what liquid tenderness she turned and looked back on that hard, fighting, exhausting life in Paris.” But, once reunited with Constance is loathe to leave her again.

The sisters, with their dogs in tow, visit a hotel in Buxton, which was of interest to me having visited this lovely town in the Peak District of Derbyshire just recently. I met a couple there that mentioned that Buxton was once a destination place for honeymooners. Bennett also refers to Buxton in this way earlier on in the story.

Bringing dogs to the hotel takes courage; “to bring one dog indicates that you are not accustomed to deny yourself small pleasures for the sake of a few extra shillings; to bring two indicates that you have no fear of hotel managers and that you are in the habit of regarding your own whim as nature’s law.” I thought this was an interesting insight.

Sophia had intended to use the stay at the hotel in Buxton as a springboard to a new life with Constance away from the dark brooding house in Bursley. I guess she thought that once she had lured Constance away from Bursley she could keep her away. After all she missed her light and airy boarding-house in Paris.

However, she neglected to take into account Constance’s stubbornness, and they return home to Bursley to live out the rest of their days together. At the very least, Sophia hoped to brighten the house up, however it seems to defeat her.

Bennett describes the sisters’ final decade together. I was glad they shared these years but felt sad that they didn’t make more of them. They seemed resigned to living out their years quietly in the dark brooding house and fighting with the servant girl, and caring for their dogs. It gave me a sense of melancholia. Arnold refers to “the futility of Sophia’s career, and the waste of her attributes.”

Both Sophia and Constance bequeath their monies to Cyril, Constance’s son and only child. Of Sophia, Arnold writes, “She had grown old and hard in joyless years in order to amass this money which Cyril would spend coldly and ungratefully, never thinking of the immense effort and endless sacrifice which had gone into its collection.” I feel very sad indeed.

Standout quote:

“He had not ruined her, as a bad professor may ruin a fine voice, because her moral force immeasurably exceeded his; he had unwittingly produced a masterpiece, but it was a tragic masterpiece.”
