I needed something quick and easy after finishing Bleak House, so I turned to one of my guilty pleasures: old westerns. Elmore Leonard's are among my favorite. The Law at Randado is an early one for him, published in 1954 when he was just 29. This is long before his Detroit/South Florida crime novel days. Even in his early work, Leonard's skill at writing real "sounding" dialogue is evident. He eventually became almost peerless at it, but you can see it early on.

Every character has a distinct voice in speech and internal dialogue. That is no easy feat.

Plotwise, this is pretty much your standard western. Young deputy gets embarrassed and run out of town by older citizens who want to assume the power of law. He regroups and returns with the sheriff to set things straight. There's some growing up in the process. Along the way, there are plenty of hideouts, chases across the desert landscape, and a mysterious hired gun who everyone is afraid of. The ending is less cliche than one might imagine, though. There's a shootout at the local saloon, of sorts, but not the kind you're thinking of.

Kirby Frye, Dandy Jim, and Sherrif Danaher (especially the first two) have surprising depth for as short as this book is.

Loved it and would recommend. Three stars because even though it's good, it's still junk fiction. Takes more to get to four.
