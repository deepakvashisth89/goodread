Chunkier than most of Rubens's work, Brothers attempts a multi-generational saga on the experience of the Jewish diaspora. It tells of pograms, industrial accidents, and totalitarian oppression, as the Bindel family somehow survives each harsh twist of fate, even as individual members secumb. There are no short cuts, as the reader turns through 500 pages. At times it felt only marginally shorter than living the real thing.I think the subject matter merits a lengthier treatment, only I'm not sure Rubens herself entirely succeeds in spinning out the yarn quite this far. It does manage to be gripping at regular intervals, but Rubens also feels slightly over indulgent at points, leading on the pathos like a Passover feast where no space was left on the table for the eye to settle. In all respects, this is a heavy book. Some judicious thinning, or alternatively leavening with levity, could have helped.I have observed before that Rubens seems to write either comedy or tragedy. Here it's clearly the latter, leaving 'Brothers' to fly or fall on how far it plays on the heart-strings. It's partly successful and there is an earnestness that feels true, even if its embellishment at points feels over-wrought.
