Set aboard a ship traveling to Australia in the early 19th century, Edmund Talbot is a young aristocrat who has been tasked with recording his experiences in a journal for his godfather. He will be taking an administrative position when he arrives. He encounters a microcosm of society aboard the ship. In his journal, he documents the tensions that arise between different social classes, particularly in the relationship between officers, different social strata of passengers, and crew. One passenger in particular, Reverend Colley, is singled out for humiliation. Colley had joined the ship late, and inadvertently incited the captain’s ire.Talbot initially comes across as arrogant and entitled, and it is obvious from his writings that he considers himself superior to pretty much everyone else on board. A central theme ofRites of Passageis the dehumanization inherent in classism. Talbot undergoes his own rite of passage as he witnesses the darker aspects of human nature. Eventually, we learn Colley’s viewpoint, and Talbot starts to feel guilty about his previous indifference toward the Reverend. Talbot’s growing awareness of cruelty forces him to confront uncomfortable truths about himself. By the end of the novel, he begins to see the limitations of his privileged worldview. It is written in the style of a nineteenth century novel. It engaged me from beginning to end. I can see why this book won the Booker Prize in 1980.
