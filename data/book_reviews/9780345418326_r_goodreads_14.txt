The Secret Pilgrimby John le Carre is basically a form of reminisces and a memoir in a series of reflections on a long career in espionage during the height of the Cold War as our narrator Ned is now close to retirement and running Sarratt, the training school for new recruits to British intelligence. And as such he has invited the legendary George Smiley to address the soon-to-be graduates at a dinner and following the dinner, the student body of Sarratt spent the night learning more of the past experiences of the legendary spymaster. And in this setting where the responses of George Smiley to their questions allow Ned to think back and recall memories of his own long career in the secret service. Ned’s career in British Intelligence, known as the Circus, proves to be an intricate weave of suspicion, danger, boredom and exhilaration that is the essence of espionage. It is a look at a thirty-year career in the Circus, burrowing deep into the twilight world where he ran spies or “joes” from Poland, Estonia, and Hungary, men and women to whom he gave his most profound love and hate.“And just occasionally we meet the reality we’ve been playing with,” he said quietly. “Until it happens, we’re spectators. The joes live out our dreams for us, and we case officers sit safe and snug behind our one-way mirrors, telling ourselves that seeing is feeling. But when the moment of truth strikes—if it ever does for you—well, from then on we become a little more humble about what we ask people to do for us.”It is in the telling of the story of his own secret pilgrimage that Ned illuminates the brave past and even braver present of George Smiley, the reluctant keeper of the flame, as Smiley combines within himself the ideal and the reality of the Circus. And I will end with a quote from the book jacket ofThe Secret Pilgrim:“‘The Secret Pilgrim’ holds us galvanized by its storytelling genius, by its perceptions of the moral conundrums at the heart of our society, and by its singular grasp of the myths and fantasies underlying the condition of nations. It is John le Carre’s most magnificent novel.”
