Herbert and Ransom unite to revisit a former world of Herbert's called Destination: Void, a serialized story about a brain operating an intergalactic vessel loaded with human and faux-human cargo. The duo expanded on the idea for The Jesus Incident, with Herbert once again blending theology, philosophy, and speculative fiction hijinks as we visit an egg floating in space called Pandora, a world replete with its proliferation of lifeforms hazardous to those aiming to colonize it. As we work to visualize such a world outside of the popularized version of it that (perhaps) James Cameron fused into his Avatar series as he's mined quite a lot from hard science fiction over the years, we also get the classic Herbertisms: political intrigue, ulterior plots and motivations, alien ecology, and women displaying feats of physiological strength in the raw. The approach that aged sci-fi tends to take as we veer from the gaze of a once-male-dominated genre. Yet we get visions of the Crucifixion and electronically powered alien kelp that has its own language living with behemoth jellyfish creatures that float across the terrain, and "demon" worms that attack en messe looking to lay its eggs in your brainstem. There's a lot to unpack here with its cast navigating life on the planet and life with Ship, an artificial intelligence that has taken on a godlike conceit. It can only end badly as battle is waged, entire populations eradicated, and a strange birth takes place. Again, classic Herbert, though it appears to be the first installment of a greater arc that is yet to make its story as corporeal as was in its first volume. This concept of a techno-deity and a hostile alien ecology against the backdrop of these lives is both effervescent and daunting at times, especially when Herbert cannot help but vocalize his characters in blunt, plastic dialogue without any fleshy exterior or warm, pulsating interior despite these internalized subjective thoughts he'd used to effect in . Still, I'm interested in seeing where he takes this.
