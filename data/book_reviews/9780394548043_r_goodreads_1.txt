Phenomenally written retelling of life before and in the camps during the Cambodian genocide.

Throughout the first half of the book, Someth describes his early life in Cambodia spent with his family. I grew up Cambodian American, with only bits and pieces of Cambodia and Khmer in my every-day life, yet always felt so detached and isolated. In each description of folklore and tales passed down in his early life, I recalled my grandparents telling me the same ones when I was a child. While reading Part 1, I found myself excited to tell my mother about the parallels between Someth's early life and my grandparents stories. It's such a uniquely beautiful connection that I haven't felt much before in my life.

The raw description of Someth May's life in the camps was gut-wrenching, and I couldn't help but recall the ways my own family had detailed how their loved ones were killed in the genocide as they have told me throughout my life. It was difficult not to envision their deaths over and over through reading, as what Someth had experienced was so similar. It made my heart ache in the way you miss someone, but I have never met them before in my own lifetime.

I appreciated Someth's description of famine, and the emphasis on manmade famine and inequity during the Khmer Rouge regime. The confusion among the people and the clarification in hindsight was very immersive. He provides political context to the genocide while also keeping you following his life as a teenager.

Anyone who is interested in reading about Cambodia and its modern history should absolutely read Someth's story. His description of conditions and events in the genocide is insanely detailed despite long-term periods of torture and no possible way to write any notes. It often feels rare to find autobiographies from Cambodian genocide survivors, and it is commendable that he has left such an important piece for us to remember not only himself, but the lives of millions by.
