I believe we are fooling ourselves if we think God is with us. We are a ridiculous group who are rightly called Lo-Ammi: "Which means not my people, for you are not my people, and I am not your God." Hs. 1.9 and we very seldom do right by each other or by God. We are only becoming more ridiculous and sinful by the day and it does not seem that putting this or that fellow in office fixes anything at all, so it is natural to ask, "Where is God in all of this?"

Interestingly enough, God seems to have checked out halfway through the history of Israel, and there is a consistent pattern of the Hebrew Bible's narrative- of God's role becoming less and less focused, and man's action becoming more and more prominent. God is closest to us and literally walks with us in the opening chapters of Genesis. By Exodus he appears as a pillar and cloud and leaves the people after guiding them to Canaan. His miracles are notably smaller and smaller, by Kings He never performs a public miracle again. His "Name and Glory" is mentioned for the last time in Kings as well. Some private miracles occur until Ezra-Nehemiah. By the book of Esther, God is never mentioned.

This should not be a surprise. Whereas causing his face to shine upon you is God's favor and guidance, His hidden face is the opposite- reservation. What are we to make of the hidden face of God in our day? As Gideon says, A position of constant struggle between boundaries and responsibilities is the answer Friedman gives. When humans are made, we are made in 'the image of God'. Now, whatever you believe this phrase means, whether God literally looks like humans (male and female, which brings some odd images to mind), the image of God also pertains to our role in the world. Our blessing and call for dominion is an express example of this concept, but one may wonder if the snake's temptation of the knowledge and redefinition of good and evil would have worked on, say, a dog.

In this it tracks that humans are born at odds with God. Jacob meets God in the wilderness and immediately wrestles with Him- if you met God, is that the first thing you would do? (I posit Yes, in one way or another.) From the conception of humankind we take from God's authority and give it to ourselves. The tree of knowledge was in God's position to define what is right and wrong, and we steal it for ourselves, to redefine good and evil in our eyes- with disastrous consequences. The first thing we do when closest to God is try to become God ourselves. We try again to reach the heavens with the Tower of Babel, and again we are stifled. As Zarathustra says, "If there were gods, how could I endure it not to be one! Thus there is no god." It seems that the human condition is one of struggle with the almighty simply by knowing there is an almighty. After all, the wilderness generation of Exodus, closest to God every day (he literally tents with them) is the most rebellious generation of all.

Thus Friedman says humankind cannot be all we can be in the presence of a god. Whatever the "image of God" entails it may be fundamentally at odds with God's nature and sovereignty. This explains the innate disgust humans have with Reformed theology- 'nobody is born a Calvinist'- We want our free will, logically at odds with an omniscient God who has to know what we will do. Whether we believe we are rightly damned by God or not, very few humans can say without pinching themselves that it is just and fair for the potter to have authority over the clay. Therefore God has hidden His face, to see what our end shall be. We have become so far removed from Him that we forgot how to say His name, His miracles become a moment of the past, up for debate. Most strikingly, when He returns to save us, we don't recognize Him anymore; we kill Him.

'So our coming of age leads us to a true recognition of our situation before God. God would have us know that we must live as men who manage our lives without Him.' We have done a poor job without the training wheels, as much as God has attempted to guide and restrict us. The wrestling match is over, we won, now what? It is frightening to give up God. Many of us live our lives in a legalistic way that fulfills the terms of the covenant, secure knowing we are doing what our creator wants. We like the covenant, because God lays out explicitly what He expects of humans and what we may expect of Him. But God is unknowable, and as long as we flow out into a God we sacrifice flowing into building ourselves.

In the Bava Metzia there is a curious story of a legal disagreement with two interpretations. A majority opinion is reached but Rabbi Eliezer is not in consensus. God is on Eliezer's side, and performs many miracles, which the majority all scoff and handwave. Finally God's voice from heaven comes down and tells the school that Eliezer is right and not to discount him. The congregation, then, quotes Deuteronomy 30.12- "It [the accordance] is not in heaven." (The Torah is with man.) God laughs, goes back to heaven, and says, "My children have defeated me. My children have defeated me."
