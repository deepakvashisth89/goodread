“At the age of twenty-four, I was more prepared for death than I was for life. My first experience of the world outside the classroom had been war…I learned the murderous trade at Quantico, Virginia, practiced it in the rice paddies and jungles around Danang, and then taught it to others at Camp Geiger, a training base in North Carolina. When my three-year enlistment expired in 1967, I was almost completely ignorant about the stuff of ordinary life, about marriage, mortgages, and building a career. I had a degree, but no skills. I had never run an office, taught a class, built a bridge, welded, programmed a computer, laid bricks, sold anything, or operated a lathe. But I had acquired some expertise in the art of killing…”-	Philip Caputo,A Rumor of WarFor good reason, America’s war in Vietnam remains the most controversial conflict in its history. It was fought for unclear reasons premised more on domestic political considerations than a cogent foreign policy. It encompassed two decades of gradual escalation that not only failed to avoid the sunk cost fallacy, but actively embraced it as an organizing principle. It killed and wounded thousands of Americans and millions of Vietnamese. It cost billions of dollars, ravaged a countryside, and alienated an entire generation.In tallying up the costs, it is obvious that Vietnam’s miseries were orders of magnitude higher than that suffered by the United States. But at least the North Vietnamese understood their purpose. They knew exactly what they were fighting for. The same could not be said for the Americans they faced. For many American soldiers, Vietnam was a bewildering and terrifying tour undertaken without objective, often at terrible cost to the body and the soul.The apparent senselessness of America’s misadventures in Southeast Asia has resulted in a robust literary output, proving that history is not written only by the winners. Some of the best of these titles come from the participants themselves, working through their tangled memories and raw emotions on the page.Even in a crowded field that includes celebrated writers such as Michael Herr, Tim O’Brien, and Karl Marlantes, Philip Caputo’sA Rumor of Warstands out. Justly hailed as a classic, it is an unflinching look at the nature of war, which necessarily involves an unflinching look at the nature of man.***A Rumor of Waris a rather slim book, just over three-hundred pages in length. It is divided into three sections covering the period from 1964-1965. There is also an epilogue that takes place ten years after the central narrative, when Caputo was a journalist in Saigon covering the last stages of North Vietnam’s dismantling of South Vietnam’s armed forces.The first section covers Caputo’s military training and his arrival in Vietnam. Unlike many who followed in his footsteps, Caputo was not a reluctant draftee. Rather, he joined the Marine Corps following his graduation from college with a major in English. As he readily admits, Caputo wanted in on the action, a desire that he interrogates throughout. Upon reaching Vietnam, he was given command of a rifle platoon charged with providing security for the airbase at Danang.The second section details Caputo’s time serving on the brigade staff as the casualty documenter. This entailed the writing of clinical descriptions of combat deaths, a job that he relates with pitch black humor. Though safer than being out in the field, Caputo desired to return to platoon leader.In the final section, Caputo gets his wish and returns to a rifle platoon. In the book’s most blistering sequences, he encounters booby traps and confused firefights, all culminating in his implication in the murder of two Vietnamese civilians.***Good soldiers do not necessarily make good writers, while good writers are not typically soldiers. The advantage that Caputo brings to the table is that he was already a writer-in-the-making when he joined the Marines.Caputo writes in clear prose, eschewing the hallucinatory adornments often found in the literature on Vietnam. Though his presentation is not stylized, he is still quite capable of evoking a strong sense of place. Caputo is also very good at developing a set piece, whether that is a clash of arms, a visit to a brothel, or a grim scene in which a general has dead Vietnamese corpses laid out for viewing by American servicemen.***Caputo is not simply interested in relating what happened to him in Vietnam, but in meditating on how it felt. For example, he does a deep examination on the thrill he felt in going into battle. Terrified as he might have been, he also enjoyed it.The heart ofA Rumor of Waris Caputo’s court-martial for a double murder carried out by his men. This episode is unflinchingly confessional, as Caputo tries to untangle the moral knot that led to the deaths. One of the reasons it is so effective is that he is not trying to defend or excuse himself. Caputo’s account makes the killings feel both entirely avoidable and utterly inevitable. Without straining, he turns this intimate bit of violence into a microcosm for the Vietnam War writ large.As noted above,A Rumor of Waris part of a relatively vast collection of Vietnam War accounts. Many are more graphic, detail more atrocities, and entail larger battles. Part of what makes Caputo’s work special is that it was on the cutting edge of this trend. While not exceptionally shocking now, it is worth remembering that it was among the first to tackle the first-person perspective of a United States soldier in Vietnam.***It almost goes without saying thatA Rumor of Waris not about the big picture in Vietnam. Caputo lays out the basics, but this is a stripped-down, intensely personal chronicle. Unlike David Hackworth’sAbout Face, he does not spend a lot of time critiquing the civilian and military leaders whose mistakes, self-deceptions, and outright lies allowed such a slow-moving disaster to occur.In other words, it helps to have some prior idea of the overall context before picking this up. Though I have had this on my shelf for ages, I waited until I knocked out a half-dozen general nonfiction histories of Vietnam before even considering this. With Caputo’s recent death – as an old man, despite everything he faced – I figured the time was right.***An interesting aspect ofA Rumor of Waris that it occurs so early in the period of American direct combat operations. Caputo served in-country before Tet, before My Lai, before the bombing of Cambodia. The war had not yet turned into a stalled-out attritional slog against a foe who lived on the land being fought over, and therefore could not be effectively attrited without being totally annihilated. While many blunders had already been made, the costliest were still to come.Despite this,A Rumor of War– which came out two years after the fall of Saigon – provides a very stark indication of the road ahead. It demonstrates in miniature the largescale madness of the United States’s intervention, in which the most powerful nation on earth tried to “save” a far-smaller country from its own people, and attempted to do so by killing those it intended to help.
