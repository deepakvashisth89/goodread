Bukowski has legions of fans who revere his candor, while some find him despondent and crude to the point of nihilistic. It’s easy to admire his rebellious persona and his unfiltered voice that scrutinizes everything and anything, regardless if it offends or disturbs. Just like the impact of alcohol that undergirds much of his work, the poetic free verse memories and anecdotes complied in What Matters Most is How Well You Walk Through the Fire often have a moody quality that consistently veers into moments of sobering rumination and sage intuition.

What I find undeniable about Bukowski’s work is how addictive his prose can be and how hard it is to refrain from wanting to hear more about his nemeses: cigarettes, booze, gambling, and women. However, the raw and personal agenda of over 400 pages of Bukowski’s free verse reveals him as much more than bawdy and depressive. He can be tender and deadly funny, but more often than not he can be deeply introspective and speculative, yearning and declaring his and our existence into the void that life can feel like when we’re lost, and for Bukowski when life is hard, he knows how to express how cruel, unyielding, numbing, bleak, and blasphemous it can be.

What I take away most from this encompassing volume of Bukowski’s poetry is his stubbornness to persist, to conclude that life is worth struggling and suffering through amid all the shame and regret and all the careless and hurtful acts we and others commit because if we keep going and forgive we can experience flashes of bliss, comfort, and serenity, moments of finding acceptance with self and others, of finding solace and purpose, regardless whether life can challenge us daily with feelings of meaninglessness.
