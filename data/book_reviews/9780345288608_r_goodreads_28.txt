"Everything written with vitality expresses that vitality: there are no dull subjects, only dull minds. All men who read escape from something else into what lies behind the printed page; the quality of the dream may be argued, but its release has become a functional necessity.All men must escape at times from the deadly rhythm of their private thoughts. It is part of the process of life among thinking beings. It is one of the things that distinguish them from the three-toed sloth; he apparently—one can never be quite sure—is perfectly content hanging upside down on a branch, not even reading Walter Lippmann.I hold no particular brief for the detective story as the ideal escape. I merely say that all reading for pleasure is escape, whether it be Greek, mathematics, astronomy, Benedetto Croce, or The Diary of the Forgotten Man. To say otherwise is to be an intellectual snob, and a juvenile at the art of living."A brilliant and very funny essay, often unfairly caricatured by later critics as a purely negative appreciation of the preceding era of "classical" detective fiction (Christie, Sayers, et al.) when it's clear that, in fact, Chandler harbours a deep - if admittedly begrudging - affection for the period. Studded throughout with surprisingly insightful critical and psychological gems, like the above cited passage.
