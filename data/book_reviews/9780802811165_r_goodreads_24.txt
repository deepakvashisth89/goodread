This collection of nine sermons from 1939-1956 almost perfectly encapsulates, in separate essays, the things about C.S. Lewis that I love and the things about him that drive me nuts. I am rating it a three as a matter of averages. It contains some of the best work he ever did, and some of the worst.Two of the essays are superb and make me want to call myself whatever kind of Christian C.S. Lewis was. The title essay, "The Weight of Glory," is justly famous for its peroration that goes like this:It is a serious thing to live in a society of possible gods and goddesses, to remember that the dullest most uninteresting person you can talk to may one day be a creature which,if you saw it now, you would be strongly tempted to worship, or else a horror and a corruption such as you now meet, if at all, only in a nightmare. All day long we are, in some degree helping each other to one or the other of these destinations. It is in the light of these overwhelming possibilities, it is with the awe and the circumspection proper to them, that we should conduct all of our dealings with one another, all friendships, all loves, all play, all politics. There are no ordinary people. You have never talked to a mere mortal. Nations, cultures, arts, civilizations - these are mortal, and their life is to ours as the life of a gnat. But it is immortals whom we joke with, work with, marry, snub, and exploit - immortal horrors or everlasting splendors.This is a great sermon. Perhaps one of the greatest ever sermed. But there is an equally great one towards the end of the book called "Membership" in which Lewis explores the difference between participation in the Body of Christ and affiliation with a collective. Here is the money quote from that sermon, which, I believe, should give serious pause to anyone who claims membership in a religious congregation, since it means that we are probably doing it wrong:I am afraid that when we describe a man as "a member of the Church" we usually mean nothing Pauline; we mean only that he is a unit - that he is one more specimen of some kind of things as X and Y and Z. How true membership in a body differs from inclusion in a collective may be seen in the structure of a family. . . . If you subtract any one member, you have not simply reduced the family in number; you have inflicted an injury on its structure. Its unity is a unity of unlikes, almost of incomensurables.These are the highlights of the collection, and they are easily worth the purchase price of the book (especially since there are tons of used copies on Amazon for, like, a dollar).But there are also some sermons representing Lewis at his worst, such as "Why I am Not a Pacifist." The core argument here is fine--it is the same argument that Orwell makes in about 1/4 the space in his classic essay "Pacifism and the War." But the way that Lewis prosecutes the argument is pedantic, overbearing, and, ultimately intellectually unfair, because he hides under the cloak of the unimpeachable Oxford don who (he seems to feel) must be allowed to state what is true and what is beautiful without being challenged.|And then there are essays in which Lewis plays his favorite game of "hide the fundamentalism"--or where he takes a very hard line on biblical literalism and pretends that anybody who claims to be a Christian in any sense of the word could not possibly disagree with a particular sense in which he reads the Bible. He does this most in the essay "Transposition," but he also does it in "Learning in War-Time," a somewhat-dated sermon in which he defends the young men who want to go to Oxford instead of fighting Nazis. Here he says, for example,I know that many wiser and better Christians than I in these days do not like to mention heaven and hell even in a pulpit. I know, too, that nearly all the references to this subject in the New Testament come from a single source. But then that source is Our Lord Himself. People will tell you it is St. Paul, but that is untrue. These overwhelming doctrines are dominical. They are not really removable from the teaching of Christ or of His Church. If we do not believe them, our presence in this church is great tomfoolery.This is the version of C.S. Lewis that drives me nuts--the Lewis who says that somebody who does not take a traditional Christian view of the doctrine of hell (which, full disclosure, I do not accept) is intellectually deficient if they try to derive any other benefit from Christianity. He combines an unsophisticated biblical literalism with an intellectual smugness and then dares his reader to disagree--either from an intellectual perspective or from a religious perspective--while he bludgeons them with whichever tool they leave in the shed.In all, I would say that my three-star rating of this book corresponds exactly to my rating of CS Lewis as a Christian intellectual. Sometimes I read him and think, "wow, this is the best explanation of the topic I have ever seen, and he speaks the words of my heart with rhetorical power I can only dream about." And sometimes I read him and think, "what an insufferable, arrogant bastard." Both versions of the man showed up for this book.
