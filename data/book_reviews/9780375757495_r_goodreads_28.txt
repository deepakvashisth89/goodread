Victober Read No. 1

My favorite time of year, literature-wise, has to be Victober. If I must be thankful for my passion for books and reading as much as I do, I must thank Victorian authors for turning me into the reader I am now. Charlotte and Emily Brontë as well as Dickens must be right there at the podium. This year, 2024, I wasn't thinking of participating in Victober, for the first time in four years. It was already October 20, and not a single Victorian writer had been brought up to mind. Then, I thought, if I skip it this year, I will regret it for the rest of my life (don't forget I tend to be a little dramatic at times). Fair enough, I said to myself, what if I pick up two short novels, and one regular one, and then I can say I did Victober and call it a day? Three short books are better than nothing. But what books? Trollope? No, usually too long. Charlotte Brontë? Mm, a lot of French in the ones I have not read, or so I was told. Mary Elizabeth Braddon? I'm afraid I'm not up for sensation novels right now...But, wait a second? I was planning to finish up this year, I must have written about it here, in my notes...mm....yes, right here. It says, 'Read all of George Eliot in 2024'. That was my goal. I don't think I'll make it on time, but never mind. is huge for only 10 days, and I have to read two more authors besides. ? I am reading that one with J. and the book club later before Christmas, 'Don't forget that.' But, wait, ...how many pages?...30...no, around 200 hundred pages. That works. Yes, it does. Let's pick it up right away—this is, more or less, how I ended up here.

is probably my least favorite George Eliot novel so far, yet it still has all the author's essence and the complexity we are used to when reading her books. It is a tale about forgiveness and acceptance, especially when life has been tough, and one must try till the end. That is the case of our protagonist, Silas, a weaver whose situation is not easy by any means: when he is found guilty of something he didn't do, he is forced to start over and look for new opportunities in a different place. Here, after some turmoil, he will receive some news that might change his life forever. Some hope, we can say that.

In truth, this novel reminded me of a little. It has a similar atmosphere, the setting is very much alike—although the countryside is typical in almost all Eliot's works—and organic protagonists for whom the reader cares. The problem here, though, is that there are unnecessary characters whose roles in the story don't affect the main plot, on the contrary, they make the story tedious at some point. Although I don't blame Eliot here because I'm used to her chapters where she describes the surroundings and the people who live there, I wasn't expecting this to happen in a short novel. That said, I found the descriptions top-notch and so beautifully written. I would also add that the ending was worthwhile. Had the ending been different—there are at least two possible ways this could have ended—I would have given no more than 3 stars to the book. I saw it coming and I was thinking "Please, Eliot, don't you dare to end the story like this. I know you care about him, be easy on him." And she did something even better than I could have possibly imagined. I was satisfied. Then I moved to the next Victorian author.
