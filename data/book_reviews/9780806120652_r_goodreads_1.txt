This short book is an excellent resource for anyone interested in how archaeology enhances our understanding of past events. It also offers some great detail into the Battle of Little Big Horn. One caution is that you should know a bit about the battle before reading this book. While the authors provide a summary, knowing the common beliefs about the fight help the reader understand how the archaeology confirms or dispels the common assumptions. I originally read this book several years ago to support our ROTC staff ride, and I’m just now organizing my notes into something coherent.The story begins with a 1983 prairie fire that uncovered a wealth of artifacts hidden under dense grass and sagebrush. This discovery sparked an ambitious effort to map and interpret the battlefield. While the archaeological team cataloged skeletal remains, clothing, equipment, and other items, the highlight for me was the analysis of ammunition shells. Using modern forensic techniques, the team was able to trace individual weapons as they moved across the battlefield, revealing larger patterns and offering fresh insights into what might have happened.The artifacts paint a picture of the battle’s flow. Once Gall and the warriors cross the Big Horn at Medicine Tail Coulee, the casings show a double envelopment around Custer Ridge. The evidence suggests Calhoun’s soldiers executed a fighting withdrawal but that the south skirmish line and Keogh troops did not. It shows that some Native American warriors uncharacteristically took position on Calhoun’s Hill and sniped at the soldiers from over 400 meters away.The .45/55 casings from the Army Springfields found among Native American positions add a layer of mystery. Were these from weapons captured here or at the Rosebud or does it show retreating cavalrymen under heavy pressure? Likely both, and it’s this kind of conjecture that makes the book so compelling.The casing analysis offers more, however. One old myth is that the trapdoor Springfield, the standard Army cavalry weapon, had tendency to jam once hot put the soldiers at a disadvantage. The evidence showed a total weapon failure rate of about 3% on both sides. That means the infamous extraction issues of the cavalry’s trapdoor Springfield rifles weren’t the deciding factor in the battle—weapons reliability was a problem both sides faced equally.The authors also used statistical analysis to explore the firepower dynamics of the battle. Of the 119 firearms identified from the battlefield (not counting the Springfields or Colts), 55 were modern repeating rifles. In terms of firepower, the Native American warriors had a clear edge – with at least one repeater for every soldier on the battlefield.At its core, this book is about the archaeological process, the insights it provides and how it was done in a particular place. In the end, the authors introduce an anthropological concept called the "battlefield pattern," which ties artifacts to specific moments and movements. By analyzing the spatial and temporal distribution of these artifacts, they reveal the larger patterns of movement and action on the battlefield.
