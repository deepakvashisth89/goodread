Of Wolves and Men is a fascinating exploration of the complex relationship between wolves and humans, weaving biology, history, mythology, admiration, and hatred. Lopez presents this relationship as a profound dichotomy, rooted less in the nature of wolves and more in the nature of humanity itself.

Although some sections are admittedly dry, the author’s ability to craft thought-provoking and often beautiful passages will keep you reading until the end. One of the most profound takeaways that I took from this book is just how little progress has been made in human tolerance of wolves after the calculated extirpation of this species from nearly all its range in the Unted States. Despite being published in 1978, much of the narrative seems as though it could have been written yesterday.

These two passages from the book exemplify this point:

“Throughout history man has externalized his bestial nature, finding a scapegoat upon which he could heap his sins and whose sacrificial death would be his atonement. He has put his sins of greed, lust, and deception on the wolf and put the wolf to death-in literature, in folklore, and in real life. ”

“It seems to me that somewhere in our history we should have attempted to answer to ourselves for all this. As I have tried to make clear, the motive for wiping out wolves (as opposed to controlling them) proceeded from misunderstanding, from illusions of what constituted sport, from strident attachment to private property, from ignorance and irrational hatred. But the scope, the casual irresponsibility, and the cruelty of wolf killing is something else. I do not think it comes from some base, atavistic urge, though that may be a part of it. I think it is that we simply do not understand our place in the universe and have not the courage to admit it.”
 
As Lopez effectively articulates, this is an issue of man not of wolves. In today’s world, wildlife is often constrained by two forms of carrying capacity. The first is biological carrying capacity, determined by the availability of resources. The second is social carrying capacity, or the maximum density of a species that human communities are willing to tolerate. For wolves in America, it is, and has always been, the latter.

Lopez writes:

“The miracle is that in such a climate of human hatred, misunderstanding, and harrasemnt wildlife biologists have managed to bring the wolf out of the darkness of superstition at all.”

While some progress has been made in this realm, there is still much distrust of wildlife managers today.

In the afterword, written in 2003, Lopez reflects on the years since the book’s original publication, particularly the reintroduction of wolves into Western ecosystems and the conflicts that followed. The feelings from these final pages were deeply felt. Wolves did not ask to be returned to landscapes now dominated by humans, just as they never asked to be removed in the first place. Nor will they ever understand that they are seen as competitors for resources by the same species that displaced them and claimed their territory.

My favorite quote appears at the beginning of the book and serves as a fitting summary of my thoughts from the book and its entire narrative:

“The truth is we know little about the wolf. What we know a good deal more about is what we imagine the wolf to be.”
