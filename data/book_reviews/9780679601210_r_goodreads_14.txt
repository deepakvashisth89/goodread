Ok.I had it in my mind to return to Freud, who I find very enjoyable and interesting to read. I had tead this before, well maybe I had read this before, I think I have told a similar story about a couple of other books that I have reviewed on Goodreads, but anyhow when I reached page 552 ( out of 607 pages of text in this edition) I found an old train ticket which I would have been using as a bookmark. So it is quite possible that I read up to that point and then stopped for some reason, Freudian or otherwise.Before going further I must acknowledge that two Goodreads friends have taught me that Freud wasn't a particularly nice man. MihaElla that when Freud escaped Vienna that he did not help his sisters to leave the country; and Katia N that he was a patrichal and demanding husband. All the same I enjoy reading his writing fir two reasons in particular.Firstly, while on the one hand he is an Enlightenment figure, in that he believes that the world and people can be understood through the application of human reason. The world may appear random and crazy, but the thoughtful person, with application and science can explain it. But what does hisapplied reason show us? Only that beneath the rational surface of his society, the reality is anything but rational, and to make it worse (or maybe better for us psychologically) we are unconscious of our drives towards self-destruction or subordination, while being superbly capable of providing reasonable and rational explanations for this.Secondly, for me, Freud reads people and society as we might read a novel; you might convincingly argue that I have phrased this back to front. I think I read somewhere that somebody put it to Arthur Schnitzler that his fiction was inspired by Freud's ideas to which he angrily replied that he and writers like him had those ideas long before the likes of Freud had got off the train from Moravia, and on the contrary, Freud was inspired by them.Something not really relevent to the Interpretation of Dreams but which as a confirmed optimistwhich I am because I certain that things can always get worseI also appreciate, is Freud's social pessimism as seen incivilisation and its discontentsand that pleases me enormously.This book is a bit odd because Freud continued to revise and reprint it over several decades, he even encorpriates responses to earlier editions in later ones so the text becomes a little circular, claiming that work inspired by it validates the original thesis. Parts read very much likethe psychopathology of everyday life, publish a couple of years before the first edition of the interpretation of dreams, other section feel weighed down by his slightly later theoretical structures, leaving those to one side his original discussion of the various types of dreams of a healthy person seems fairly constrained, even unremarkable.What stood out for me though was, as in "psychopathology" was his interest in word play and cultural references. The unconscious for Freud, apparently has a sense of humour and a good all round literary education. Only one dream discussed by him is from a working class woman. Despite this, Freud , I guess managed to articulate and publish something that was resonant to many readers, even beyond the German linguistic frontiers, possibly because what he had to say about the unconscious and how itvreveals itself intuitively made sense to readers of fiction, particularly those who had read the more pyschological fiction of the nineteenth century, or works which were symbolist, or drew on the structures and patterns of folk or fairy tales.I have a couple more books by Freud to get to on my shelves, if I will read them this year or not perhaps only an old train ticket could say.
