The start was quite promising. I really liked how Postman used the works of Orwell and Huxley to illustrate his arguments. Even though Postman's focus is on TV, it translates well to the current situation with our phones. I think that there are many truths in this book, things that are still relevant today, and some of his predictions are our reality. It did make me see different perspectives, and I will never be able to normally watch the news or a talk show ever again haha.

However, by the end of the book, the content felt flat and repetitive to me. Each chapter ended up having almost the exact same conclusion. Besides that, Postman has written and defended his statements in a way that made him feel unreliable to me. Instead of using factual sources, he theorized a lot and wrote his own (biased) examples like they were factual. The thing that frustrated me the most was when he erased an entire group of people to make his argument seem convincing. At some point, he mentions literacy rates in the US (17th century) and says (in my words): "Yay, look how good women and men were at reading, so smart and it didn't vary on a large scale! Now everybody illiterate, TV bad :(". I mean, yeah, kind of true, but you also completely left out the Native Americans and slaves that made up a big chunk of the population, and then still, there were very specific groups that had access to these resources.

Postman really was on to something, but I wish he either wrote this book in a 'nuanced theorizing' way or a 'factual, one clear stance' way if that makes sense. This could also be a very personal problem though. I would still recommend reading this book and finding things out on your own. It really makes you see some different perspectives regarding digital literacy.
