Having read and enjoyed Beerbohm's sardonic "deal with the Devil" story, "Enoch Soames" some years ago (you can check it out here, and be sure to check out the historical "after the fact" event as reported by Teller - of Penn & Teller - here) - as well as "Hilary Maltby And Stephen Braxton" and Beerbohm's essay "A Defence Of Cosmetics" - I placed this oddity on my "to read" list, and promptly forgot about it. A couple of years ago, editing a 1930s story for my day job, a character had a boat christened "Zuleika Dobson" and I thought "that sounds familiar", and so moved the book a little closer in my reading list, after checking. And so here we are...

This is not a book for everyone. I am not even sure it's a book for me, although all evidence would point that it should be: The fabulously wealthy and incredibly handsome, arrogant and dismissive Duke of Dorset, the Perfect Dandy, is attending Oxford when Miss Zuleika Dobson visits. Miss Dobson, a world-famous magician and charmingly beautiful woman, accidentally succeeds in enthralling the entirety of Oxford's students through her sheer magnetism. The Duke (endlessly chased by gold-diggers, and self-presumedly immune to the wiles of women due to his Dandyish nature) succeeds in ignoring Miss Dobson for an entire shared meal, but even he must admit, by the next day, that he has fallen in love. Which turns out to be the problem. Because Miss Dobson, immune to men's charms (after having been romanced the world over by crawling sycophants), has taken the Duke's indifference as proof that he is the first man really deserving of her love. That wouldn't seem to be a problem, but when the Duke confesses that he has fallen for her, Zuleika realizes that she cannot love him now. Which breaks the Duke's heart, upon which he decides he will drown himself the next day, which Zulieka agrees is probably the best course of action. Will love prevail? Will The Duke take the final plunge? Will the entirety of the Oxford student body follow suit? ("To 'Die For Miss Dobson' was 'the thing to do.')

You probably shouldn't read too many of the other Goodreads reviews here too closely, because spoilers don't seem to be a consideration. Yes, this is black comedy, although of a gentle sort, lacking any real morbidity or bite and more construed to make a gentleman (drinking his brandy and smoking his cigar next to the fire) chuckle into his beard at the sheer dashed audacity of it all (or, put another way, it's a bit naughty but not in the way you are thinking). In fact (not really knowing the field well enough) I might hazard a guess that ZULEIKA DOBSON is one of the earliest examples of a truly "cult" novel, intended for certain sensibilities. Despite the rather arch proceedings, you also get a primer on Life At Oxford at the time (Beerbohm himself was a graduate) - with all the minutia, history, folklore, traditions and whatnot. There's also light lashings of fantasy (observing Roman busts, the ghosts of Henri Chopin and George Sand) and metatextual frippery (The Author moves among the characters like a ghost, privy to their inner thoughts, thanks to magical help from Olympus), all in service of the ironic humor.

I liked it, although (as another reviewer notes) the rather extended time put into The Duke's agonizing and emotional gymnastics can get a bit wearing (said reviewer was moved by them, I was not) - still, they are a nice illumination of the psychology of The Dandy (which means more than just the "Beau Brummel/received cultural term" we think of now) as it operates under duress. I also like the fact that Miss Dobson isn't actually a very good magician (as evidenced in one scene), her worldwide fame arising from her charm, character and insouciant sparkle (in a sense, you could argue she's kind of a late Victorian/early Edwardian version of our own "manic pixie dream-girl" concept) and that she's not "traditionally" (i.e. "classically") beautiful (being from the lower classes, she has a bit of "Gypsy" blood about her). There's a good bit with American Rhodes Scholars students and their presumptuous neurosis. I also liked the final scenes involving Noakes "at the window", which culminates in a darkly funny way (just edging onto morbid).

Of course, it's a mistake to read too deeply into the book, looking for symbolism or deeper meaning - one might as well search a cream cake for nutrition. I wondered, as I was reading, if perhaps Miss Dobson was meant to symbolize the concept of "unattainable Glory" that lured two generations of Brits to their deaths in WWI, but seeing that the book was written in 1911, that's patently impossible. Also, unlike another reviewer, I don't think a movie version (even "at the time" like the 1930s) would have been a good idea - the whole thing is too internal and self-referential, really, and I imagine putting a character like The Duke on screen would make him more insufferable than he comes across in print, where you at least have recourse to his internal thoughts.

An odd book. Glad I read it, not knee-slappingly funny or anything (I chuckled more at ) but it has some funny lines and moments, and illustrates a certain way of viewing the world that has mostly come and gone.
