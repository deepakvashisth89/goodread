Linguistics is often an appealing subject for sci-fi, offering a chance to apply its principles to completely new and (literally) alien examples. China Miéville's Embassytown revolves around a species which speaks through two mouths simultaneously (so characters are called things like ᴱᶻ⁄ᵣₐ) and whose language is incapable of untruths; Ted Chiang's ‘Story of Your Life’ (the basis for the Villeneuve film Arrival) concerns the translation of an alien language which operates in the past, present and future simultaneously.

Annoyingly, these ideas tend to be, even hypothetically speaking, linguistically nonsense, which most of the time is totally fine for a sci-fi story. I had higher hopes for , though, just because Suzette Haden Elgin actually had a PhD in linguistics – and it's true that, to the extent that we learn anything about Láadan (the language of this novel), it makes sense. But we learn almost nothing about it – in fact I'm not sure a single word of Láadan appears in the book, and there's only very minimal discussion of its features. I found this a bit frustrating given that the creation of this language is what the whole book is about.

is set in the 22nd century, after the US Constitution has formally declared women to be inferior to men. Earth is in contact with an increasing number of alien species, whose languages must be decoded in order to make interplanetary trade deals: society has become dominated by a hereditary group of Linguist families, who are hated by the general population for their elite status.

I just found a lot of the details a bit baffling or contradictory. Women are subjugated, yet still work important jobs as linguists. The linguist families are known to exist outside the US as well, yet women's status is supposedly based on the US Constitution; what's going on with women in Europe or elsewhere? The men are so cartoonishly misogynistic and stupid that it's a bit difficult to take it seriously. And the linguistic details are…unnecessarily outré (for instance, we learn that when a baby is exposed to a ‘non-humanoid’ alien language, it physically explodes from non-compatibility). Elgin's writing is also extremely clunky.

It's instructive to compare this with Atwood's , which came out the following year, and which is subtler, more nuanced, and a lot more plausible as a feminist dystopia. Also, since it's a lot better written than this is, it even seems to have more to say about language than this does. is worth a read because its ideas are so interesting, but the execution falls a long way short.
