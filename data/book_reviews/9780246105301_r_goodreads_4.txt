A strange book; almost like two distinct books.The first part, dealing with the origin and history of lighter-than-air-craft travel (as well as comparisons to heavier-than-air-craft) is highly-interesting and informative; but the author's account of the Hindenburg's tragic final journey is pretty much historical fiction.Why the author would lump all of his research into what is, in effect, a long prelude to the fiery denouement in May of 1937, seems a worthwhile question. Unfortunately, there's a few generations of the public that have bought the author's sabotage theory of the tragedy (enhanced by the George C. Scott character in the 1975 movie version), when the obvious explanation remains behind the veil of more dramatic conjectures.Although the book's well-written, and a nice read, I can't help but think how much better it would be had the sabotage theory, which had plenty of plausibilty behind it, was jettisoned, so to speak, for a discussion of the flammable interplay of the hydrogen gas with the chemical coating on the airship's fabric surface. This rather mundane--but, also pun-friendly, explosive explanation--remains the most logical.Misguided by the lack of attribution as we are, the reader is left to wonder which parts of which conversations are invented, and, further, which characters are authentic and which are imaginary. The result is that we end up knowing a lot about zeppelins, and very little about the cause of the fiery death of the Hindenburg, along with a great number of its passengers and crew.At any rate, for technical and background information, this is a valuable book; for anything else, it's a leap in the dark
