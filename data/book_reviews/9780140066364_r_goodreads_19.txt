Where to begin with Arenas’ undefinable masterpiece, Farewell to the Sea? The third in his god-tier Pentagonia series which covers “the secret history of Cuba,” and like the previous two (and perhaps even more so) this is not meant for timid readers. Not at all a novel in the traditional sense, instead here is the burdened testimony of the unjustly imprisoned, of a nation under siege by its own government, of the artist whose heart overruns its ability to articulate itself. Arenas writes like it will save his own life, and perhaps he was. He wrote this while jailed for his homosexuality; the first manuscript was destroyed, another version was rewritten, then confiscated, rewritten a third time, then smuggled out of prison before his release and finally published in Barcelona years later. In the 90s, he committed suicide in New York while suffering from AIDS, with a final letter dedicated to Cuban liberation.

The story follows a small family and told in two parts. First, in the perspective of the wife as a single unbroken paragraph in gorgeous, delirious stream-of-consciousness style. Then, of the husband, as a series of six cantos in a long poetic sequence, merging prose, poetry, flights-of-fancy, and Arenas’ actual autobiography.

There’s so much here—abstractions, the author experimenting within the bounds of literature, pushing and pulling at the limits of fiction, expressing the soul’s inexpressible truths—that it’s impossible to cover in a review. Not everything worked for me, but readers preference doesn’t mean a thing with a tome like this. The man quite literally bled out his divine substance and put it to paper—not just for Cuba, but for all of us. Like his others, this has rewired my brain. Utterly beautiful, magical stuff. I look forward to continuing with the rest of the Pentagonia series after a quick palette cleanse.
