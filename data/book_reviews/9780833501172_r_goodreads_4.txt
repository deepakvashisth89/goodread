I can’t describe how much I enjoyed these two The Book of Lost Tales!
As someone who craves every single word J. R. R. Tolkien ever wrote, I’m immensely grateful that they exist.

Christopher Tolkien guides us brilliantly—through clear and purposeful commentary—across the early versions of events that would later, in their “final” forms, become the canon of The Silmarillion. Readers of The Silmarillion will quickly notice that some well-known stories were radically altered in Tolkien’s revisions, while others remained more or less intact in their original form. You’ll recognize many elements, which makes it possible to follow the evolution of Tolkien’s thought regarding the mythology of Middle-earth. For me, alongside all the fascinating new details, that’s the most beautiful aspect of this work.

I mentioned canon—that’s a word many Tolkien scholars tend to throw around rather loosely. I understand that we primarily rely on The Lord of the Rings and The Silmarillion, and later Unfinished Tales, to construct a coherent canonical history of Valinor and Middle-earth. However, these earliest versions of events are full of information that never made it into the canonical narratives published in The Silmarillion.

My personal idea of canon is a bit hybrid: I mentally incorporate additional details that enrich and expand Tolkien’s world, as long as they don’t alter the core storyline. These are usually broader descriptions or untold parts of stories that don’t interfere with the established narrative. For example, I’m happy to accept the unpublished, detailed account of the battles in the streets and white walls of Gondolin, but I don’t accept the idea that Melkor’s army contained hundreds of Balrogs. Tolkien himself suggested in his letters that there may have been at most seven. If we exclude Durin's Bane, who hid in Moria, we can assume that no more than six Balrogs were slain during the Fall of Gondolin.

My rough count goes like this: one was slain by Glorfindel, one (Gothmog) by Ecthelion, two by Rog and his warriors, and one by Tuor. That would leave two surviving—though it’s possible that Turgon’s guard also killed one. That, in turn, makes Durin’s Bane perhaps the only Balrog to survive even the War of Wrath.

That’s my way of approaching canon in a flexible, hybrid sense. Instead of reducing the Fall of Gondolin to just a few lines in The Silmarillion, I adopt the full original version—but filter out anything that would contradict later developments or the events of the Second and Third Ages.

Take the opportunity to follow the development of Tolkien’s mythology in the best possible way. Expand what you already know—or discover what you don’t—about the events of The Silmarillion by interpreting canon with a certain degree of flexibility. And beyond all that, even what doesn’t directly enrich the narrative will reward you with that unique feeling that only Tolkien’s writing can evoke.
