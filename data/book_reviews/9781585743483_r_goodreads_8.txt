Submariners are like experimental parachute testers; both know that the whole point is the plunge, and despite best practices, they must have a certain amount of faith that they will stop under their own control, not by the earth’s crust.During my formative years as a young adult, I dreamt of becoming a submariner. For unknown reasons, everything I had read or watched about submarines enthralled me. Life chose another path, but stories of those riding beneath the ocean’s surface in big, bloated steel whales continue to fascinate and captivate.Audible, like many other sites, tracks user data. The app knows what I like to read and which titles I’ve consumed most recently, and thus, being the wise and predatory capitalists that they are, the site kindly offers me additional titles to choose from the moment I’ve used up my monthly credit. That’s how this book came into my hands, or more appropriately, my ears.The cover image also helped.A black-and-white image of a submarine making its slow descent into the deep, a stalker, predator and vessel of defence and destruction. How could I resist?The Death of the USS Thresher is a nonfiction account, technical in nature, crisp and concise in prose. The book is a brief read at a mere 208 pages, or a thrifty listen at a hair over five hours. I was all ears from open to close.Author Norman Polmar wrote this shortly after the 1961 tragedy, and it was published in 1964, a year after the Navy Court of Inquiry investigation and Congressional hearings. This book is the revised 2021 edition, following the release of additional official documents in 2021.Polmar has authored 50 books on the US and USSR Naval forces and has served as an advisor to three Secretaries of the United States Navy. He led major projects for the USN, DoD and foreign governments. Polmar is considered one of the finest naval historians by civilian and naval experts.You might expect such a book to be dry, dull, or cumbersome, full of jargon and minutiae of naval life, but, read aloud by Sean Crisden, a veteran voice actor and prolific audiobook narrator, the technical-heavy text remains captivating.The USS Thresher was designed and commissioned during the Cold War between the US and the USSR. The state-of-the-art submarine was the most advanced in anti-detection, searching sonar, depth capability and speed at depth; she was a hunter-killer, seeking, stalking and ready to destroy enemy nuclear submarines. The brand-new nuclear-powered submarine was the fastest and quietest ever put to sea.Two years after being commissioned, the USS Thresher would be lost, taking 129 souls to the ocean floor.On April 10, 1963, under the command of Lt. Commander John Wesley Harvey, he led his crew and an entourage of civilian technicians 220 miles off the coast of Cape Cod to complete dives to her test depth of 1300 feet. To that point, the Thresher had completed numerous such dives without significant issue. She would never return to port.Chaperoned by a support and rescue vessel, the USS Skylark, nothing seemed out of the ordinary. Communications between the two ships by radio and underwater telephone (UQC) were dutifully logged with the Thresher making contact at each designated interval.Then the following three messages from the Thresher changed the exercise from routine to tragic;“Experiencing minor difficulties…”“Have positive up-angle…”“Attempting to blow…”The Skylark continued her attempts to contact the submarine. No calls were answered. The commander of the Skylark transmitted the details to the mainland. Five minutes after receiving the news, the commanding Vice-Admiral dispatched a full rescue mission, calling in every nearby available vessel, including those from the nearby Canadian Navy.An oil slick and some debris in the vicinity all but confirmed the worst outcome. Although not concrete, it was evidence enough, and the rescue operation shifted to recovery and investigation.1300 feet was the designated test depth, but the ocean floor at the exercise site lay much deeper, down to 8500 ft. There was no possibility of survival for those aboard the stricken submarine.The subsequent Naval Court of Inquiry would last several months. Definitive proof of the Thresher’s demise came in the form of photographs and rubber boots marked with the Thresher’s hull number, SSN-593.So, what happened? How did the most technologically advanced nuclear submarine of the time, under the command of a seasoned commander and veteran crew, slip into her demise?Ultimately, that is a question that cannot be definitively answered. The 1963 NCOI concluded that flooding in the engine room caused the nuclear reactor to shut down (Scram). That resulted in power loss, making steering, propulsion and surfacing impossible.Further investigation declared that moisture and particulate contamination in the air lines (a known issue) most likely froze. With no propulsion, the sub was too heavy to blow ballast and rise to the surface. Another suspect was a failed silver-brazed joint in the engine room. Batch testing of silver brazing at the naval shipyard revealed inadequate quality control.However, there were those who refused to believe the loss of the USS Thresher could have come from internal causes and instead searched vehemently for external sources. In due course, enemy action, sabotage and collision were all ruled out as reasonable explanations.In 2021, the United States Navy released the full document load of NCOI and a deeper look at the 1963 findings. According to the updated information, no direct evidence for joint failure was found, changing the ruling from “certain” to “probable only.”SOSUS recordings, the sound surveillance system from fixed seabed hydrophone arrays, determined the following sequence of events; flooding-scram-failed blow-descent-implosion.It was also discovered that Navy engineers warned that ballast would not work below 300–400 feet, making it incapable of saving the submarine at test depth. The nuclear power plant’s shutdown (scram) had a slow, restricted restart procedure. The Navy also concluded that there were significant quality-control failures at the shipyard.Following the disaster, the Navy implemented the SUBSAFE program; protocols determined to fix and safeguard future submarines from the same fate. Many systems were overhauled, including a new and more rapid nuclear reactor restart procedure.The final chapters of the book cover other submarine accidents and disasters, including several incidents from the Soviet submarine program.Being a submariner is a harrowing occupation. The crew enters a metal tube and descends below the ocean’s surface. They are designed to operate in isolation and often, complete silence, cut off from the world above for extended periods of time. The depths at which they operate make rescue near impossible.And yet, despite the ever-present crushing pressures outside the hull and sailing sightless, they still go. Courage is often described as acting in spite of fear; submariners must have reservoirs of the stuff.For a technical, non-combat, peacetime account, The Death of the USS Thresher is educational and holds the reader’s attention. If you’re a reader of naval history or of submarines, this book is a five-star affair.
