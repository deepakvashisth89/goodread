I have David Day’s A Tolkien Bestiary, which is a beautiful collection of artwork and descriptions that very strongly evoke Middle-Earth as Tolkien wrote it. I occasionally browse through it when I want an interesting non-standard monster in an adventure session. It is, however, focused on the things that matter to Tolkien’s readers, not to the characters adventuring through fantasy worlds.

I have never played the ICE middle-earth game, or even fully read the rules, but I have always enjoyed the covers. This sourcebook, however, while aimed at being a MERP sourcebook, is very useful for any fantasy game because it focuses on the things game masters need to know. Where David Day tells us that a thrush carried a message to Bard the Bowman, this book tells us that they speak in high-pitched voices and “make excellent scouts”.



There are two basic sections: normal animals, which includes the intelligent avians and even huge ocean-dwelling eels, and monsters, which includes balrogs, dragons, crebain, and were-worms, and so on. This means it does not have creatures that fit into neither category. For example, it has Huorns because they are monsters, but it does not have Ents, because they are neither animal nor monster.

For the animals, every couple of pages is a map showing where the creatures usually appear in Middle-earth.

The descriptions talk about things like how big the creatures are, their habitats, and how often they are encountered. The game stats (for Rolemaster, which is what MERP used) are in a table in the back. There is a conversion chart for other games in the introduction, converting the 1-100 numbers that Rolemaster uses to the 3d6 and 2d6 rolls that other games use, such as Dungeons & Dragons (3d6) and Tunnels & Trolls (3d6, 2d6).
