To say that General Douglas MacArthur was one of America's most controversial military leaders is an understatement. He hailed from quite a pedigree as the son of an army officer who had won the Medal of Honor during the Civil War and later became the U.S. military governor of the Philippines in the early 1900s.

MacArthur had graduated at the top of his class at West Point (1903) and began a slow, steady upward climb through the ranks. In the process, he learned to be a supreme self-promoter which attested to an ego that did not always do him credit. But as a battlefield commander, MacArthur showed himself to be a brave, resourceful, and intelligent leader in both World Wars. His planning and execution of the landing at Inchon during the early stages of the Korean War was, perhaps, his finest hour as a general officer. But it was his lack of respect for civilian authority over the military which led President Harry Truman to relieve him of his command, that illustrated how MacArthur's inflated sense of self (which some of the junior officers who served under him often encouraged) proved to be his undoing, prompting an abrupt end to a generally distinguished military career.

Manchester does a wonderful job of fleshing out MacArthur in this biography, which was what made for a thoroughly delightful reading experience.
