Released in May 1964, the slim paperback entitled "Weird Tales," edited by Leo Margulies, drew together eight previously uncollected stories from the legendary pulp magazine. It was apparently a successful venture, as just eight months later, its follow-up companion, "Worlds of Weird," was released, another slim paperback that is at least as great as the first. In this volume, Margulies again selects the stories--seven of them, this time--while pulp fiction authority Sam Moskowitz gives us a trenchant general introduction, as well as an intro to each tale. And, oh...for those of you who may have been under the catatonic spell of a centuries-old witch for the past 90 years, "Weird Tales" magazine specialized in horror, fantasy and strange sci-fi material, and over the course of its 32-year run, from 1923-'54, attracted a small but intensely loyal following of both readers and contributors. Its 279 issues introduced readers to dozens of now-famous authors who comprise a veritable Who's Who of some of the finest genre writers of the 20th century. The seven authors in this second collection (four of whom appeared in the first) are all closely associated with the magazine, and so I suppose this volume can serve as a nice introduction to those new to the "Weird Tales" universe. All seven entries are wonderful, and three of them are recognized masterpieces of the short-story form.

As for the stories themselves: The collection kicks off tremendously with a tale that was deemed a masterpiece soon after appearing in the 1/38 issue of "Weird Tales," and it is Seabury Quinn's "Roads." Quinn, of course, was the magazine's most frequently published contributor, with a whopping 145 stories, most of which dealt with the occult detective Jules de Grandin. "Roads," however, is not a de Grandin tale, but something more on the order of a "superhero origin story." Without giving away too much, let's just say that Moskowitz, in his intro, says that it may well be "the greatest adult Christmas story written by an American," and that it tells of a blond-bearded gladiator named Klaus, who has several encounters with a certain well-known Jewish preacher and crucifixion victim over a 30-year period. This story is beautifully written and displays an inordinate amount of historical research on Quinn's part. (Still, Quinn is guilty of several anachronisms here, such as when he mentions Vikings in the year A.D. 30, and the fact that Klaus, after the year A.D. 30, fought Arminius in the Teutoburg Forest, a battle that actually transpired in A.D. 9.) An historical fable that segues into outright fantasy, it is ultimately quite moving and really a bravura performance by its author. No wonder August Derleth gave it the hardcover Arkham House treatment 10 years later, and that it was chosen for inclusion in Cawthorn & Moorcock's "Fantasy: The 100 Best Books." Anyway, next up in this "Weird Tales" assortment is a hard-core fantasy from an author who had previously never let me down, Nictzin Dyalhis (yes, that IS his real name). His story here, however, "The Sapphire Goddess" (from the 2/34 issue)--in which a man is whisked to another dimension, only to realize that he is an amnesia-stricken king caught in a battle between dueling wizards--is a bit too sketchily drawn and fast moving for my tastes; this tale could easily have been expanded to book length for greater effect. Still, fun enough. Up next is another offering from the famous creator of Conan, King Kull and Solomon Kane: Robert E. Howard. "The Valley of the Worm" (also from the 2/34 issue) tells the story of how Niord--an "Aesirian" at the dawn of Earth's history--teamed up with a former enemy, Grom the Pict, to slay the fearsome monster of the title. Exciting as can be, and narrated by one of Niord's future incarnations, the story features no less than three awe-inspiring monsters, one of which is a tentacled horror that H.P. Lovecraft himself might have looked on with approbation. To my mind, a masterpiece from Robert E. Howard, and my favorite story in this volume.

The collection offers yet another masterpiece immediately after, Edmond "The World Wrecker" Hamilton's oft-anthologized "He That Hath Wings" (7/38). This lovely story, of a child born with wings who later attains the gift of flight, and who must make some tough choices after falling in love with a woman who deems him a "freak," is surely one of its author's finest shorter works, both touching and unforgettable. Clark Ashton Smith, one of the magazine's most gifted stylists, is here represented with his tale "Mother of Toads" (again, from the 7/38 issue). This creepy, unpleasant story, which takes place in the France of what might be today or 300 years ago, shows us how dangerous it can be to reject the amorous advances of a witch; the tale's swampy finale should surely satisfy all fans of the macabre. David H. Keller, M.D., a psychiatrist turned sci-fi and horror author, is here shown to good effect with his short tale "The Thing in the Cellar" (3/32), in which 6-year-old Tommy Tucker exhibits an overriding, irrational fear of something in the Tucker house's basement. But IS Tommy's fear so irrational, after all? This story concludes on a surprisingly bleak note, but one that should again greatly please all horror buffs. And the collection is brought to a close with a piece of hard science fiction from Frank Belknap Long, "Giants in the Sky" (8/39), in which a race of beings from the planet Icurus, each being "thousands of miles tall" (!), bags our entire solar system for study under the microscopes of their world. Ultimately rather affecting, with the power of love both uniting two Earthlings while laying low one of the Icurians, the story is somewhat clunkily written but yet manages to impress. Anyway, there you have it...yet another winning batch of stories from "The Unique Magazine." All fans of Golden Age horror, fantasy and sci-fi should most certainly pounce on these "Worlds of Weird"!
