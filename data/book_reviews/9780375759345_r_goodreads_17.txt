This very slim collection of poems has been my faithful nightstand companion for the last couple of weeks. While I cannot claim to fully "get" Eliot’s poetry, I’ve found the experience of reading it rewarding, nevertheless.

“The Waste Land” is, without a doubt, a fascinating text. I do agree, however, with those who argue that it is almost hermetic to a fault. At times, the sheer density of the allusions made it feel less like a poem and more like a scavenger hunt through Western civilization. I too felt that the poem speaks to the intellectual part of the brain —the part that enjoys a good puzzle — rather than the part that gets triggered by beauty.

I found myself returning more often to the earlier poems, such as “The Love Song of J. Alfred Prufrock” and “Preludes”. Reading them, I was reminded of those other “urban stroll” poems I so much love: “Zone” by Apollinaire and “La Passeggiata” by Aldo Palazzeschi.

Compared to the latter two, however, Eliot seems to be a more tragic spirit. Whereas both Apollinaire and Palazzeschi express the exuberance of the modern machine age and the chaotic vitality of the street (with a touch of melancholy in Apollinaire), Eliot depicts the same urban landscape as a site of spiritual exhaustion.

Here is Apollinaire talking about a city dweller who has drank “burning alcohol”, has had enough of “living in Greek and Roman antiquity” and rather marvels at :

“À la fin tu es las de ce monde ancien
Bergère ô tour Eiffel le troupeau des ponts bêle ce matin
Tu en as assez de vivre dans l’antiquité grecque et romaine
Ici même les automobiles ont l’air d’être anciennes
La religion seule est restée toute neuve la religion
Est restée simple comme les hangars de Port-Aviation
(...)
Voilà la poésie ce matin et pour la prose il y a les journaux
Il y a les livraisons à 25 centimes pleines d’aventures policières
Portraits des grands hommes et mille titres divers

J’ai vu ce matin une jolie rue dont j’ai oublié le nom
Neuve et propre du soleil elle était le clairon
Les directeurs les ouvriers et les belles sténo-dactylographes
Du lundi matin au samedi soir quatre fois par jour y passent
Le matin par trois fois la sirène y gémit
Une cloche rageuse y aboie vers midi
Les inscriptions des enseignes et des murailles
Les plaques les avis à la façon des perroquets criaillent
J’aime la grâce de cette rue industrielle
Située à Paris entre la rue Aumont-Thiéville et l’avenue des Ternes”


Palazzeschi sounds even more playful and happy in the way in which he conveys the many voices heard on the modern street:

“– Andiamo?
– Andiamo pure.
All’arte del ricamo,
fabbrica passamanerie,
ordinazioni, forniture.
Sorelle Purtarè.
Alla città di Parigi.
.
Benedetto Paradiso
successore di Michele Salvato,
gabinetto fondato nell'anno 1843.
avviso importante alle signore !
La beltà del viso,
seno d'avorio,
pelle di velluto.
(…)”

Eliot’s stroller, by contrast, focuses on the decay he notices all around him; No “burning alcohol” for him, but rather “faint stale smells of beer”:

“The winter evening settles down
With smell of steaks in passageways.
Six o’clock.
The burnt-out ends of smoky days.
And now a gusty shower wraps
The grimy scraps
Of withered leaves about your feet
And newspapers from vacant lots;
The showers beat
On broken blinds and chimney-pots,
And at the corner of the street
A lonely cab-horse steams and stamps.

And then the lighting of the lamps.”

Pick your favourite and enjoy the read!
