First time reading a first-hand account of a polar expedition and found it a surprisingly enjoyable read. Shackleton is an engaging narrator with a touch of the romantic about his descriptions, a reasonable amount of self reflection/deprecation and a good eye for a funny anecdote about the crew. We get plenty of lead up to the expedition, including the finances and the state of the ship, not hiding the sorry state that either were in. How the Nimrod got to Antarctica with only 3ft 6 of freeboard feels like a miracle.

Life at Cape Royds is also entertainingly described, whether it’s the collapsing beds, monthly baths (or as often as required), or humorous cabin decorations. There’s no doubt that Shackleton views the team as all being incredibly hard working even if the expedition comes across as rather ill thought out as a whole. There are so many scientific instruments on the roof that it started to sag dangerously but “no notice was taken, and nothing happened.” It’s a shame there isn’t enough to build up individual portraits of m/any of the men given that a lot of the crew anecdotes are unassigned, so they just come across as generically nice with all of them getting along which seems unlikely.

The main meat of the book though comes from Shackleton’s account of his attempt on the geographic South Pole which is told through his (supposedly) unedited diary entries with a few added notes. Some people might prefer a more condensed, less repetitive narrative style as we get in the rest of the book but I found it really affective. The repeating diary entries of, “we thought we reach the plateau today but we didn’t, we hauled so many miles and have this many miles to go, we will have to cut back on rations again, and the ship is due to leave in x weeks” really sell the monotony of the physical labour and the decreasing chance of them getting to the Pole, let alone back to the ship. This gets even worse when they do finally turn back and their condition gets steadily worse and worse. Shackleton also has an eye for a great line, such as “difficulties are just things to overcome after all” and “our food lies ahead and death stalks behind”.

If the book had ended there I probably would have given it 5 stars, but we then have the other teams’ mini-expeditions to cover. The Western Party’s account is a relatively concise narrative, not the most exciting but at least reasonably brisk to get through. The main issue is Professor David’s account of the Northern Party’s attempt to get to the magnetic South Pole. It’s told in a strange mix of ‘copied’ diary entries and condensed narrative, though some of the diary entries give information that they found out ‘much later’ so are clearly not as accurate as they are implied to be. It’s difficult not to compare it to the Southern Party’s account, and in comparison it feels like a less impressive copy, there seems to be far less urgency, the goal is far less impressive and the dangers (as they are closer to the base/ship) feel smaller. It also ends before the Southern Party return, so the crew are worrying about the fate of the SP, but we’ve already seen them return in Shackleton’s account. It would have raised the suspense to have the Northern Party’s account before the Southern to build suspense about whether all the men would make it back, but perhaps unsurprisingly Shackleton wanted his story first.

There is a fun section about penguins at the end, which feels very tacked on but does have some funny anthropomorphising. There are also a few maps (but no photos/pictures) in the 2000 Penguin addition at least, but wish there were some more detailed ones of Ross Island given how many pages are spent describing the ship unloading and them building the hut.
