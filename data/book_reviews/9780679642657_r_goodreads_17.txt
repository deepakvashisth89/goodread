Before startingSuttree, I’d heard it was a retelling ofThe Odysseyand even if I hadn’t, I would have sensed it, especially in one particular section. ButSuttreeis even more episodic than the original and doesn’t have one overarching story of the protagonist trying to get back home. Instead, Suttree makes his way back to his houseboat several times after leaving it for various reasons, as if he is Sisyphus—doomed to push the same boulder up the same hill.Most of his reasons for leaving his boat are understandable—death of a relative; the promise of work—but one sojourn to the mountains of Gatlinburg in the depths of winter seems to have no reason and that’s when I wondered if perhaps Suttree is schizophrenic and/or that he takes off when his thoughts become too much for him. That latter impression was reinforced at another juncture when he contemplates the stars and is grateful for a respite when they are clouded.The story is set in the early-1950s Knoxville area among outcasts living on the outskirts. Brutal vernacular is of the time and police brutality is an ever-present danger. The depiction of a young woman Suttree has a sexual relationship with for a time bothered me for certain reasons, though I understand its purpose. The character of Harrogate, a clueless young man Suttree met in prison, and his over-the-top mishaps are comic genius.McCarthy’s language is the star of the show. Without it, I wouldn’t have cared for reading about drunks, fights, and debaucheries. At times the grotesque and even gross imagery turns poetic and beautiful; many times it fittingly remains gross. The book’s final death has me thinking more than its plot-point probably warrants and I have my own interpretation of the ending, though it contradicts the events of the final pages, which are beautifully written, especially the last two paragraphs.I’d previously read only McCarthy’sThe Road. I’ve heard that once you read eitherSuttreeand/orBlood Meridian, or, the Evening Redness in the West, you’ll fall into either the Camp-of-Love or the Camp-of-Not-for-Me. And if you fell into the former, you’d want to read more of him. Once again, I feel contradictory (I don’t believe in absolutes), because I might read more of him, but I could also see myself thinking this is enough.
