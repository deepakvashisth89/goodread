This is a book in the Modern Library Chronicles series, where gifted scholars, usually with long careers under their belts, are tasked with presenting sweeping overviews of a large subject for general readers. And, incidentally, they also appear to be expected to condense one of the big argumentative contributions that the scholar has contributed to the field. Sometimes the conflict between presenting a famous thesis and an overview of a historical subject is not a problem, but it is in this book. Hans Kung has made his name as a sharp critic of the historical claims of the papacy and the politics of the modern papal curia. This book is mostly a history of how a more egalitarian church of the Gospel lost its way through an ever-hardening church hierarchy with a Pope on the top, a hierarchy and particularly an ascendant papacy that was the creation of accident, politics, and outright fraud. Kung hopes that someday the Church may be restored to its original Gospel egalitarnianism.

Kung, an ordained Catholic priest, ably presents his evidence and his case, although much of his perspective and the evidence he draws owes a deep debt (bordering on indistinguishability) to what Orthodox and Reform theologians have argued about the papacy for at least six centuries now. Someone who knows little about the history of the papacy will no doubt learn much about its evolution and development. But as the book is always on the attack, it is hard to quite trust that one is learning the full story of the papacy itself, and there is no question of this book being any kind of comprehensive treatment of broader Catholicism, as just about everything that does not touch on the merits of church hierarchy falls to the wayside.

I left this book thinking more about Kung himself and his positions than about the Catholic Church. By all accounts, he identifies the wayward drift of the Church into un-Christian hierarchy as happening very early, perhaps even by the end of the first century CE, and that it fell victim to the forms of Roman imperialism a few centuries later and that the papacy was aggrandized by various political maneuvers, forgeries and power plays in the Early Middle Ages. If a church has, in one's view, deviated from the Gospel nearly since the time of the Gospel, and its highest institutions today are mostly the product of shams and worldly ambition, why does Kung remain attached to it? There have been innumerable Restorationist and Primitivist back-to-the-Gospel movements among Protestants and the Orthodox Church of the East has as worthy a claim to succession from Christ and the Apostles as the Roman West (and under Kung's presentation, perhaps quite a better claim). What is Kung trying to save?
