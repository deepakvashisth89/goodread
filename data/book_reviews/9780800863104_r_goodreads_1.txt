A SURVEY OF SIX INFLUENTIAL PSYCHICAL RESEARCHERS OF 19TH-20TH CENTURYPaul Tabori (1908-1974) was a Hungarian-Jewish author, novelist, journalist and psychical researcher. He was the vice-president of The Ghost Club for a number of years until his death, and was also the literary executor for the estate of British psychic researcher Harry Price.He wrote in the Introduction to this 1972 book, “there have been … [many] who suffered considerable harm and loss because they engaged in research … which were … considered disreputable and beneath the dignity of an academic or a public servant… And yet the truly dedicated psychical researcher must ignore all these considerations. Unless he remains unswayed by the views and criticisms of others, unless he has the courage of his own convictions (however unpopular these may be) he will betray his vocation. He must be above financial interest. And truth must be his paramount concern---whether it means the exposure of a dishonest, fraudulent medium or the open admission that he HAS witnessed dome occurrence which has no ‘normal’ explanation and which, by the existing scientific standards, is beyond human reason… To err on the side of credulity can be just as wrong as to be hyper-critical.” (Pg. 9)He continues, “While objective students of occultism will agree that there is a good deal of untainted, respectable EVIDENCE in favor of survival, this is not the same thing as PROOF. The difference is not only semantic but essential… Clear-cut cases are rare; one has only to refer again to the innumerable cases of mixed mediumship when obvious fraud is often inextricably interwoven with apparently genuine phenomena… Thus great caution is needed not to swallow everything offered by the champions of spiritualism… Spiritualism is a workable, justifiable hypothesis—where the difficulty begins is in its acceptance as more than a theory. And here the psychical researcher needs more than ever a sense of proportion and a balanced judgment.” (Pg. 14)He explains, “To illustrate this struggle, its heartbreaks and triumphs, its setbacks and achievements, I have chosen six of these pioneers of six different countries… Hereward Carrington… Sir Oliver Lodge… Baron von Schrenck-Notzing… Charles Richet… Cesare Lombroso… Raphael Schermann… These six studies are not intended to be full biographies of their subjects; they only present the highlights, the most striking features in their careers in psychical research. But I hope they will serve to attract the reader to a fuller exploration both of the whole subject and of the pioneers.” (Pg. 23)In the chapter on Hereward Carrington, he reports, “There were to mediums with whom Carrington was specially associated and whose work, in some way or other, influenced his life and career. One was the Italian Eusapia Paladino and the other the American woman known simply as Margery.” (Pg. 26-27) He acknowledges, “He also faced quite squarely the vexing and discouraging problem of [Paladino’s] mixed mediumship. Carrington made no secret of it that Eusapia and made repeated attempts to fake phenomena and, in some cases, succeeding in doing so---though without fooling the trio of Anglo-American investigators. Yet at the same time there were many occasions when inexplicable things happened under perfect and complete control… why DID Eusapia cheat? Carrington had a simple explanation: ‘I believe she does this sometimes simply and solely because of her love of mischief…’” (Pg. 30-31)When Margery was tested by the Scientific American Committee, “The … report was adverse by a majority of four to one. Even Carrington who voted for Margery, had his reservations by then. ‘Many of the observed manifestations might well have been produced fraudulently---and possibly were produced.’ He added, however, ‘But I am convinced that genuine phenomena have occurred here…’(Pg. 41)He explains, “Sir Oliver Lodge always represented an uncomfortable and difficult problem. Here was a great scientist, a brilliant expositor of scientific theory… and at the same time a spiritualist, a firm believer in life after death, a deeply religious man who saw no difficulty in reconciling his personal faith with the laws of the physical world he did so much to elucidate.” (Pg. 61)In 1894 he attended four sittings with Eusapia Paladino: “The sittings were ‘very memorable’ as Lodge put it and his first experience pf physical phenomena. Like practically everybody who sat with Eusapia, he was quite conscious of the ‘mixed’ character of her mediumship---though he was charitable enough to believe that she only resorted to trickery when her power was failing or was very weak. And she always had the alibi of warning her controllers to hold on tightly to her hands---otherwise some power she could not control would make her cheat.” (Pg. 70)He continues, “To him, the evidence for such survival did not depend argument but on evidence. He argued that there were a growing amount of such evidence---and that it had to be critically examined and if it stood the test, it had to be admitted… He went on to point out that the frivolous or trivial aspect of some of the evidence did not mitigate against its genuineness.” (Pg. 91-92)He says of Charles Richet, “Richet’s work differed from that of many other psychical researchers in his absolute determination to accept no explanation of any of the phenomena whose existence he admitted but an extension of human faculties. In other words, he was not a spiritualist, he did not believe in the supernatural or survival after death... Richet found it easier (or at least more scientific) to believe that the power to see or hear what was happening hundreds of miles away was due to some special sensibility rather than to say ‘transmitted vibrations of human thought.’” (Pg. 104-105) He summarizes, “To psychical research he gave a prestige and solidity which it needed badly. Many of his colleagues when assailed for ‘chasing spooks’ summoned his example to support and explanation… He made a decisive contribution in freeing hypnotic and occult phenomena from the limitations of primitive superstition helping them to become acceptable and accessible to serious scientific study.” (Pg. 131-132)Of Baron von Schrenck-Notzing, he says, “He was one of the sitters of Paladino, and colleague and fairly frequent collaborator of Lodge and even closer friend, translator and supporter of Richet. He also knew Lombroso well… he married a rich lady… and this enabled him to devote most of his time to psychical research… neither of [the others] had the means and facilities which the Baron possessed… Schrenck-Notzing spent almost forty years in psychical research, investigated countless mediums, published a large number of books, edited a series of reviews and reports and travelled widely in search of psychic phenomena.” (Pg. 140-141)He records, “Other who participated regularly in the seances found it somewhat surprising how tensely and impatiently Schrenck-Notzing awaited the phenomena, with what a deep excitement he followed their course… Others found the phenomena rather boring if they attended many seances and found that they were always the same.” (Pg. 167) Later, he adds, “He may have occupier himself with a comparatively narrow range of phenomena… yet by his patient, tenacious work he established a general pattern for research … Even his most determined adversaries were unable to claim that ALL these results were fraudulent or inconclusive.” (Pg. 172)Turning to Cesare Lombroso, he states, “Lombroso, like his colleagues, had no illusions about Signora Paladino’s inclination to cheat---the mixed nature of her mediumship. Yet there was, for him at least, ‘too much material, too many authentic instances, to worry about the possible trickery. However doubtful each separate case may appear, in the ENSEMBLE they form such a compact web of prof as wholly to baffle the scalpel of doubt.” (Pg. 183)Later, he adds, “Lombroso rejected the purely mechanistic explanation because he thought that while, in the case of telepathy and thought-transference, it could perhaps account for the transmissions over short distance, it would not provide the answer to the riddle of telepathic communications from one hemisphere to the other.” (Pg. 192) He continues, “Lombroso did not believe in pure, disembodied spirits… but he believed that these phantasms or entities had a physical substance so subtle and refined as to be both invisible and imponderable, except under special circumstances.” (Pg. 198)Of Raphael Schermann (who was killed during the Nazi occupation of Poland), he laments, “The man who had foretold the future of countless people, who had advised thousands as to their plans and the dangers to avoid was, by an ironic, cruel twist of fate, unable to foresee his own destiny. His death was a stupid waste of a great talent and the tragic end of an important pioneer in the exploration of the unseen.” (Pg. 243)This book will be of great interest to those studying the history of psychic research and mediums.
