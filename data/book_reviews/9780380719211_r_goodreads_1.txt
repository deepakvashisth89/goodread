Wow, this one was surprisingly good — a solid 4 stars, and then extra points for exceeding my initial low expectations, (yetanotherspy story set in Berlin? That city has been totally played out by everyone from le Carré and Deighton to Tom Hanks and Charlize Theron). But turns out this is no gloomy post-Cold War East/West tale; instead it's a post-Lockerbie terrorist plot involving a rejuvenated Red Army Faction (remember them?) that turns out to be eerily prescient of 9/11, some nine years later.The book is a nice return to the "simpler" spy world of the early '90s, before the internet and so a time when spooks could still operate in alias and invent cover stories on the fly without fear of being instantly found out through a basic online due diligence search; when they could just find a phone somewhere and securely (?) report back to HQS; when the main counterproliferation threats were still chemical, biological and nuclear weapons, rather than drones, AI and space weapons. (I also enjoyed the dated shout outs to dodos like Pan Am and TWA, as well as Quiller's claim [in his new if temporary cover as an arms dealer] that a then-still-up-and-coming China was probably eighth on the list of world suppliers; believe they're now number four.)But as a former government flunky myself, the most rivetting chapter in the whole book wasShowdown, in which Quiller gets on an open landline phone (!) and goes over the head of his "Director in the Field," arguing his case higher and higher up the chain of command until someone actually has to wake up "Bureau One" in Washington, DC…it would be like me arguing with my embassy section chief and then walking in to yell at the ambassador, yada yada until ultimately waking up the Secretary of State (then John Kerry — remember him?) because I didn't like what I was being asked to do — makes me break out in a bureaucratic sweat just to think of it!These books always end suddenly, with a fair number of strings hanging — which I rather like. Did the big bad actually get caught or killed? And what happened to George and Helen? Well, who cares? What matters is that the mission was accomplished, and the executive survived to come back again, (if only two more times; see below).I'm reading theQuillerbooks wildly out of sequence as I can find them, but it does seem like the whole series just got better as it went along. Sadly, only two more came out after this one:Quiller Salamander(the best I've read so far), andQuiller Balalaika(written on Hall's deathbed, and surprisingly hard to find…well, nothardper se, but just priced out of my normal range for such books). Good — and surprisingly under-appreciated — stuff.(Oh yeah; also ignore the fact that the first Quiller book [1965] references his service In WWII; so even giving him the benefit of the doubt that he was just 18 or 19 in 1945, that would make any normal human at least 65 at the time of this story — no matter; spy years are apparently the opposite of dogs years…)
