I’ve been a huge Jimmy Buffett fan since I was a teenager in the early 90s—Squarespace says I’m in the top .04% of listeners. I never got to see him in person, though, so instead I’m now reading through all seven of his books in order, and this is his first one. It was published in 1988, three years after the song "Jolly Mon Sing," co-written with Will Jennings and Michael Utley, which came out on the album Last Mango in Paris. He co-wrote the book with his daughter Savannah, who was around eight when they were writing, or nine when it came out (I was eleven), and while I don't know what that process was like, from all my work writing for children's media—besides being the stay-at-home parent when my children were young—it makes me think of this as a wonderful father-daughter bonding experience, an exploration in the creation of media, as much as a book for the rest of us. They did another one as well, , which would come out in 1991.

We need to say up front that the song is better. It's terse and therefore leaves more to the imagination, and just makes it feel more magical. I like that I always thought of the song as a legend from long ago while the illustrations here place it in the present; that was a nice change. But there's a very different feel in the illustrations by Lambert Davis, which seem rather static, removed, and impersonal, composed like Neo-Classicism and in long shots, from the gliding music of the song. I'm writing this about a month after the passing of Robert Greenidge, Buffett's steel drum player, and his glistening arpeggios add a layer of island magic that you can't catch in text or even illustration.

So we have to treat the book as a completely different iteration of the core idea; the characters are more developed, the story more detailed, and the ending different. But standing on its own this is a great little story book—there's too much text, at too advanced a reading level, for it to qualify as a true picture book for emerging readers. It's more of a book for young readers around Savannah's age then—8-10-year-olds—disguised as a picture book. It's a fun new way to experience the story and engage with the song, which is the best way for parents and children to interact together with media—through play, different iterations, discussion, and repeated consumption.

In fact, I know the chords to every song under the sun are online today, but back in 1988 they weren't, and I appreciate that the music is included on the last page of the book. It originally came with a CD, which would've been really cutting edge, but the used copy I bought from abebooks in 2026 obviously didn't. But I still broke out my ukulele and was able to play through it easily; like "He Went to Paris" and some other Buffett songs, there are only three chords, and the melody's there if you're on piano.
