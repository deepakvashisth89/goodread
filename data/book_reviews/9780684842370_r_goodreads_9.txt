The rich are different from us. It’s not simply that they have more money. It’s what that money does with regard to the fundamental imperatives of human life. Having wealth means not worrying about the basics: food, shelter, clothing, and medical care. It also allows you to avoid hard or dangerous labor. This all adds up to something just as valuable as cash: free time.

For the characters of Edith Wharton’s , this freedom provides the opportunity to create rigid hierarchies, an established seasonal pattern, and elaborate social rituals that tells a person their worth within the elite.

has been hailed by a masterpiece, and won the Pulitzer Prize upon its publication in 1920. That said, it has an extremely familiar feeling to it: a love triangle set within the confines of a comedy of manners.

The setting in New York City in the 1870s. The tip of the triangle is Newland Archer, a wealthy young man who has done precisely nothing to earn his wealth, and who is engaged to the beautiful-yet-conventional May Welland. The careful choreography of Newland’s life is upended when May’s cousin, the Countess Ellen Olenska, returns to New York from Europe, having left her husband back on the continent.

The arrival of Ellen upends elite New York society, and there is much consternation about how the arrival of the scandalous countess should be treated. It is not a spoiler to say that Newland falls in love with Ellen, and is therefore forced to choose between his heart and his mind.

***

Wharton tells from the limited third-person perspective, choosing Newland as her point-of-view. Everything in the novel runs through him, and it is to his thoughts alone that we are privy.

The dramatic hinge of the narrative is the tension between Newland, the dutiful May, and the rebellious Ellen. Your mileage may vary with regard to the heat engendered by Newland and Ellen’s forbidden attraction. To me, Newland’s passion felt more of a plot-dictated necessity than the unstoppable force of desire it is meant to be. Perhaps that is because we meet Newland at the same time that his life is about to change, thereby denying us any idea of his personality before finding himself at center stage in a work of classic fiction.

In any event, this does not really matter, as the chief selling-point of is its deconstruction of the beneficiaries of the Gilded Age. The real conflict here is between “old money” and “new money.” It is about having the bank account, the name to go with it; about who is allowed to hold parties; about who can be invited; and about how long you must wait before trying on the fashions from Paris.

is also about Wharton’s lovingly detailed descriptions of various homes, with each of her observations meant to convey something about the owner’s ultimate place within the social order.

***

The chief advantage of using Newland Archer as the gateway into this cosseted environment is that he accepts many of its peculiarities. As a result, instead of judging these people and their rituals, they are merely described. This turns out to be far more damning than self-righteous condemnation.

With just the hint of irony and some marvelous dialogue, Wharton reveals the cast as pampered nitwits at best, and low-key assholes at worst. The overriding trait of nearly everyone in is obliviousness to the realities of life outside their bubble. Most of them do not work, and those that do – such as Newland – hold a position mainly as a hobby. Their days are spent on the gossip circuit, engaging in petty backstabbing while taking tea.

The only redeeming personality is that of Catherine Mingott, May’s grandmother, who has reached the age where she is allowed to do and say as she pleases.

For most of , I enjoyed the ride without making any lasting connection to the material. However, the whole is uplifted by the ending. Without giving away too much, there is a time-jump that allows Wharton the opportunity to show the protagonists in a somewhat different light. It also provides a final scene that is melancholic, poignant, and earned.

Wealth has always been an exclusive club. The manifestation of wealth, though, changes with the time. In our own dark timeline, the merely rich spend their days conspicuously consuming with their planes, cars, yachts, and multiple homes. Meanwhile, the ultra-rich are searching for immortality, buying up islands to survive the end of the world, and are destroying the foundations of democracy.

It is therefore a sad statement that the indolent men and women of can actually come across as charming. At the very least, they believed that their wealth came with some semblance of decorum.
