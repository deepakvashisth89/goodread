After the King: Stories In Honor of J.R.R. Tolkiensounded so good!It was published in 1991, to celebrate the centenary of J.R.R. Tolkien’s birth (on 3rd January 1892) and contains nineteen fantasy and science fiction short stories. Mine was a borrowed DAISY disc, with no details, so I went on to the publisher’s website to find out the titles. Tor Books, I discovered, is an American publisher based in New York, (which explains the spelling of “honor”).The list of stories looked promising. After an introduction by Jane Yolen on the role of Tolkien for the genre of fantasy, they were:“Reave the Just” (Stephen R. Donaldson)“Troll Bridge” (Terry Pratchett)“Long Night’s Vigil at the Temple” (Robert Silverberg)“The Dragon of Tollin” (Elizabeth Ann Scarborough)“Faith” (Poul and Karen Anderson)“In the Season of the Dressing of the Wells” (John Brunner)“The Fellowship of the Dragon” (Patricia A. McKillip)“Decoy Duck” (Harry Turtledove)“Nine Threads of Gold” (Andre Norton)“Conjure Man” (Charles de Lint; story starts with a quote from Tolkien)“Silver or Gold” (Emma Bull)“Up the Side of the Air” (Karen Haber)“The Naga” (Peter S. Beagle)“Revenge of the Sugar Plum Fairies” (Mike Resnick)“Winter’s King” (Jane Yolen)“Gotterdammerung” (Barry N. Malzberg)“Down the River Road” (Gregory Benford)“Death and the Lady” (Judith Tarr)“Halfling House”(Dennis McKiernan)About half these names were familiar, so I began to look forward to it.After the King: Stories In Honor of J.R.R. Tolkienwas a Festschrift anthology. I had to look that up … it means:“a celebratory academic book honoring a respected scholar upon retirement or a milestone birthday, typically comprising essays written by former students and close colleagues. The best publishers for festschrifts depend on the specific field, but major academic presses routinely handle these collections.”The publishers seemed to have stretched that definition a little, but never mind. The blurb assured me that it was“anoutstandingcollection of new fantasy stories by anextraordinaryassemblage of some of thevery bestwriters to ever continue the tradition Tolkien began with The Lord of the Rings.”Phew! How could it fail? Especially if these authors were invited to contribute stories in honour of the acknowledged modern master of Fantasy:“This book is a celebration and a tribute to his lasting influence; a collection of magical and powerful tales from leading fantasy writers.”So we began. Chris thought they could not be especially written for the anthology, since he had already read the second one (by Terry Pratchett) some time ago. Perhaps then, they were the authors’ favourites? Or those which they thought owed the most to Tolkien’s influence? Perhaps a couple might be set in his world of Middle-earth - either literally - or in some other clever way?The first story, “Reave the Just” (Stephen R. Donaldson) we agreed was boring and ponderous, with no noticeable “Tolkien effect”. Then came “Troll Bridge” (Terry Pratchett). It featured the ancient, decrepit and toothless Cohen the Barbarian (from his Discworld series) and a mangy talking horse who has been sold to him by a wizard. It sounds amusing … but again was a bit of a damp squib.“I’d forgotten it really - perhaps it was an early one”, suggested Chris.We had over 21 hours of this to come! So I decided to look at a few reviews online, to see if there was a general consensus, or stated aim of the collection.One reviewer put what we had been saying to each other succinctly:“What the book misses is some explanations from the authors as to why they included or wrote a particular short story as a celebration of Tolkien, how did Tolkien’s life or work influence them?”And another voiced our unspoken fears:“It feels like the editor was so afraid a short story collection wouldn’t sell, that he put the name of Tolkien on the cover. While some of the stories play with Tolkienian themes, most of them only have the genre of fantasy in common.”Oh dear; our worst fears were being confirmed. The reason the extravagant blurb had to be hyped up so much, was because most of these stories were, as another reviewer said:“plodding and tedious and pointless”.Or they:“were hard to get through - in no way meant they were in the same vein as Tolkien. I struggled to finish, plus a few that I felt were tasteless.”So many were singing the same tune, that I looked for favourable ones. One was simply descriptive:“a collection of mostly fantasy to me. A couple are more sci fi-like and some are more magical realism-like.”And one brave soul thought the authors could:“spin out stories that are poetic and spellbinding, funny and haunting, evocative and magical. Their stories honor Tolkien by making brilliant use of language and fantastical worlds.”Let’s stay with that idea, then: nineteen great short stories of fantasy and science fiction. But I’m afraid we didn’t get further than the first two.I have not quoted from Goodreads reviews, but if some of these short quotations were your words, I am happy to credit you.The narrators for this DAISY disc were Jonathan Oliver and Lorelei King, both of whom were professional, and made of the material what they could. The playing time was 21 hours and 3 minutes. The cover Art for the printed book looks familiar … perhaps it is by a regular illustrator of Fantasy.Maybe we were both in the wrong mood for several days, but I’m afraid I don’t recommend this collection.
