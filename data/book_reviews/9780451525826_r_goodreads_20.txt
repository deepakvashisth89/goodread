It Can’t Happen Here is a dystopian and political novel written during the Great Depression where Franklin Delano Roosevelt promised A New Deal in his inaugural address in 1933 but also conceded that America was in grim times given the stormy economic and social climate. Sinclair Lewis was able to tap into the nervous undercurrent that accompanied the volatile politics of this period. As is stressed in the Introduction by Michael Meyer: “With the rise of Adolf Hitler and Benito Mussolini in Europe and the alarming popularity of a variety of demagogues from both the left and the right in the United States, there was widespread concern that the country could be taken over by a fascist dictatorship.” Sinclair Lewis placed these concerns at the center of the satirical and somewhat prescient novel, predicting the rise of Donald J Trump to the American presidency. His intention was to shake up the American electorate so complacent in their beliefs that it would be impossible for a police state to rise in the midst of democracy. Perhaps also adding to Sinclair Lewis’s insight into the volatile time was his marriage to Dorothy Thompson, a foreign correspondent who had interviewed Adolf Hitler and had written a series of articles between 1931 and 1935 warning Americans about the Nazi propaganda machine that masked the persecution of Jews and the growing presence of concentration camps designed to annihilate them. These articles ultimately led to her expulsion from Germany.

The times were frightening in 1935 when this book was published where it is imagined that Roosevelt is defeated by the improbable election of an authoritarian Berzillius (Buzz) Windrip, modeled after Huey Long, the Louisiana demagogue who was assassinated a month before was published. There is a theme of journalism being the bulwark against tyranny throughout the book as our hero is an editor of a liberal Vermont newspaper, Doremus Jessup. At various times in the novel, the author uses Jessup to denounce and satirize the DAR, the KKK, Aimee McPherson, Mary Baker Eddy, Billy Sunday. Father Coughlin, William Jennings Bryan, Huey Long, Tammany graft, Chicago gangsters, Prohibition, lynchings, anti-Semitism and racism. Journalism throughout this cautionary tale is what inspires hope for political change. The similarities between this fictional tale and the reality of what is facing the United States of America is frightening. This is a turning point in America today and how we react is paramount.
