As a young man in the 1940s, Eric Newby served in the British army, was captured by the Germans off Sicily, spent some time in a prisoner-of-war camp in Italy, escaped and was fed, hidden and helped by Italian farmers, an experience he described in charming detail in his most famous book, Love and War in the Apennines. The experience gave him such a deep and abiding love for Italy that more than two decades later, when he’d become a well-established travel writer, he bought a ramshackle house in the foothills of the Apennines, close to the famous Cinque Terre. In the 24 years that followed, he and his wife spent a considerable amount of time there, getting to know intimately both the area and the (farmer) lifestyle. is his memoir about that time. At turns funny, sad and informative, it’s a love letter to Italy, as well as an elegy on a lifestyle that no longer exists.

gets off to a fairly slow start, with a hunt for a house in the Italian countryside and an introduction to the house Newby and his wife end up buying, which probably goes on a little too long. However, once Newby starts introducing his neighbours and their lives, the story becomes very enjoyable. His neighbours, you see, are real, old-fashioned , who have outdoor toilets, milk their cows, wash their clothes in a basin in the backyard (fed by water from a stream), make their own agricultural tools, attend religious festivals and wakes, harvest grapes together, forage for fungi together and basically lead tough but delightfully communal lives.

A large chunk of the book is devoted to the , the annual grape harvest and start of the wine-making process, in which all the local families get together to get the work done, supported by copious amounts of food and wine. The work may be back-breaking, but the togetherness that pops off the page is beautiful and very, very Italian.

Needless to say, life in rural Italy in the 1960s and 1970s isn’t an idyll. There are anecdotes in the book about leaky roofs, knocked-over chamber pots, mice, adders and a cockroach infestation of biblical proportions. Unfriendly neighbours and Italian bureaucracy also make an appearance, and needless to say, people die. That’s what happens to people when you follow them over a 24-year period. That’s not what you’ll remember, though. What you’ll remember is the beauty of it all – the sense of community, of being connected to a larger whole, something that seems to be sorely lacking these days.

Newby and his wife sold their home in Italy in 1991, by which time their part of Italy was overrun with tourists, modernity had well and truly arrived in the countryside, and many of the traditions he describes had died. The final pages of the book read like an elegy on a lifestyle that no longer exists. They are sad. Thankfully, though, the rest of the book is not. There are some very funny moments in there, and some dialogue and observations that made me chuckle, such as, ‘If Attilio went to bed with his umbrella up, it was not difficult to imagine Signor Armando in bed with his hat on’. Newby has a great ear for dialogue, as well as a very English sense of humour. Throw in the magic of rural Italy in the 1960s and 1970s and you have a winning recipe.

isn’t Newby’s best book, but if you love Italy and/or good travel writing, this is well worth your time.
