English Passengersis historical fiction that attempts to balance maritime farce with the devastating brutality of colonial genocide in Tasmania. In 1857, Captain Illiam Quillian Kewley and his crew of Manx smugglers on the Sincerity have their contraband seized by British Customs. To stay in business, they take on three English passengers bound for Tasmania on a bizarre expedition.The leader, Reverend Geoffrey Wilson, is a religious fanatic convinced the literal Garden of Eden is hidden in the Tasmanian wilderness. Dr. Thomas Potter is a eugenicist secretly developing a pseudo-scientific thesis on the "races of men." The third, Timothy Renshaw, is a young botanist sent off by his father to make something of himself, and he wants little to do with either man's obsession.The voyage alternates with the story of the Aboriginal Tasmanians facing annihilation by British colonists and soldiers. This history comes through Peevay, an indigenous man who witnesses the violence against his people as they fight for survival and who eventually crosses paths with the arriving ship. The book is told through more than twenty distinct points of view, delivered in letters, diaries, and official reports.Kneale varies the dialects well, and I had no trouble telling the characters apart, though I would have preferred fewer of them. The first-person narratives show characters who consider themselves righteous and justified while the reader sees their ignorance plainly. I particularly enjoyed the comic relief from Captain Kewley and his desperate attempts to hide his contraband from his passengers.The novel is long and starts slowly, partly because of the time it takes to introduce such a large cast. The first hundred pages felt like a chore. The shifting perspectives drag on the book’s momentum, and I had to readjust constantly. The account of the genocide is graphic and disturbing in ways that grate against the seafaring farce. Between the slow build, the constant shifting, and that tonal clash, the book never quite came together for me into a cohesive whole.
