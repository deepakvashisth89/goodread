I began reading this book on February 16, 2023, the day that its author, Tim McCarver, died at the age of 81. McCarver had a 21-year Major League baseball career as a catcher with the St. Louis Cardinals, Philadelphia Phillies, Montreal Expos, and Boston Red Sox. With the Cardinals, he was a two-time World Series champion and an All-Star. After his playing career ended, McCarver became an award-winning baseball broadcaster. My familiarity with him as a broadcaster stems from his 16 years in the broadcast booth of the New York Mets.I remember McCarver the broadcaster as being very knowledgeable, opinionated, voluble (sometimes excessively voluble), and often funny. His book reflects all of these characteristics. The book includes many entertaining and insightful anecdotes from his playing days. He provides an “inside look” at some of the best players in the game, notably his Hall of Fame battery-mates Bob Gibson and Steve Carlton. He’s not afraid to offer criticism of players, managers, and specific plays if he thinks it’s warranted.McCarver takes justified pride in his career as a catcher. “Catching is the most demanding, underrated job in sports. Don’t tell me about the beleaguered quarterback or the besieged goalie. No athlete has it any tougher than a catcher.” Although he definitely comes across as self-confident, he is also candid in his self-assessment, acknowledging, for example, that he succumbed to pressure in the 1967 World Series.Some of McCarver’s dislikes (which I happen to share with him) include the designated hitter (that battle’s been lost), the singing of the National Anthem before a game (he’s not against honoring the country, he just hates “The Star-Spangled Banner”), and “baseball chapel” prayer meetings in the locker room. As for the latter, he says, “Too often, players will use God as a cop-out. Occasionally, around the league you’ll actually hear wins or losses attributed to ‘God’s will.’ Jimmy Frey, when he was a coach with the Mets, once set Pete Falcone, a devoted member of baseball chapel, down with the question ‘Do you think God would mind if you threw your breaking ball over for a strike every so often?’”McCarver’s baseball analysis is peppered with colorful imagery. (I remember mostly enjoying this aspect of his broadcasts, but sometimes his penchant for puns got to be too much.) For example, in describing the pressure of a pennant race, he says it’s “a time when even water goes down in lumps.” He contends that the playoffs are more pressure-packed than the World Series: “It’s the quagmire before the promised land. It’s the Red Sea that has to be crossed. If you don’t cross it into the World Series, you’re a loser. You’re forgotten by Thanksgiving.”Sometimes I have to wonder if McCarver doesn’t let the facts get in the way of a good story. He mentions how pleased he was when a stadium in Memphis, Tennessee, his hometown, was named “McCarver Memorial Stadium” in his honor. “Tug McGraw once asked me why they called it a memorial stadium, since I am still alive. I told him the name referred to my arm, which had died ten years before.” Good story, but according to some quick research that I did, the actual name of the stadium was “Tim McCarver Stadium.”One of the most interesting parts of the book for me as a long-time Mets fan is the section in which he records his personal day-to-day observations of the Mets’ 1986 season. He didn’t know beforehand that the Mets would produce the best season in their history that year and end up as World Series champions. As a fan who lived through it and remembers some of the highlights, I really enjoyed getting McCarver’s take on that magical season. Non-Mets fans no doubt would not appreciate that part of the book as much as I did, but I do think that most baseball fans would enjoy much of McCarver’s wit and wisdom.
