What. The cocaine-fuelled fuck. Was that.

I've read a lot of Stephen King. Like, a LOT. Most things he's ever written, with a few exceptions that I'm getting to on my own Great Stephen King Reread. I've rarely actively disliked one of his books before, and never to the extent of The Talisman. The only other one I gave a 1-star rating was The Outsider and that was only after it turned into a Scooby-Doo episode. So here we are, with The Talisman, or as I call it, The MacGuffin.

It's not only that the titular talisman is the ultimate MacGuffin. Everyone wants it, it does whatever the hell you want it to, heals people, teaches boys what vaginas look like, blows up everyone who's ever been mean to you, unlocks the multiverse, whatever. It's that multiple other MacGuffins propel the plot too. The MC needs a token to prove his trustworthiness? Give him a fancy guitar pick, oh look it's a shark tooth in the other world, both are kind of triangular I guess. Use your special coin to repel monsters, sure. Bad guy needs a thing to shoot out lightning and also one time makes a snowstorm? Why not have him just instinctively know that an antique toy's wind-up key is a special interdimensional tool, for no reason, he just feels it while randomly walking by one day. Replace each item in the book with "item A/B" and it changes nothing. It reminds me of a thing I hate in some middle grade fantasy stories, where the kid acquires a random item then uses it to solve a random problem later, in a way that makes it clear that the item was planted solely for that, by the author's hand. What's that, Percy, you turned into an animal? How about you solve that by chewing on some magic vitamins? Good thing that Greek god gave you magic vitamins all those pages ago for no specific reason, right? Anyway, King has frequently done the "character just senses the power of this totem" thing across decades, but here it felt really lousy and overdone. The story was simply too reliant on all of this to remain interesting.

Speaking of "interesting", what was this book not for multiple long stretches? This was such a slog to read. The page count varies by edition but it sure felt like 1000 pages while reading. It was very uneven; there were energetic, exciting parts, but so much slog too. It took so long, multiple library loans, and concentrated willpower to get through it. There was very little tension. Any time he needed, the main character could shift worlds to get out of danger. Even after he ran out of world-shifting juice, it was obvious that he would be then able to do it by his juiceless self. Even in the context of expected heroic outcomes, there was never any sense of doubt that the boy would find the talisman and save his mom and a whole other world. So that leaves you with just the journey to get there. Was it an interesting journey? It was not.

Exception: the long middle sequence in the book involving Wolf and the Sunrise Home, that boys' workcamp evangelistic prison. That whole section of the book had the electric King energy that I adore. That's where the cocaine energy was fuelled appropriately. In other places . . . I've never disliked King's writing per se, but as this book progressed I found it more and more unhinged. Poor word choices, weird cringy description, poor choices in general.

I suppose Straub is maybe also responsible, but I don't know how this collaboration worked. This bothers me about a lot of co-authored books; I want to understand who did what, who has final say in any area, how it could possible work out between two talented authors. I'd like to think that King wrote some parts and Straub wrote other parts once they had agreed on the storyline. There were sections that felt distinctly not-King to me, particularly early in the book, sections that were a bit more literary in style, or things that were simply not in King's voice. Maybe Straub wrote the part that came immediately after the Sunrise Home sequence, but after all that thrill, that energetic climax, the next part of the book was pbbbttthhhttt. A drag.

Most of that particular drag was because of the addition of Richard to the story. Richard was a drag. He was an an unbelieving drag, then a only partially conscious drag, then a physical drag. He added nothing to the story, he helped the quest for the Talisman not at all, he was in fact a huge hindrance. I suppose he was meant to offer some parallel; he and the MC (I really can't remember his name at this point. I'll call him Tad because that's all I can think of because that's the teen boy protagonist of the most recent book I finished) are the sons of the pair of humans who first launched this cross-world quest; one is dead at the story's outset, the other is the main villain. Is there any payoff to having the villain's son as a co-hero? There is not. Does Richard go through any struggle to accept that his dad is evil? *shrug*. Does his dad have any feelings about his son in this regard? *shrug.* Does Richard's presence make any difference in the book's outcome? Nope. So WHY THE FUCK DID I JUST READ 400 PAGES OF RICHARD BEING SUCH A FUCKING DRAG?  

Maybe if the villain presented any kind of real threat, it would have been a better book despite the clearly forecast outcomes. He does not. He's menacing, but he's never shown to be competent. His only achievement is to pay off hotel staff to ignore his ex-partner's wife. Otherwise, he fails at everything he attempts on-screen. He is continually foiled by a 12-year-old boy. He can't shoot lightning straight to save his own ass. He's surrounded by incompetent minions. Both he and his main minion, the leader of the Sunrise Home, are utterly histrionic. Histrionic! That's what replaces effective antagonists in this story.

It all went particularly downhill towards the end (you can tell you're there when you reach choice dialogue like, “Fushing feef, fushing FEEF!” and "Bwana make juju"), and this is where I see King's then-current cocaine habit most wreaking havoc. I measure this in this case by the density of penis talk. Penises came up so many times in those last, I dunno, 130 pages. It was like: Penis. Penis. Testicles. Hardon. Goat's penis. Vagina—oh look, I can see the Talisman's true form, it looks just like a vagina! I've never seen a vagina before but I just know that's what it looks like! I love vaginas now! I came from a vagina just like that one! I have to save my mom! That was the main character speaking, btw. A real coming of age moment.

And let's not even talk about the Magical Negro.

To sum up, was just the worst.

Penis.
