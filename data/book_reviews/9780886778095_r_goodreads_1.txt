I first read this book in high school and utterly adored it. I was somewhat concerned that a reread might reveal it to be lousy. What a relief to discover that yes, it really is good.

Jerlayne's greatest strength is its portrayal of the complex relationships between characters, whether they're family members or lovers. It's also got one of the most original takes on elves and Faerie around. Among other unusual features, many of the children elves give birth to do not stay elves, instead turning into gnomes, dwarves, sylphs, or even dragons. Jerlayne finds herself unable to have < elven children, and her attempts to remedy that lead her to secrets that undercut everything she believed about her world. I filed this under both "high fantasy" and "urban fantasy" because the book takes place mostly in the high fantasy realm of Faerie, but there are occasional excursions to and intrusions from the late 20th-century mortal realm.

The plot is engaging; I reread its 500+ pages of small type in eleven hours during which I couldn't bring myself to do anything else. There are several twists that took me by surprise the first time (and some small ones I forgot enough that they surprised and delighted me on reread). Generally it's a fairly serious novel with plenty of sadness and bitter secrets. However, there are also pleasant bits of humor scattered around. (e.g. when a modern mortal ends up inside Jerlayne's homestead in Faerie, readers discover that the elves' tendency to scavenge unwanted materials from the mortal realm means that the homestead looks less like Rivendell than like a thrift store with eyeball-peeling wallpaper clashes.) It isn't exactly high literature, and this edition could have used some TLC from a copy editor before printing, but it's still well worth reading.
