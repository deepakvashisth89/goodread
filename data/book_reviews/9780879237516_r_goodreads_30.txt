Je cherche en meme temps l’eternel et l’ephemere.When you buy a new washing machine or a new car you expect it to come with a user manual, a booklet detailing the features of the device and the way to access them. Wouldn’t it be nice if we had something so helpful for life as we live it?Georges Perec worked for almost a decade on this project, a masterpiece or an obsession depending on the observer. I fall into the first category, and I was not surprised to learn afterwards who else was a member ofOulipo, that eclectic clique of writers who set out to break apart and reassemble the art of the novel: Italo Calvino, one of my personal idols.Do we need to break life apart and reassemble it in order to understand it? Apparently yes, because this novel is deliberately constructed as a giant puzzle, made up of hundreds of smaller, individual puzzles in each chapter.You know what else needs to be cut apart and examined for our existential demonstration? The apartment building at 11, Rue Simon-Crubellier in Paris, France. It is presented to us as a sort of exploded diagram of parts in one of those technical manuals I mentioned for car or house appliances.A puzzle, an IKEA assembly diagram, a game of chess or Go played with human pieces, a crossword challenge – all of these are valid descriptors for the nuts and bolts Perec used in constructing his novel. I didn’t read about them in advance, and I am in a way glad I went in unprepared, because it would have taken away from my joy in trying to figure out for myself what his game is. The Oulipos believed imposing artificial restrictions and rules on their writing would help their creativity and would ‘displace’ the readers from their comfort zone, encouraging fresh thinking. You can read about all the ways Perec used lists, diagrams and visual aids in his creation, but I recommend doing this only after you finish the lecture.... the parts do not determine the pattern, but the pattern determines the parts: knowledge of the pattern and of its laws, of the set and the structure, could not possibly be derived from discrete knowledge of the elements that compose it.... the whole labour consisting precisely in performing the ‘displacement’ which gives the puzzle or the clue its ‘meaning’ and thereby renders any explanation tiresome and unnecessary.The actual key to the novel is offered in the Prologue, an essay of sorts on the art of creating puzzles. I believe this is enough information for embarking on the journey:The art of jigsaw puzzling begins with wooden puzzles cut by hand, whose maker undertakes to ask himself all the questions the player will have to solve, and, instead of allowing chance to cover his tracks, aims to replace it with cunning, trickery, and subterfuge.Any child or adult who has actually tried to assemble a puzzle knows you need to start with several known elements, with a road-map. In the novel I think this is achieved in two ways:The painter Serge Valene, the oldest person living in the apartment building at the date oftwenty-third of June nineteen seventy-five(the date is also important, but I’m not going to spoil its role), plans to put on canvas the exploded diagram of the building, removing the facade and exposing to light all the rooms and all the lives of its tenants.Bartlebooth, an eccentric millionaire also living in the building, is engaged in a lifetime project that can be interpreted as a mirror of the writer’s goal in writing the novel, a Perec self-reference that can be applied to several other characters in the book.Bartlebooth’s idea was to go and paint five hundred seascapes in five hundred different ports.A young Bartlebooth decides to dedicate his whole life to his project: first he takes painting lessons with Valene for 10 years, until he is satisfied with his technique. Then, accompanied by his gentleman’s gentleman Smautf, he travels the world for 20 years, painting his watercolours in exotic ports. Each month, he sends back the seascape to Gaspard Winkler, an artisan living in the same building, who transforms the paintings into 500 pieces of unique, hand-cut puzzles. At the end of his journeys, Bartlebooth returns to Rue Simon-Crubellier and becomes a sort of recluse whose only activity is to reassemble one of the 500 puzzles each month.Bartlebooth resolved one day that his whole life would be organised around a single project, an arbitrarily constrained programme with no purpose outside its own completion.Is he trying to demonstrate that the pattern of life can be unlocked by determination and focus? Or is this just a pointless exercise, a hobby designed to pass the time? Or that the secret to life is in the living of it? The answer must come from each reader in turn.so be wary of reading other reviews or online comments in order to avoid spoilers. Such as the fact that both Valene and Bartlebooth fail in their endeavours :the inexorable outcome was blindness.The next quote is somehow related to the three elements in the jigsaw art: the picture, the pattern and visual memory.
