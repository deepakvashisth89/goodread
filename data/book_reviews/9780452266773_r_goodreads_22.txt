one of the ultimate offerings in the category of "mother/daughter dynamics that manifest as (self-)harm." the prose here is often light-handed, but gorgeously rendered in spite of that. this is the kind of clever, unsparing bildungsroman that i wish more contemporary YA embodied. it doesn't flinch away from sex or violence (or their intersections) or class or race or colonialism and the ways these things invariably force girls to come of age long before their peers ever have to. the resentment and ire exhibited by lucy is both convincing and earned. it felt almost rejuvenating to follow a teenage girl with so many unsavory thoughts—thoughts she often gives voice to. for most of this, lucy drifts ambivalently through life and that ambivalence makes perfect narrative sense, given that the novel opens with her first and formal separation from her only family.

in other words, on the level of subjectivity and theming, this was excellent. in terms of craft, it left a bit to be desired. perhaps intentionally, there's a kind of incomplete, epistolary feel to that calls to mind images of a young girl transcribing herself and her ugliest feelings in a diary or journal of some kind.

still, while reading this i got the sense that kincaid's transitions were belated, load-bearing guardrails meant to easily and conveniently communicate change/transition/development in time and character. i do understand beginning a scene break with, "One day, [life-altering epiphany or event described]," is far more economical than giving readers a drawn out scene and allowing that epiphany or event to burgeon of its own volition (or appear that way). but this compressed, almost abrupt quality in the novel's transitions began to feel like a shortcut towards meaning. meaning through change, specifically, given how much of the novel is rendered in the interior.

we don't, for example, get meaty scene-writing in the way of a friendship between peggy and lucy. we don't watch them meet or converse much at all. we can't ourselves, as readers, track the changes and nuances in their (sometimes homoerotic) dynamic. there isn't a lot of subtext to sift. instead we get declarative transitions: "My friendship with Peggy was reaching a predictable stalemate; the small differences between us were beginning to loom..." and then, 15 pages later, "Because Peggy and I were now getting along, we naturally started to talk about finding an apartment in which we would live together."

the transition between these two fluctuations in peggy/lucy's friendship is brusque, the kind of narrative convenience you see suggested in more episodic writing ('this is happening because in order to create movement, and because we only have so much time and/or space to create that movement!').

in literature geared towards younger readers, i think this "let's beam meaning/change through quick, overt explanation!" makes a lot of sense. the choice lends itself well to super accessible narrative voices like the one in this novel. it is not, however, my preference, and it grated here how unsubtle the character- and relationship-writing became. there were also a lot of threads i wished got more attention and didn't drop off so suddenly, but again—economical, compressed coming-of-age story. i get it.

the prose in this was beautiful. it truly warranted the near-constant highlighting my secondhand copy received from the reader who came before me. i found the complexities of the mother/daughter relationship thoughtful and incredibly paced. i loved the concept, the set dressings, the themes. the overall reading experience was dampened some by the (faintly) rushed, incomplete quality i was chasing throughout the novel, though. all in all, a great book for young readers, but not enough for me. 3.4/5.
