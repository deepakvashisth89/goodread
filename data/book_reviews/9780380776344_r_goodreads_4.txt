First published in 1996, Eagle's dual time-strand novel might be considered historical fiction now: its main storyline is set in 1973, with a secondary storyline set in 1933. In 1973, Vietnam vet Zane Lone Bull is determined to keep out of Indian activism after serving jail time following a Lakota court-house sit in protest which turned violent. He just wants to keep out of trouble and be there for his extended family, even though he's not even sure he's actually related to them. His Unci, or grandmother, took him in as a child, but never talked about his parents, and neither does the sister with whom she now lives; his Uncle Martin is mentally impaired and mute; his cousin Chickie has dumped her two kids on her mother (again) with promises to return. And despite Zane's discouragement, Chickie's brother Randy seems to be taking up where Zane left off, getting involved with AIM, chasing both justice and a possible claim to land that both seem equally unlikely to pan out.

But when Randy
, Zane finds himself chasing down the clues that Randy first turned up, which leads him to Michelle, a white schoolteacher who now owns the house that once belonged to the doctor in charge of the Hiawatha Asylum for Insane Indians (sickeningly, a real institution)—and to the asylum's records. As Zane and Michelle begin to explore the past through the records, and explore their own growing attraction toward one another, their narrative is intercut with scenes between two other young lovers: nurse Rachel Trainor, who has come to South Dakota to serve at the Asylum, and Adam Lone Bull, a WWI vet whose odd younger brother, Martin, is a patient at Hiawatha. Eagle slowly and carefully leads readers through these two intertwining stories, only gradually revealing how the two pairs of lovers are related.

It was difficult to give this one a star rating. On the one hand, Eagle's writing is superb. Engrossing, nuanced, and evocative, with characters who really felt like grown-ups and double storylines that continually ratchet up the tension until you get mad each time the story switches from one to the other, because you can't believe you have to wait to find out what's happening with the couple you've just spent time with. Eagle doesn't deploy Native American stereotypes; she's clearly on the side of the Lakota here, intent on revealing the gross racism deployed against Native Americans by the U.S. government and by many, many white Americans, and the horrific toll exacted by that racism on the bodies and minds of Native American people, by making her audience FEEL their pain and humanity. Not a surprise that this won the 1997 "Most Hanky Read" in the All About Romance reader's poll.

Everyone always mentions that Kathleen Eagle, though white herself, married a Lakota man, and taught on a South Dakota reservation for many years before turning to writing. If I were reading this novel when it was originally written (1996, 20+ years after the events of the primary story take place), I most likely would have taken those justifications without question, and celebrated the book unequivocally, for the education it gives white readers about the atrocity of their ancestors' treatment of the native peoples of the country (and thus the incentive to not continue it).

But reading it in 2020, in the midst of protests against anti-Black racism, it's hard to avoid thinking about audience here. Whose pain is being used as not just education, but entertainment, and for whom? It was also uncomfortable to realize that at book's end, we have a half white-half Lakota man and a white woman as our heroes, while the fully Lakota people in the story are not granted nearly so happy an ending, despite the horrific traumas they suffer for the readers' edification and tears.
