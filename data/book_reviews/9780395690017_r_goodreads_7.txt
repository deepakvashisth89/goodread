On January 7, 1974, in Gombe National Park in Tanzania, a group of eight chimpanzees crossed the border of their range and came upon Godi, a chimpanzee eating peacefully in a neighboring territory. The group attacked Godi, tore him, punched him, bit him, and left him. He died shortly thereafter. A victim of random violence.The attack on Godi was the first such attack witnessed by humans. Up until that time, naturalists waxed poetic about the the "arcadian existence of primal innocence" enjoyed by chimpanzees, an idyllic state "which we (humans) once believed was the paradise that man had somehow lost."Could the violence inherent in all human society possibly be in our DNA? Could it be tied to something as simple as food? The need for our ancestors to leave the forest and search out foods in the plains with rich energy stores...such as roots. Why is the Bonobo a relatively peaceful animal, while its close relatives Homo Sapiens and Chimpanzees so violent? Could it be because the Bonobo is a matriarchal society where sexual relations between females is commonplace, as opposed to the male dominated Chimps and Humans? Could violence be genetic, and not evil, or even moral?Wrangham and Peterson explore many of these ideas in their search for the origin of human violence. Their conclusions are worth the effort. An incredible book.
