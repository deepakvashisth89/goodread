Half of this book is an exciting overview of one of the largest and most consequential industrial families in history. From 1811, when Friederich Krupp founded his Gusstahlfabrik or "cast steel factory", to 1968, when his legacy "einzelne Firma" or sole proprietorship became a joint-stock company, Fried. Krupp was one of the most powerful steelmakers in Europe, and one with a particular connection to both armaments and the German government. The stories of the family leaders in this book sparkle. From Alfred (1812 to 1882) the hypochondriac manure-lover (he thought the smell was healing) and reactionary who took a little firm and expanded it into a colossus that powered Bismark's reunification of Germany, to his son Fritz, a salesman and a diplomat, who killed himself in 1902 when his Italian romps with underaged boys became an international scandal, to Gustav von Bohlen and Halbach, who married Fritz's daughter Bertha, and, in a special ceremony led by the Kaiser, inherited the Krupp throne and even name, to Alfried, the rigid automaton who led the firm through the Nazi era and was imprisoned after Nuremberg, the author brings the characters of often grey men to light, and shows their profound influence on history.

The book likewise does a good job demonstrating the consequential products these men made. From the steel breech-loading cannon that won Sedan for the Germans in 1870, to the "Big Bertha ("dicke Bertha") cannons that overwhelmed Liege in 1914, to the Unterseeboots at the Grusonwerks that attacked British shipping, to the 88m antiaircraft guns and Panzer tanks of Hitler's Reich, the Krupp firm made them all. The fact that first Kaiser Wilhelm II and then Hitler both passed special laws and made special orders to ensure the integrity of the Krupp firm and the sole ownership of the Krupp family shows the intimate connection between state, family, and company in this period.

The problem, however, is that fully half of the book takes place in the years since 1940. Much of it reads as a dreary recitation of Nuremberg testimony that Alfred employed sklavenarbeiten (slave labor), ran concentration camps for Jews and other workers for his plants, and stole several Jewish plants and even a Rothschild firm in Paris as part of his empire-building. If anything, the tedious repetition of these stories takes away from the horror of Alfried's actions and the truth that one of the most prominent Germans in the post-World War II era was a mass-murderer, thief, and slave-trader. Although the book more than makes its case that Alfried's conviction at Nuremberg was well-deserved, and that his pardoning just three years later by American procurator John J. McCloy and Alfred's return to being the richest man in Europe, is itself a monstrous crime, much of that material belongs in briefs rather than in an otherwise well-written nonfiction book such as this.

Yet there is no other work that will give you a better window on the combined political and economic rise of Germany in the 19th and 20th centuries, and of some of the horrendous consequences of that rise.
