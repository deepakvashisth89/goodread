A Man Called Blessed is the sequel to Blessed Child, which revolved around the beloved Caleb's journey. This novel, however, develops its plot around the Israel-Palestine conflict with a current focus on the discovery of the Ark of the Covenant. The story follows Rebecca and her Israeli squadron's search for the Ark in a certain remote monastery (wonder where the connection to the prequel is?) as well as Ismael in his attempt to thwart the Ark's discovery but more decidedly to kill Rebecca. Eventually, Caleb is unassumingly absorbed into the journey, and a stirring adventure between three parties unfold with the Ark at the centerpiece--its existence, and even its significance, in laced with uncertainty.

I was initially jarred by the initial disjointedness of this book from its prequel with Caleb hardly in sight in the first third of the story. When he finally appears, the only continuity from the first book was Leiah and Jason's marriage, Caleb's miraculous demonstrations, and his pure faithfulness to God in his childhood days. Once I stopped wondering what the developments of this book had to do with the events of the first, I was able to appreciate the intensity of the rising external and internal conflicts, in which the search for truth was thematic concurrently both outward and in.

In the midst of an action-packed fictive adventure, I appreciated the highlights of different elements surrounding the real-world history of the Israel-Palestine conflict; historical events and landmarks were deftly woven, prompting the reader with starting points to learn more about yet not taking away from the flow of the events in the story. Dekker once again did not fail to deliver enthralling battles with many dramatic stages in conjunction with battles of the heart within his characters.

While this book is by no means a citation for the objective analysis of world religions, I found it to be a fair take on the core motivations of and ensuing friction between the Islamic, Judaic, and Christian worldviews. I was ambivalent towards this book's ending; I found it abrupt, but I applaud Dekker for its inconspicuousness until the very end. I also recognize Dekker's attention to the sensitive nature of the plot's setting by avoiding a reckless conclusion that could send inadvertently charged messages toward the present real-world conflict.
