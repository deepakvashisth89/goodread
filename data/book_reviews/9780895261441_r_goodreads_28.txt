IS THERE A "GAY STRANGLEHOLD" ON CATHOLIC SEMINARIES?Michael S. Rose is editor of St. Catherine Review, and has also written 'Priest: Portraits of Ten Good Men Serving the Church Today and Striving to Serve Him Faithfully,' 'The Renovation Manipulation: The Church Counter-Renovation Handbook,' and 'Ugly As Sin: Why They Changed Our Churches from Sacred Places to Meeting Spaces and How We Can Change Them Back Again.'He wrote in the Introduction to this 2002 book, "In the months before this book went to press, Catholics throughout the United States were scandalized by one revelation after another about sexual abuse among the Catholic clergy... The big question now: Why is this happening?... I researched and wrote this book over the past two years... Although I did not set out to write a book about clerical sex abuse, what I discovered provides at least part of the answer to the burning question: How could this have happened?"'Goodbye, Good Men' presents documented evidence that the root of this problem... extends down to the very place where vocations to the priesthood germinate: the seminary. Too often men who support the teachings of the Church... on sexual morality, are dismissed for being 'rigid'... while those seminarians who ... 'come out' as gays ... are given preferential treatment and then ordained to the Catholic priesthood... gross sexual immorality and the protective network formed around that immorality is only one of the major issues that needs to be forthrightly addressed by the shepherds of the Catholic Church." (Pg. ix-xi)He notes that the Kansas City Star "presented evidence that more than three hundred priests have died of AIDS since the mid-1980s... [which] translated into an annual death rate of about four per ten thousand---four times the rate of the general population." (Pg. 23)He quotes a seminarian who "gradually became aware of a gay subculture in the seminary... there was the very close allegiance between the more militant gay priests on the faculty and the gay nuns who wanted to be priests... I noticed the formation faculty was overwhelmingly gay, while the academic faculty was predominantly heterosexual." (Pg. 72)Rose says, "From my own research, it appears that, although there is a very serious problem among seminaries that cater primarily to those training for the diocesan priesthood, the homosexual problem is deeper and more pervasive among the religious orders." (Pg. 82)He also notes (second-hand, it should be observed) "one particularly notable case of sexual harassment... He took academic classes at the seminary and received his formation at the religious house. When he got into seminary... he was constantly accosted by homosexual superiors. Although a homosexual himself, [he] wanted to be chaste." (Pg. 80-81)Another told him that when he was a 14-year old minor seminarian, "Sometimes I'd get a knock on the door of my room late at night, and one of the upperclassmen would slide a copy of Playboy under my door. This was the common way to circulate these magazines, but I would slide it right back out. A few days later one of my high school teachers, a priest... [told] me that fourteen-year-old boys shouldn't be praying in Latin and reading the Code of Canon Law. He told me that I ought to be 'reading' Playboys like the other guys. I was aghast." (Pg. 161)He suggests, "many, many vocations were destroyed through the undermining of chastity. Once desensitized in sex workshops or by poring through sexually explicit material in seminary textbooks, the prospect of dating and carrying on a straightforward sexual relationship with a woman (as opposed to a perverted sexual relationship) probably struck many seminarians as rather reasonable. Be that as it may, the saints have repeatedly taught that it is difficult to remain faithful to the Church if one cannot remain chaste." (Pg. 205)He was told by some orthodox seminarians that "there does exist an 'underground network' of orthodox seminarians.... but they keep their prayer life and spiritual direction with orthodox priests in strict confidence... students who are loyal to Church teaching and oppose the gay stranglehold on the seminary trade notes, conference tapes, and spiritual reading... without it, very few of us would survive... there are some "closet-orthodox" members of the faculty who try to offer the truth, although they must often do so surreptitiously.'" (Pg. 61)This is definitely a provocative and thought-provoking book, which will interest most readers with an interest in Catholic issues. While one may quibble about Rose's research and his large reliance on testimonials, his book is challenging reading for persons on all sides of the issues he raises.
