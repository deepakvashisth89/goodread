It’s always a trip when finding out the way one sees the world—and I mean this physically—if not to be “totally wrong” could be “very much incomplete”. And even more so when the person telling you as much has the receipts to prove it. Science books have increasingly been something that fascinates me. Obviously the ones by and for scientists may not (yet) be at my skill-set, but the books such as “Einstein's Unfinished Symphony: The Story of a Gamble, Two Black Holes, and a New Age of Astronomy” that hit the sweet spot between being too simple and too difficult are excellent challenges. I want to learn, to be excited, to be fascinated, to know more today than I did yesterday and Marcia Bartusiak wildly succeeds in satisfying my needs

The focus at least early on may seem to be on Albert Einstein and his thoughts do bubble up from time to time, but this is not a biography ala the one by Walter Isaacson. This also is not an ‘intellectual biography’ that looks at the man mostly through his thoughts. Rather, using Einstein’s scientific output—specifically his theory of relativity and its relation to the search for gravitational waves, we get front row seats to what the author aptly describes in the title as a symphony that has not yet reached its conclusion...but given recent events that were of such importance the original book was massively updated in 2017 and that is the version I had the fortunate ability to read.

A big question I’m sure most any has when reading this is just how “difficult” is it going to get. Are we getting a book that while may not be a biography per say may still spend a lot of time focusing on Einstein doing math things? Thankfully, no. As noted, this is neither. “ Einstein's Unfinished Symphony” in a way is an expanded yet easy to parse explanation of Einstein’s Theory of Relativity and how a major aspect of it was later proven right. Explanations like this really do make it easy for those with less advanced intellects (hello!) to finally “get it”:





On that note, Marcia Bartusiak really does an excellent job of always focusing first and foremost on the science, but still pulls back from time to time to allow the lay reader to take it all in. Sometimes this comes simply from describing advanced concepts in simpler terms and other times from talking less about the technicals and more about the emotion *behind* the technicals:



Normally, as Shakespeare noted in Hamlet and I learned from a great book “May Contain Lies: How Stories, Statistics, and Studies Exploit Our Biases—And What We Can Do about It” by Alex Edmans, that when you have a piece that “doth protest too much”, caution should be used. Sometimes a little bit of “protesting”—here in the form of these ‘emotional sections’ should not be seen as alarm or injecting sensationalism to cover mediocre results, but simply to give a little bit of respite before diving back in to what makes both our world and space itself so amazing: how in spite of its immense size, it and us may be a lot more connected than we can imagine. In fact, among the large cast of characters—all given just enough ‘screen time’ to make us care for them, one can zero in on Joseph Weber being the most tragic of figures: his findings may be disputed, but he stuck to his guns and only eventually admitted conceit well into his final years. And perhaps without him, this book nor this review may have been written.

This book is full of wonders; wonders of science (duh), of space (also duh), and of sheer human fortitude to prove what needs to be proved.
