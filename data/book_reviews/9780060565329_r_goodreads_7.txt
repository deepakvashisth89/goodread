I greatly enjoyed this biography of one of history’s more controversial figures, despite some problems with sources and context. The “Mitford sisters” remain a perennial source of fascination for biography readers, having produced a remarkable number of biographies for a group of women who, with one exception (Nancy), left relatively little mark on the world. Diana comes from this group, but the interest in her generates mostly from her devotion to her husband, Sir Oswald Mosley, and his party, the British Union (of Fascists). Upper-crust British women are not known for being rough-and-tumble street fighters or crude racists, and therein lies the interest.

De Courcy had access to Diana in her declining years and interviewed her extensively. This is not, however, an “authorized biography,” and Diana insisted it not be published before her death. She had objected strenuously to her son-in-law’s biography of Mosley, and this book is, if anything, more critical and disapproving of him as a person, although it achieves some level of sympathy with Diana, if not her politics. My suspicion is that she would have been pretty upset by it, precisely because of her dogged loyalty to the man she loved and who, some might argue, ruined her life.

De Courcy traces Diana’s life, from her upbringing in an aristocratic but poor family, through her marriage to Bryan Guinness, heir to the Guinness Beer fortune, through her meeting Mosley and beginning a passionate affair that led to her divorce and eventual marriage to a man her family thoroughly disapproved of (at least at first). It also traces her political development, from naïve, vaguely leftist “Lloyd George liberal” to devoted believer in The Leader to eventual Holocaust denier and unrepentant fascist. She was visible in politics due to her proximity to Mosley, although not herself a rabble-rouser. More significant was her eventual closeness to Hitler, who thought of Mosley as a man he could work with on the British scene, and enjoyed being with the young and beautiful Mitford sisters (Unity Mitford was closer to him, having lived in Germany throughout the late 1930s). She and Mosley were secretly married in Germany, at a ceremony with Hitler present, and she was involved in conducting secret negotiations for radio broadcasting stations located on the continent just before the war. For this reason as well as general public sentiment, she and her husband were locked up for most of the war, and the imprisonment only hardened her views and made her see herself as a martyr to her cause. De Courcy covers the later years of her life, after the war, as Mosley made various efforts to revive the Union Movement and after, as she devoted herself to scathing replies to any criticism of him.

I mentioned that there were some problems. De Courcy does use Diana’s evidence carefully, and had access to many relevant documents about her life, which she obviously read meticulously and critically. She also appears to have a wide knowledge of the British literary and social circles in which Diana moved. Her knowledge of German history and the sources she used to learn about it are more problematic. She makes some really odd errors, some of which (stating that Hitler was born on April 21, for example) should have been caught by an editor. More troubling is when she claims that Hitler had “consulted the mystic Gurdjieff” in a chapter which only mentions David Fraser’s biography of Erwin Rommel as a source. Not only does Fraser not make that claim, I have not seen it in any of the literature on Hitler and the occult, from Nicholas Goodricke-Clark to David Suster. She really shows off her ignorance of German history on pages 153-154, in which she states “Streicher had been a loyal worker from the inception of the Nazi Party – his membership number was 2 (Hitler’s was 7).” It would take a while to break down the inaccuracies of this claim, but Streicher only joined the NSDAP after being convinced to merge his own, separate völkisch party with it, and Hitler’s original party member number was 555 – he negotiated the number 7 retroactively after a spat in which he briefly resigned. I have no idea where she got the idea that Streicher held #2, but if he did it must also have been a similar retroactive arrangement.

Such errors will bother specialists in German history, but they do not detract from the overall thrust of her narrative. And in spite of her obvious liking for Diana, she gives more information about the duplicity of Mosley, and the brutality of his party, than other sources I have read. He often comes out seeming like a noble, honest man who simply made a political error, but in De Courcy’s version he is a cad, a liar, and a deliberate stirrer of the worst instincts of his followers, wholeheartedly including antisemitism. It is not hard to imagine, despite his protestations to the opposite, him becoming a full-throated Quisling after a successful Nazi invasion. Diana, too, comes off as pretty terrible politically, although one wonders how fair it is that the public always seems to have hated her more than Sir Oswald. I can’t help but see it as part of the socialized misogyny always targeted at divorced women and “mistresses,” an expression of sexual insecurities and double standards. In short, this book gives us plenty to think about and is well worth the time.
