Non-English majors are warned, this one is upper-level undergraduate seminar. In this book our Eternal Champion is Ritter Von Bek, a wandering noble with good intentions but prone to infamy and scandal. Moorcock loves his literary forms: where Elric inhabits the world of epic saga, Von Bek's adventure is a picaresque, one lavish adventure after another.

If that opening paragraph seemed pretentious and opaque, then congratulations and welcome to the opus of Michael Moorcock. I can only imagine how your mainline reader of 80s fantasy, without the internet at hand, appreciated the dense mythic allusions. Much less translated the snippets of Goethe in the original German. It's a rich enough tapestry, with the stakes being no less than the transcendence of humanity itself, in a battle between absentee (Christian) God and a notionally ethical Satan.

This review is already too verbose and I haven't even gotten to the plot yet. We begin with Von Bek, fallen noble but dedicated enough republican, escaping the Reign of Terror. At first his adventures are typecast: a villainous Frenchman; a conniving Scotsman (equipped with dirigible); and our idee fixe, a beautiful but mysterious Dutchess. About a hundred pages in, our Scotman's dirigible is used to transit to the titular City in its magical twilight realm, where the Dutchess conscripts Von Bek to quest for the Grail and engage in further occult rituals.

As if his name on the cover isn't enough, Moorcock telegraphs that weird transition early and often. We're going from the purportedly rational but horrifying Reign to the utterly irrational occult quest for the Grail, do you get it? (Shall we cite Diderot?) Once we enter the magical realm, the plotting takes on dreamlike qualities. Even though Von Bek needed a dirigible to enter the magical realm, everyone else seems to come in through the back door, new contestants in the quest for the Grail. There are non-explicit sex scenes, lengthy dinners with curious locutors, and Black Masses.

I'm certain to have missed many more allusions of mythical or occult significance. (For instance, the balloonist is named after St. Odhran, the "charioteer" of St. Patrick.) Moorcock also takes a fleeting sojourn into queer sexuality, positing union or "concordance" of sexes through hermaphroditism (which I learned for the first time is a portmanteau of Hermes and Aphrodite). Without spoilers, the closing chapters point toward a Golden Dawn, though it sometimes gets longwinded to the point of incoherence.

If you enjoy picaresque (in its original sense) and don't mind all the occult mumbo-jumbo, City in the Autumn Stars is a pleasant enough read. For more patient readers, there are nice surprises, both in the adventurous encounters and some fairly snappy dialogue. And while Von Bek's obsession with the Dutchess can be offputting, there was some real emotional impact to the denouement. Recommended well enough for fans of Moorcock and anyone else pretentious enough (even, ugh, yours truly), but probably too dense or demanding for most fantasy readers.
