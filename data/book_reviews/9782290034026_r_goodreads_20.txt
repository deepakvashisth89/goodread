I finally understand the hype. Dracula is a slow burn- the kind of book that makes you want to dnf in the opening chapters if you aren't prepared. It’s a classic, but it’s a heavy classic that requires some literary stamina. It demands a reader already well-acquainted with the patience the genre requires. As a seasoned classic lover myself, even I found it difficult to get into initially. If you are new to older literature, I’d actually advise holding off on this one as diving in too soon might just ruin the experience.The story is told entirely through a collection of journal entries, letters, ship logs and newspaper clippings. This is where the difficulty actually lied in for me. Jonathan Harker’s initial journals at Castle Dracula are atmospheric and deeply unsettling, but once the setting shifts back to England, the pacing slows down dramatically. We spend a lot of time reading the daily correspondence of Victorian aristocrats, courting rituals and medical logs. However, later do we begin to realize what the author is trying to do. He is slowly building a sense of dread. He lays down a meticulous track of breadcrumbs and when the horror finally bleeds into the ordinary lives of the characters, the payoff is spectacular.It is a wild experience to engage with the text that undeniably shaped modern vampire lore. Even though Sheridan Le Fanu’sCarmillaintroduced the concept,Dracularepresents the mainstream debut of this genre. It was the definitive moment vampire fiction which lays the foundations for the what we know of the genre persisting today.The story begins with Jonathan Harker, an English solicitor traveling to Transylvania for a routine property transaction. Poor man. His only real mistake is not running away after seeing an endless parade of bad omens and foreshadowing done in the story. What begins as boring legal paperwork quickly devolves into the worst business trips in literary history.Cut to the 1890s. The scene shifts back to England, where a series of bizarre, chilling incidents begin taking place around Whitby. The most heartbreaking thread follows Lucy, whose life is slowly being drained away by a mysterious illness while her best friend, Mina (engaged to Jonathan and then later married), stands by helplessly. Mina is easily one of the most capable figures in the entire book. Brilliant and emotionally resilient, her journals and sheer clarity of mind are what I truly admired. It isn’t until the brilliant Professor Van Helsing investigates that the truth is revealed- this is the sole work of Count Dracula himself. Suddenly, the situation is life and death, leaving Harker, Mina, and Van Helsing as the only ones standing between London and a monster.Reading this was a kind of strange experience for me. It felt as if I knew it all when I actually didn't. Felt so familiar. I was actually dying for an origin story, but nothing was mentioned. To be honest, perhaps that mystery is exactly what makes him so terrifying- a primordial terror who doesn’t require a neat backstory to be effective.
