Ned, a career spy in the British secret service, is the central character in le Carre’s The Secret Pilgrim. At the end of his career Ned is the head of Sarratt (the Nursery) where new agents are trained, and he invites George Smiley, who has supported Ned at various points throughout his career, to provide what amounts to a commencement talk to the new agents. Of course, this turns into quite the event, given Smiley’s accomplishments and reptation; he keeps the agents in training entranced throughout the evening. Toby Esterhase and Burr, the current Chief of the Service, make an appearance for this historic evening. As a result of Smiley’s talk, Ned ruminates on his career, which he tells us about in his own voice.Ned’s career has its ups and downs. He makes mistakes, which on a few occasions Smiley plays a role behind the scenes in helping Ned survive. As might be expected with a new agent, Ned aspires to help his country and the world through his work, but from the beginning he is forced to deal with the reality of that work. It eats at the soul and spirit of the man; le Carre shows us this dehumanization process in Ned while having Smiley describe and summarize the impact as a warning to the new agents. While not le Carre’s best novel (it feels like a collection of related short stories interconnected by Smiley’s evening at the Nursery), The Secret Pilgrim serves admirably to present le Carre’s view of the social and political order following the end of the Cold War. Despite this more serious purpose, the stories themselves are thoroughly entertaining and provide further evidence of le Carre’s ability to write an excellent spy story.For someone reading this as part of the George Smiley series, there may be some disappointment. Smiley does not play a significant role in the novel and is absent much of the time, similar to some of the early novels in the series. But it does provide some limited insight into George Smiley in retirement. Much to my delight, Smiley is at peace with himself.
