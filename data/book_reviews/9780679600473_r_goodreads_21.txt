[read as part of a Reboot book club]This book was truly eye-opening. Jacobs' way of writing, like Sontag or Graeber, is so confidently defiant of a perceived consensus or status quo that you can't help but agree with her every step of the way. You will finish this book as a staunch opponent of the Garden / Radiant City philosophy and utterly contemptuous of urban planning as a discipline (at least, as it stood in the 1960s). Note that this is an enormous book, and it took me a couple months aided by a book club to get through all of it. Buy it, put it on your bookshelf, and read a chapter or two every time you step out into a new part of a city. :)Where I suspect Jacobs' analysis is lacking, particularly when applied to cities of our time, is the role of global capital in re-shaping the face of the urban landscape. Urban planners and bureaucrats are the main target of her critique; while developers are certainly mentioned in the book, I felt as if she didn't contend enough with the sheer force of capital to remake cities in its image. Jacobs calls cities problems of organized complexity, likening them to complex biological systems like cells and organs. True, but they also are engines of capital. Policy decisions at the local level aren't just the results of the whims of urban planners and bureaucrats like Robert Moses - they are driven by the demands of multinational real estate development firms, shrinking tax bases, the race to the bottom for tech companies to build headquarters in one city over another. This -1 star is not really a critique of Jacobs approach - it's more of my own unrealistic expectation that this book would have given me all the tools I needed to understand today's cities, in addition to those of Jacobs' time.
