An absorbing dive into a soap-philosophical opera that is about more than tears and swaying. It is a celebration of life itself and thus of immortality. I am astonished by how beautifully woven this story is. You read something, then something else, and in the end you begin to notice that all that exists is what humans are made of. Huxley manages, in a subtle and intimate way, to peer into people’s souls and draw out the finest, the grotesque, the despicable, the marvellous in them. There are so many ways in which you can hate and love someone, in which you can be devoted, in which you can deceive, cherish, despise, and so many ways in which you can be subjugated by a creed, by someone you love, by your gut, your instincts, and your most vivid reveries and darkest nightmares. The narrative unfolds in such a way that it is impossible not to delight in the grim realism that Huxley constructs step by step, little by little, sorrow by sorrow, spiralling into absurdity. And somehow, you can still make sense of it; in the end, it all makes profound sense. It all builds up to a moral that, this time, after reading so many unsuccessful books over the past few months, is finally not only pseudo, but truly tangible. The narration goes hand in hand with people’s interactions; it is their foundation. Therefore, I cannot say that I liked, or had the “time” to grow fond of or resent, any character, but only that I was totally in awe of and enthralled by their interactions. I craved those interactions. Jeremy and Obispo, Porter and Pete, have truly meaningful connections that transcend the mere exchange of ideas. You could see, even before the apotheotic ending, that the characters really and deeply tried to change their way of thinking, their faith, and their object of adoration or resentment as they spoke and exchanged ideas. I really liked how the act of cogitation changed faiths, derailed thoughts, and guided the characters who were being spoken to. It was not only conversation for the sake of it, but something that gained meaning in itself. It is truly wonderful that what you would like to see more of in daily life takes such an august shape and state of mind in the hearts and minds of those willing to listen. I am not really accustomed to stating why I gave it 4, not 5, stars; it is a subtle and yet huge difference, but I did not feel that it hit me as much as it could have. I just know deep down how I should have felt when being deeply hit by a book and, sadly, this one missed some spots, was too fragile at times, and the transitions were probably too swift for me. Probably, I can only guess now, some transitions from gravity to Virginia’s innocence and her little games were a little disruptive. Thinking about it now, the instances in which Virginia appeared were too tedious to read and far weaker by comparison. Of course, everything took a turn for the better and Virginia herself had a moment of glory that I admired at the end, but throughout, her part was, in a sense, far less bitter, more childlike, gentler, and at times devoid of substance due to repetition. Maybe her sanctified appearance would have hit differently without being emphasised so much. It would have been more powerful and saint-like; by repeating her purpose, she was, sadly, stripped of what made her actually distinctive. But that is just my own impression, what I felt in my at times sensitive gut. I would recommend this book to anyone eager to take a scalpel and dissect some emotions and ideas with the assurance that the slaughter will not be in vain.

pp. 121–122: “We don’t even make the simple Greek distinction between erao and philo, eras and agape. With us, everything is just love, whether it’s self-sacrificing or possessive, whether it’s friendship or lust or homicidal lunacy. It’s all just love,” he repeated. “Idiotic word! Even on the human level it’s hopelessly ambiguous. And when you begin using it in relation to experiences on the level of eternity—well, it’s simply disastrous. ‘The love of God.’ ‘God’s love for us.’ ‘The saint’s love for his fellows.’ What does the word stand for in such phrases? And in what way is this related to what it stands for when it’s applied to a young mother suckling her baby? Or to Romeo climbing into Juliet’s bedroom? Or to Othello as he strangles Desdemona? Or to the research worker who loves his science? Or to the patriot who’s ready to die for his country—to die, and, in the meantime, to kill, steal, lie, swindle and torture for it? Is there really anything in common between what the word stands for in these contexts and what it stands for when one talks, let us say, of the Buddha’s love for all sentient beings? Obviously, the answer is: No, there isn’t. On the human level, the word stands for a great many different states of mind and ways of behaving. Dissimilar in many respects, but alike at least in this: they’re all accompanied by emotional excitement and they all contain an element of craving. Whereas the most characteristic features of the enlightened person’s experience are serenity and disinterestedness. In other words, the absence of excitement and the absence of craving.”
“The absence of excitement and the absence of craving,” Pete said to himself, while the image of Virginia in ‘her yachting cap, riding her pink scooter, kneeling in her shorts under the arch of the Grotto,’ swam before his inward eye.

p. 160: “Watching them through the pellucid Water, I marvel at the strength and unimpaired agility of these great Fishes, of which the oldest were perhaps alive when the Utopia was written, while the youngest are coeval with the author of Paradise Lost. Philosophers have wasted their own and their readers’ time in speculations upon the Immortality of the Soul; the Alchemists have pored for centuries over their crucibles in the vain hope of discovering the Elixir or the Stone. p. 188: “The Christians talk much of Pain, but nothing of what they say is to the point. For the most remarkable Characteristics of Pain are these: the Disproportion between the enormity of physical suffering and its often trifling causes; and the manner in which, by annihilating every faculty and reducing the body to helplessness, it defeats the Object for which it was apparently devised by Nature: viz.: to warn the sufferer of the approach of Danger, whether from within or without. In relation to Pain, that empty word, Infinity, comes near to having a meaning. This is not the case with Pleasure; for Pleasure is strictly finite and any attempt to extend its boundaries results in its transformation into Pain. For this reason, the infliction of Pleasure can never be so delightful to the aspiring Mind as the infliction of Pain. To give a finite quantity of Pleasure is a merely human act; the infliction of the Infinity we call Pain is truly godlike and divine.”
