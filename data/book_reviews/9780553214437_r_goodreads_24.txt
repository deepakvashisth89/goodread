Imagine you are starting a new job and aren’t confident you will succeed. Even if you are excited, you might be nervous. Now imagine if you had an uncle who has been in the business a long time and is very good at it. You would probably be thrilled if he decided to coach and teach you the ropes. Say you live far apart and he writes letters to you, sending you career tips and helping you with any problems you are having. Even if sometimes your uncle gets mad at you, after a while, you’d probably start feeling pretty good about your job, and even consider yourself lucky getting his help and counsel.

This is the premise of The Screwtape Letters by C.S. Lewis. Uncle Screwtape is coaching his nephew, Wormwood, in his new career. But it’s not a typical career -- Screwtape is a devil and he’s helping Wormwood corrupt men’s souls and winning them for Satan away from God. Everything about what Screwtape is teaching Wormwood is opposite from what we’ve been taught in our Catholic schooling and catechism. God is called “the Enemy” and Satan is called “Our Father Below.” In the series of 31 letters Screwtape sends to Wormwood, we get a picture of the ways that men are tempted away from the good and swayed from doing the right thing. Most theology books tell us how to be good. Lewis’s book flips the focus and shows how devils tempt us to be bad. But by showing us these tricks, the author illustrates how simple it is to do the right thing. By being ironical, Lewis is both entertaining and instructive.

The devils call the men they are assigned to corrupt “the patient,” and this too is ironical. Doctors call their clients patients and are there to help them be or get well. Lewis’s devils want to make their patients worse or all together bad. Of course, doctors are concerned with the body; devils are concerned with the soul.

The time and place that Wormwood is tempting his patient is in England before and during the “war.” It doesn’t really matter which war - it could be WWI or WWIV - because the themes that Lewis uses are universal constants for any war - patriotism, pacifism, “the cause,” - any belief can be a way into a man’s soul when it becomes a “religion” and keeps the man from thinking about the most important things. In Letter 6, Screwtape writes “provided that meetings, pamphlets, policies, movements, causes, and crusades matter more to him than prayers and sacraments and charity, he is ours.” The way Screwtape explains things shows how close the line between good and bad in our actions are - no matter what we choose - we need to guard that we are acting humbly and in faith with God.

Lewis throws in a lot of funny stuff right from the start. In Letter 1 for instance, Screwtape warns against trying to reason with people because humans don’t believe in proof anymore and can juggle a dozen incompatible ideas in their heads at any given time. Beware even of science which can make people think deeply about things they can’t see (maybe God or his own soul) - so try to make the patient think he knows it all. Screwtape says it’s best to keep the patient befuddled, a good way to keep him from thinking about important stuff that “might lead to trouble.” Even in Letter 7 when Wormwood asked whether he is supposed to keep the patient ignorant of his (Wormwood’s) existence, Screwtape says yes - standing orders on that are for ignorance. But if the man thinks about it, to conjure up silly or comic images of someone in red tights - something that of course he can’t believe in and that his mind will avoid.

In everything the man tries, Screwtape has advice for how Wormwood can use it to corrupt the man, from becoming a Christian, going to church, making new friends, enjoying music, having fun, falling in love or being celibate, having courage or even feeling fear or hatred. For each, making the man raise any of them to the level of religion, to be proud, to focus on the material forms of better, and greater, on judging others lower or worse and setting oneself higher or better - are how a man can be corrupted in any of his actions.

There are some real gems in the letters from Screwtape, not ironical but sincere for seeking grace and knowing God. For instance, in Letter 13, though you have to remember God is the Enemy and the devils think of humans as lowly as rats, he writes “the Enemy wants to detach men from themselves…. He really likes the little vermin, and sets an absurd value on the distinctness of every one of them. When He talks of their losing their selves, He means only abandoning their clamour of self-will; once they have done that, He really gives them back all their personality, and… when they are fully His they will be more themselves than ever…. He hates to see them drifting away from their own nature.” Lewis is reminding us that the deepest likings and impulses within each of us are the unique “raw material” God has furnished us to find Him - not to strive for the “best people,” the “right food,” or “important books,” but enjoy in innocence and humility and gratitude the things that make us uniquely his children. Similarly, in Letter 14, Screwtape writes, “He really loves the hairless bipeds he has created, and always gives back to them with His right hand what He has taken away with His left.” God loves us, and even the devil knows it. And in Letter 25, another gem is talk about “the future.” Screwtape writes, “The Enemy loves platitudes…. He wants men… to ask… very simple questions: Is it righteous? Is it prudent? Is it possible?” Instead the devil makes them ask unanswerable questions about the future and progressiveness - to have their minds “buzzing in a vacuum.” When we need to decide something, we need to ask simple questions and seek simple answers for making the right choices.

But Lewis yanks us back into the irony of having devils be the story’s protagonists when in Letter 19 Screwtape admits that when he says the Enemy loves humans he is heretical. He suddenly realizes that if his letters fall into the wrong hands, he could get in big trouble. This is pretty funny when you realize that even devils need to believe in the evilness of their Enemy - that God is a liar when He tells us He loves us. And just like we’re taught to work in teams and coordinate our efforts to be more effective, devils do too. Several times Screwtape talks about steps Wormwood can take to work together with devils assigned to other people in his patient’s life, like his mother (Glubose) and his girlfriend (Slumtrimpet). By working together, devils can help sour relationships that might otherwise be loving and graceful by blowing up small things into annoying habits and irritating pinpricks.

By the end of the letters, it is clear that though the man, Wormwood's patient, did not survive the war, he had tried and succeeded in loving God and being good. And lucky him -- at the moment of his death, he saw angels and his Lord. Wormwood lost the soul, and for that, we as Christians can be happy. Even though the devils were the story’s protagonists, we didn’t want them to win.

My favorite Lewis book. 4 1/2 stars.

NB: I am agnostic but have written my review in the spirit of a Christian sympathetic to Lewis's perspective to fully flesh out his purpose. I felt this was the best way to convey its significance.
