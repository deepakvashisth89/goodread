On the 70th birthday of Lady Crystal Balaclava (which I kept pronouncing in my head as Lady Baklava) she invites an unusual guest to the annual and very obligatory family soiree: Chief Superintendent Henry Tibbett (and his wife Emmy of course). Incognito naturally, though the pretense fools no one. Crystal is convinced she's going to be murdered at her bon anniversaire celebration this year. How does she know this with such premonition and conviction? Why a message from her late husband via Ouija board of course. If it were anyone else, the police would dismiss it as the imaginings of a lonely old biddy. BUT Crystal was quite the scandalous socialite in her salad days and she wound up moving in circles and making acquaintances with carefree young gents who ultimately landed / crawled their way into impressive positions of power. She has influence, her name certainly still means something (and, what's left unspoken but understood, is that she likely has dirt on these (en)titled bums from those reckless days of youth!).The usual suspects include Crystal's three daughters, all named after flowers per her late husband's bizarre whimsy: Primrose (prim and proper, indeed), Violet (repressed and volatile with a veneer of martyrdom), and Daffodil (Daffy is not daft in the least, she's the most cunning and opportunistic of the lot, and thus the most like her mother). And then there's the husbands, all born of foreign climes and bringing very different family values and attachments. Piet is the humble Dutch rose grower, with a small family shop who believes in the traditional ways and scoffs at modern chemicals, growing techniques, and in general, the trappings of wealth and status. He's utterly charmed by and devoted to Violet for some inexplicable reason as she's quite caustic, the least charming person, and downright rude and dismissive of Piet most of the time. Edouard Duval, Primrose's husband, and the first person the reader meets, is the Swiss doctor determined to raise funding to open his own private research laboratory, whether he has to 'beg, borrow, or steal' a fortune of his own to do so. Good luck if he's thinking of begging his heiress wife, as Primrose is tight lipped and a tightwad. Meanwhile Charles doesn't want the money, not because he shares Piet's quiet disregard for it, but because he has so much of his own! The American millionaire made his money in kitchen sinks. When Daffodil learns that little tidbit from one of the other models at a social party she introduces herself to Charles, nearly twice her age, by leading with "We have a lot in common. My great-grandfather invented one of the first self-flushing water-closets in England." Six months later Charles had divorced his third wife and married Daffy.We get these witty and concise, detailed yet brief, profiles of our main characters in just the first few pages. The tableau unfolds as each couple prepares for their trip to the family pile for Crystal's birthday, most of them dreading it, and each tasked with bringing a specific present, each of which will feature later as one of the potential murder weapons. Then the first chapter concludes with this little gem:"Four airline tickets, two from Geneva, two from Paris. A double cabin on The Hook-Harwich ferry. Six packed suitcases. An elaborate iced cake. Two dozen dark red roses. A case of vintage champagne. All converging on the house known as Foxes' Trot, near the village of Plumley Green in the County of Surrey."How's that for a perfect set up? Honestly I just love Moyes writing.When Crystal DOES drop dead, almost instantly, even though Inspector Tibbett has taken a test bite of the cake, a sip of champagne from Crystal's own glass, and buried his nose in the roses for the first enchanting sniff, everyone assumes it's poisoning as the symptoms couldn't possibly be anything else. When the preliminary doctor's report comes back with a conclusion of death by natural causes... everyone is shocked, though some rather relieved. Henry doubles down that it IS murder and he will solve the case or he will resign and end his career. He can't stand to do otherwise. What follows is a most perplexing case, an interesting travelogue as Emmy and Henry visit Lake Geneva, Paris, and Holland in short order at the very end of the book as they near the conclusion of the case and narrow down the suspects to suss out the murderer. The method of murder in this was so unique and ingenious and I learned quite a bit along the way as well! The most satisfying and unexpected surprise came in the afterword, where Moyes shares HOW she came up with the plot, and a most clever pun that she's hidden for us in plain sight all along. Ingenious!
