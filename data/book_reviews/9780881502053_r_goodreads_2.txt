A collection of three Asey Mayo stories. Phoebe Atwood Taylor’s mysteries are always an entertaining mix of mystery, interesting characters, and humor, which varies from light (most Leonidas Witherall stories) to screwball (certain Asey Mayo stories). These particular stories were published in the early 1940s. They’re rather far-fetched at times, but are definitely worth a look.

It has been 10 years since the original Asey Mayo novel, The Cape Cod Mystery, and things have changed quite a bit for Asey. He is no longer a handyman. He is now a well-known detective and a director of Porter Motors. However, Asey himself has not changed – he is still clever, down to earth, and resourceful.







His unexpected visitor is David Arlington, a young man who is searching for his wife. Lora Arlington is a member of the wealthy Head family, but she has been disowned because she chose to marry the penniless David rather than the man chosen for her by her family. Now Lora’s uncle, Captain Tiberius Head, who is the head of the family, has held out an olive branch to the couple, though David is suspicious.

David reluctantly took Lora to Headacre House, but panicked when she did not return after a long period of time. He also found Lora’s skirt, badly stained, lying on Headacre House’s lawn. David is about to storm the mansion until Asey intervenes. The hot-headed young man will just make a mess out of everything. Things don’t quite turn out like the two men planned – instead of Lora, they find her uncle – dead! And it isn’t long before there is a second murder.

I feel a little for Asey here, stuck among some rather foolish and spoiled rich people who don’t know the first thing about taking care of themselves. Lora’s cousin Penny is typical – selfish, immature, and totally lacking in empathy. As long as she and her boyfriend are fine, nothing – and no one – else matters. David is rather annoying – just as immature as Penny, rather paranoid, and lacking in common sense. Lora is a little smarter than either of them, but not by much. Asey has his work cut out for him.

This is a neat little mystery. I figured out who did it fairly early on, but could not figure out how the murder was committed. Taylor’s solution for that is worthy of Ellery Queen. It was quite well done.



When wealthy Nora Latimer gets angry over a young woman trespassing on her property. The young woman has set up a camper and appears to be making herself at home. Asey is reluctant to go with Nora, but it’s her speedboat that they’re in. The girl, Cordelia Alcott, was totally oblivious to the fact that she was camping on private property. Embarrassed, Cordelia hurries to leave:









Cordelia insists that she has no idea who the man is. She cannot explain what happened to her car, her trailer, or her uncle who was traveling with her. She insists that all three were switched while she was enjoying the beach. Needless to say, Cordelia has a hard time convincing anybody of this – except, of course, for Asey Mayo.

A fun Asey Mayo story, though I found the behavior of Cordelia’s uncle hard to understand.



Asey Mayo goes to Boston to pick up his cousin (and housekeeper) Jennie Mayo, who is returning home from a funeral in Chicago. Jennie is late and Asey suspects she has wandered off to the Public Garden:



Impatient, Asey goes looking for Jennie, but just finds her luggage – and a body. A young man has been shot in the head. A quick examination of the scene shows that the man was probably taking photos of a very strange subject indeed:



Jennie soon shows up being chased by a cop, who claims that she is a purse snatcher. However, Jennie insists she witnessed the murder of the photographer by a young woman. Jennie tried to grab the young woman, but just ended up with her purse. However, the cop is having none of it and soon Jennie and Asey find themselves running from the law. Asey realizes he has to clear both of them by solving the murder.

I liked this mystery the least of all three stories, though it was amusing. The killer was easy to figure out and the motive wasn’t difficult either. As with “The Headacre Plot,” though, the how of the murder was the most interesting part. You can’t believe everything you see.

All in all, a good collection of mystery stories, but I believe the novels are better.
