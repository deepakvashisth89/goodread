At just a hair over 250 pages, this series starter is good, but not great. First off I'll be a little petty and get into the story's presentation. There's a lovely painted bookcover and Paul Ledd is a good house name for the writer. In fact, it's better than the series itself. 'Shelter'... what is that? Were all the good names taken? What about 'Shelter' says sex OR violence?

In the book, it's often shortened to Shell, which makes more sense: it's related to guns, it's usually hard, it can describe someone's emotional state. There are in fact a couple people with the last name Shell, so I don't know why they didn't just go with that.

Secondly... I'm still being petty... the tagline is "he harbored a seven-year-old grudge for justice--and pleasure!" What does that even mean? Who has a grudge for pleasure? I get that they're trying to say he shoots people and he has sex, but think about the phrasing for five seconds. "For seven years he hungered for revenge... and for pleasure!" Isn't that a bit more pleasing to the eye?

Okay, done being petty, promise. The book is a bit overlong--there are a few places where it slows things down to introduce characters who pretty much immediately take a hike and there are a couple too many villains to keep track of, to the point where some turn out to have died off-screen. Other than that, pretty reasonable Western.

Shelter Dorsett was a Confederate soldier ordered to retrieve gold that would allow the South to continue the war (not in hopes of eking out a victory, but to get a more favorable treaty). There's an interesting moral dilemma there, with some of his compatriots arguing about just taking the gold for themselves, asking what good it will really do to prolong the war *any*, but this isn't that kind of 'adult' Western. So the bad guys are bad because they steal the gold and kill Shelter's pals.

Seven years later, Shelter is out of prison and going for revenge. At least, this according to the backcover--as it comes across in story, he seems to be a POW until the War ends. Shell doesn't seem particularly to be a hardened convict, but pretty personable for a guy on a roaring rampage of revenge. In the opening chapter, he's challenged by the ex of the lady he's schtupping, a confrontation that ends with Shelter getting the guy to back down, advising him to apologize and see if he can work things out with the lady (!).

So on the one hand, that's a bit refreshing in a post-Edge Western, that the hero has some actual noble qualities. On the other hand, you'd think a guy who's been in prison for seven years, nursing a grudge, would be a bit rougher around the edges.

From there, Shell is off on a lengthy train ride to track down one of his tormentors, with some interesting problem-solving as the train is trapped in a blizzard. This is sort of like a little novella inside the larger book, because once this plotline wraps up, we settle into a 'town' story arc of Shelter going against the town boss, figuring out his plan, tangling with his henchmen, etc. It's no great shakes--one of those "frame the Indians" schemes that has to happen twice as much in Western fiction as Indians actually attacking--but it goes down nicely.

Props to the writer for bookending the story well. It opens with Shell in a deadly firefight, which turns out to be a flashback he's having while in bed with his lady--then the story ends with one last villain to be dispatched, and the lady he's taken hostage immediately deciding to seek Shelter (does that work as wordplay? not really). So I admire that level of brazenness in fitting just a little more sex and violence into the formula.
