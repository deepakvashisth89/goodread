The author of this book presents a case for the theory that Aleksandr the First faked his death in 1825 and went on to live the life of a wandering acetic in Siberia. As a story, this book is good. As a presentation of historical evidence, rather weak.Everypiece of evidence is circumstantial or second or third person information. The author suggests that a scientific inquiry into who lies in Aleksandr's tomb in Petersburg and who lies in the grave in Tomsk will clear up the controversy. However, he himself cites circumstantial evidence claiming that the bodies in the tomb were swapped, that the grave is empty, etc, so such an inquiry doesn't really clear anything up. If they find Aleksandr's remains in the tomb at the Peter-Paul Fortress, does that mean he was buried there in 1825 when he "is reported to have died," or were they placed there some time later, after the death of the starets Feodor Kuzmich?Circumstantial evidence in general bothers me, unless it's used to corroborate hard evidence. There is simply no hard evidence anywhere in this book. Troubetzkoy explains this away by saying that subsequent tsars destroyed all the evidence, and in fact claims that this further supports his theory, but there again is simply another piece of circumstantial evidence.
