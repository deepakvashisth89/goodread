DRIVE! That was the mantra of General George S. Patton, glorious commander U.S. troops in North Africa, Sicily, France and the final offensive into Germany in 1945. This diary, notes for a future book of memoirs destiny denied him writing, reveal a man obsessed with war and victory to the expense of everything else, including life itself. WAR AS I KNEW IT chronicles his victories, he discusses no defeats, from the American landings in Morocco in December of 1942 until the final triumph over the Wermacht and his disastrous reign as Military Governor of Bavaria, with candor and bluster. Patton drove himself and his men to maximum performance while driving his superiors, Supreme Commander Dwight Eisenhower and General Bradley, crazy with his insubordination. Patton thought military discipline applied to everyone but himself. He makes no apologies for this trait. When a field commander knows he is in the right he must lie, or plead ignorance of orders, to secure his objective. "A general who is not a prima donna will never be successful", he muses. Not that Patton was ever intentionally brusque. Quite the contrary. The bulk of these notes concern his diplomatic efforts to persuade, by wheedling if necessary, Ike and Bradley to switch divisions and battalions his way, or stubbornly insisting his own troops not be lent to another army, American or British. These pages make for dry and bewildering reading. Keeping up with all those acronyms for Divisions and Battalions is not easy or pleasant, until we find out why Patton deems it necessary. General Bernard Montgomery is the villain of these pages. After D-day in Normandy and against Eisenhower's orders, Monty insisted American troops be reassigned to his sector for a powerful thrust into the Ruhr and on to Berlin. Patton, for once, agreed with Ike in a broad front strategy of advancing slowly from France into Germany in an arc that ran from Holland to Italy. He is sure history will vindicate his refusal to lend Montgomery his men, and he turned out to be right. Patton also plays amateur anthropologist with regards to the people he meets. He likes the Arabs of North Africa, even reads the Koran before landing in Morocco, but says bluntly their two worst deficiencies are fatalism, "everything is 'if God wills it'", and the subordination of women. "A case can be made for spreading Christianity more broadly to these people". He is even harder on the Italians he encounters in Sicily. In every town he conquers the men are sitting on the street idle, and "they are worse than the Arabs" when it comes to fulfilling simple tasks like home construction and taming animals for transportation. Patton's views on the Black troops that served in his Third Army may be guessed. They make great cannon fodder but don't let them anywhere near the artillery corps. They're not intelligent enough to handle that job. The General writes eloquently of his terrifying visits to what he calls Nazi "slave camps" after liberation in 1945, notably Buchenwald. He observes the piano wire and reinforced whips used to torture prisoners, and surmises the technique for getting rid of gassed bodies up chimney stacks. Yet he never mentions the prisoners being Jewish, nor is there any thought given to Nazi Anti-Semitism. For a man who claimed to be supremely apolitical, Patton dealt with difficult political issues by ignoring them. Why was this war fought at all? This volume concludes with tips and counsels Patton thought would be of use to future military commanders, though he admits they are unoriginal and banal---a rare burst of Patton modesty! A military leader must live by two maxims: always attack and never surrender an inch of gained ground---a principle, oddly enough, Patton shared with Adolf Hitler--- and audacity is the key to victory. The surprise attack at a place your enemy deems impossible to conquer is the best way to win. The Germans found that out in the Ardennes in 1940. Yet Patton adds an ironic aside on why this strategy is more likely to work for the American army than others. Americans, he gushes, are a people of invention and mass production of machinery. They must take advantage of that gift to overwhelm the enemy with tanks, planes, and artillery before launching a ground offensive; a strange admission for a man who thought in battle the personality of the commander counted for nearly everything. If he fights, and exposes himself to danger, his troops will follow, and Patton recounts many occasions, from Mexico to France in World War I, each time under Pershnig, where he put himself in harm's way in the hopes of inspiring his men. "Never take counsel of your fears" he frequently notes to himself. Alas, fate paid a cruel joke on him in December of 1945. Instead of dying in battle rallying men around the American flag a U.S. army truck plowed into his staff car, paralyzing him and then taking his life shortly after his sixtieth birthday. WAR AS I KNEW IT is both first-class military history and a psychological self-portrait of a man who believed God had chosen him for great endeavors, and set out to make that afflatus come true.
