‘Every era has its own set of assumptions and truths about how life works. I’m interested in the moment when the rug is pulled out from under a person, when whatever seemed natural to rely on is all of a sudden not there.’Joan Silber is 80 years old and she’s one of those great writers who nobody has ever heard of because she writes character driven novels that are difficult to get people to read. I came across her about seven years ago. ‘Household Words’ won the Pen Faulkner award way back in 1980. It was her first novel. Her second work, ‘Ideas of Heaven,’ was nominated for the National Book Award about a year later. After that, she went over a decade without getting anything published. Complete rejection. But she persisted. She never changed her style. Instead, the fiction market opened up enough to accommodate her niche. And while she’s not for everyone, her ability to write plainly about how ordinary people conduct their lives is intensely analytical without judgment. Even when her characters are unlikable. I think of her as a bridge to the younger female tribe of writers like Kingsolver, Padgett and Strout who came of age in the 1990’s. Just my opinion, of course.‘Household Words’ is about Rhoda Taber, a first generation Jew in the 1940’s and 50’s. The reader is introduced to her when she’s pregnant with her first child. She’s married to a pharmacist. Lives in a New Jersey suburb and by all appearances leads a good life. She quits her job as a high school French teacher and soon has a second child. Both girls. She considers herself successful and above the fray. Until tragedy strikes. Her husband dies of a sudden heart attack and leaves her a wealthy widow. What’s she to do?Besides her grief, Rhoda becomes totally unmoored over the next two decades. What’s expected of her? Does she get a job? Does she remarry? What defines her? Should she micromanage her children? She has no idea she’s a complete mess. And then she becomes ill. A nebulous illness, but serious nonetheless. Is it the loss of her identity that’s put her in jeopardy? I suspect it is. Because if you think it’s tough being a woman today, imagine what it was like almost a hundred years ago. I thought of my own mother and all of the built-in cultural repression that existed during that time period. Unfortunately, it remains a man’s world.
