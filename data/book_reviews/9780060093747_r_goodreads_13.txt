The novelist is neither historian nor prophet: he is an explorer of existence.

I like Milan Kundera’s novels, I like them a lot, and I welcomed this chance to visit backstage with the artist, to glimpse the whole paraphernalia of props, lighting sources, painted backdrops and stage directions.
This collection of essays, conference papers and interviews was a mixed bag for me: many of its parts were extremely interesting and thought provoking, informative about the history of the novel, well structured as regards categories and trends, filled with useful references about important writers considered milestones in the novel’s journey through the centuries. But it is also Eurocentric and subjective, with a heavy emphasis on the German literary space and dismissive of almost everything that takes place outside Europe. Kundera is definitely smart and well-read, but he is also a little too full of himself, too opinionated and haughty about his intellectual chops. Of course, modesty doesn’t become an artist who got to the highest peaks of his profession on his indisputable merits, but his personality, such as I perceived in these pages, doesn’t make him a very interesting fellow to know beyond his written word. This is just a personal reaction on my part that is hard to pin down on any particular stance of the author, but one that got stronger as I read on through his material.

When Kundera talks about the art of the novel, his focus is not on the entertainment, or escapist, part of the exercise. In fact he generally dismisses almost all modern novels as trash and prefers the equivalent of ‘arthouse’ movies to those ‘blockbusters’ that bring in the big money.



He has a valid point here, and his historical look at the evolution of the European novel is probably the best part of the opening essay, starting with Cervantes and taking us along to all the great names that changed something in the way we write and the way we read novels. After taking the entertainment out of the equation, Kundera has the great idea of telling us what he likes, with examples, not only what he deplores:

:
- the appeal of play [Laurence Sterne, Denis Diderot]
- the appeal of dream [Franz Kafka]
- the appeal of thought [Robert Musil, Hermann Brock]
- the appeal of time [Louis Aragon, Carlos Fuentes]

I could develop each of these ideas, but the author does a much better job than I ever could. For me, the best outcome in reading this essay is that I came out with several new authors and novels to add to my wishlist, as if that TBR mountain wasn’t high enough already.



Another reason to be grateful for this exercise in explaining the evolution of the novel, is the concise answer Kundera provides to the question: what is a novel? I like his definition, and I sort of like his take on the deterioration of our common cultural space under the onslaught of mass-media. This also gives us the author’s explanation for the title of his best known work, a meditation on the condition of modern man. Once granted [by God himself] complete mastery of nature and beasts, Man is now relegated back to the condition of puny insect on a tiny pebble lost in the vastness of emptiness.



In a later chapter, Kundera complains about the many instances where his original text was trivialized or misunderstood by his foreign translators. The interviewer proposes a solution, which turns into my second favourite section of the book [’63 Words’]



Here is Kundera at his most candid, probably, where he puts aside his performing before an audience persona and gains a more intimate, more personal dimension. One of his choice words turns out to be ‘lightness’, another riff on that famous title I already mentioned.



There is more to discover in the pages of this book about books and writers, and I wish I had the clarity and the erudition of someone like Milan Kundera to do justice to the subject. I look forward to reading another of his stories instead of this expose of the nuts and bolts hidden backstage, and I hope these same nuts and bolts will not hamper me in enjoying the end result.

I guess I can affirm after reading the book that Kundera’s biggest influences, his literary idols, so to speak, are Franz Kafka and Hermann Brock – he comes back to their work time and time again in his essays.



An interesting, but rather forced and pretentious, device used by the author is to put mathematics and musical notations in his novels. The interview explains the source of Kundera’s interest, but not his insistence on using this rigid form.

Finally, since I plan to re-read Anna Karenina soon, the author has a very high opinion about the Russian titan.



Recommended to readers who like to self-examine their reading habits and their literary interests.
