David Lee Roth was one of the heroes of my teenage years. I can still remember hearing Van Halen's1984for the first time and then, only a few months later, catching the video for Diamond Dave's version of "California Girls". I distinctly recall staring at the television and wondering, "Hang on, is this the same bloke from that wonderfully daftHot for Teachervideo?"You have to remember, this was long before the internet. The music press wasn't exactly bending over backwards to cover hard rock and heavy metal... and wouldn't really do so for another year or two... and I was about fourteen, so connecting the dots wasn't exactly "straightforward".Before long, I became a devoted fan of Roth's solo career. "Eat 'Em and Smile" was a cracking record; my only complaint was that it felt criminally short at just over half an hour. "Skyscraper" was excellent as well, and the follow-up, "A Little Ain't Enough", despite being something of a commercial flop, remains an underrated gem... to me, that is. It was with "Your Filthy Little Mouth" that my enthusiasm for Dave's musical output began to wane.That said, whenever I encountered Roth in interviews, videos or magazine features, he never struck me as a particularly profound character. So when I picked up this book, I wasn't expecting much beyond a collection of outrageous tales from the rock-star circus. To be fair, there are plenty of those, and they're often enormously entertaining. What genuinely surprised me, however, was discovering a side of Roth I hadn't anticipated. Beneath all the swagger, wisecracks and larger-than-life showmanship, there appears to be a more thoughtful and reflective individual. I particularly enjoyed reading about his connection to his Jewish heritage, the pride he takes in it, and his passionate defense of Israel and its history.Another highlight was the vivid portrait of Los Angeles during the 1960s and 1970s. Roth paints a fascinating picture of a city that served as a cultural launch pad for so much of what followed, particularly in music. You can almost see the foundations of entire scenes being poured in real time.Overall, I'd give the book four stars. It's not really a conventional autobiography; Roth jumps from one period to another, from one subject to the next, with little concern for chronology or structure. At times it feels less like a memoir and more like spending an evening trapped in a booth with an endlessly fascinating raconteur who's had far too much coffee. But even when it wanders off down some unexpected side street, it's rarely dull. Above all, I was entertained.PS:David Lee Roth is still out there performing these days. In fact, if you're curious, there are plenty of videos from his 2026 shows floating around Youtube. That said, if you still cherish fond memories of theglory days, you might be wise to give them a miss. Let's just put it this way: watching them is rather like bumping into an old schoolmate you remember as a dashing rogue, only to discover he's now shouting at pigeons outside a betting shop. Some memories are best left untouched, right? At this stage, one can't help thinking that perhaps it would be wiser for Dave to take a graceful bow, thank the audience, and leave the stage with what remains of his legend intact.
