This is the 2005 updated version. Phenomenal! The majority of the pages of this book have either an ah-ha moment or an eye-opening epiphany that forever restructured my understanding of war. No other book has helped me to understand the political and physical realities that cause or limit war as much as this one.Some insights:In World War 2, inquiries made by the U.S. military found that only an average of 15% of frontline men would fire their weapons at an enemy during an engagement, even if their position was in danger of being overrun. When soldiers learned this, they were astonished because they thought that they were alone in their dereliction of duty. Based on findings from the Civil War, things were not different then. By the time Vietnam came around, the U.S. had found a way to condition our men to kill, so the number rose to above 80%. For this reason a first-world power will defeat a third-world power in open battle even without technological superiority and even when outnumbered.The ideas of the noble savage and the one-with-nature eco-conscious native are debunked pretty thoroughly.Since the early 1900's conventional war has rarely had any obvious winners, and even when, say, a large, technologically superior nation wins an unequivocal military victory against a smaller one (e.g., the Iraq war), the overall benefit and political gain has been dubious at best. This is significant when we realize that war is politics by another means.As horrific as WW1 was, comparatively very few civilians were killed. One reason was because of the unmoving front. WW2 killed at least twice as many soldiers as WW1, but also almost twice as many civilians as soldiers. This was because the front was more dynamic. Towns with no importance would be wiped off the map simply because the front moved back and forth across them too many times. This story has repeated itself since WW2 when regular armies in continuous fronts have fought their way through heavily populated territory (e.g., Korea).Modern technology has made military equipment so prohibitively difficult and expensive to build, and so lethally precise, that if a conventional WW3 did break out, so many first-power planes, vessels, and land vehicles would likely be destroyed in the first year that the war would regress to simpler munitions because we cannot produce nearly as many, say, airplanes per month as we could in WW2. The reason smaller conflicts can last longer is because the warring nations can be supplied by others; in a first-power world war, that would not be true.
