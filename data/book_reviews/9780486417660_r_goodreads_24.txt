The firstOzstory by Ruth Plumly Thompson! She was apparently a longtime fan before getting this job, and that tracks, because honestly? This book feels a bit fanficcy. The characters are kind of Flanderized, and while Baum obviously introduced new ones in each book, here there's this subtle feeling of "here's my OC, who instantly becomes best friends with the protagonists. Do not steal!" (She did, apparently, prefer later writers not to use her characters.)A few weird things that Thompson does that kind of tie into this: she keeps having the characters talk in little songs or poems (Scraps pretty much exclusively talks like that, when it was a minor trait that she had only in her first book), and there's this weird running gag where the Scarecrow is so light that like, people bowing to him blows him across the room. I'm sorry, is he a scarecrow or a small piece of lint?The Tin Woodman is absent from most of the story. Which is weird, right? Scarecrow disappears after an emotional moment, and his best friend is totally uninterested in the quest to find him. I get the feeling that Thompson just wanted to write about the Cowardly Lion more, but it feels OOC.On the other hand, fanfic tends to be character-driven, and this story actually seems to be trying to do for the Scarecrow like whatTin Woodman of Ozdid with Nick. Baum never explained why the Scarecrow is alive, and Thompson not only provides an explanation, but, via a sort of reincarnation story, provides a whole forgotten backstory for him to grapple with. It's potentially an interesting idea, but unfortunately, the execution only touches on the implications of the whole setup.Because let's just cut to the chase here: this story is pretty racist. See, it turns out that the Scarecrow's spirit used to belong to the Emperor of Fantasy China, and Thompson's depiction of Fantasy "Chinamen" is...well, the word "Chinamen" is kinda indicative. And it impacts the main plot, because while you could have had a poignant story about Scarecrow being torn between the life that he's rediscovered versus the one he has in Oz, instead if comes off as him wanting to return to the utopia of pseudo-Euro-American Oz because he's disturbed by this scary foreign place where people are overly formal and eat cats.The best thing I can say is that I've seen worse—for example, everyone's L's and R's are actually in the right place! And sometimes Thompson actually tries to make the Silver Island (or Islands, it's very inconsistent) seem appealing. But even then, it flips back and forth in that Orientalist sort of way, where we're supposed to enjoy how "exotic" the country is while also not liking most of the people there. China would be great if only there weren't so many Chinese people around, amirite?The plot also gets less sensical as we approach the end. Like, the Scarecrow is the Emperor, but apparently doesn't get consulted about whether or not he's going to have a diplomatic marriage or be magically transformed, and he acts like just saying "I'm the Emperor and NO I'M NOT" isn't an option, until it suddenly is. And then when he tries to abdicate, his evil son who wants the throne for himself...doesn't want him to? For some reason? Like, why are you still going with your "turn him back into an old man" plot, dude, you realize HIM HANDING YOU THE THRONE will get you the throne quicker, right?Despite it's flaws, the Scarecrow's story is at least focused; it's Dorothy and the Lion who stumble around episodic subplots, though it's not as annoying as in other books. And Thompson actually avoids anydei ex machina(we even get scenes where Ozma and Glinda specifically DON'T see the plot happening in the Magic Picture/Book of Records, which would be pointless additions if they're not meant as little winks at the audience), while the magic fan and parasol are pretty good plot devices.The main new character, Sir Hokus, is a knight, and...I dunno, does that seem out of place to anyone? I don't know why, since we have so many other fairytale tropes, but "misplaced knight of the Round Table" seems off to me. There's also the Comfortable Camel and the Doubtful Dromedary, who add nothing to the story but mild irritation.So overall, it's a pretty good idea with some weak execution. Thompson is clearly trying to honor and emulate Baum's style, but is different enough that reading her work is automatically interesting just as a change of pace. I look forward to seeing if her future entries get better or worse.
