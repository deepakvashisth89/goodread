There is a reason why, among the two treatises included in this volume, theSecond Treatiseis the one that is most referenced. TheFirst Treatisehas a purpose, which is to set the argumentative basis from which theSecond Treatisecould be developed. Locke had to show, in a devastating rebuttal of Robert Filmer’sPatriarchathat the biblical basis for the divine rights of kings was wrongheaded. Locke shows how basing rational arguments for the rights of kings and the conventions of primogeniture on divine mandates and on the paternal lineage from Adam is not only wildly inconsistent but frequently illogical and contradictory. In my eyes, Locke swiftly made the point to my satisfaction, but he does go on, presumably, to close as many avenues for Bible-based rebuttals as possible. It’s a bit tedious.TheSecond Treatiseis where things really get going. I won’t try to summarize, but I want to hit some things that stood out. One is that Locke, like Hobbes, starts from a consideration of the “State of Nature” in which all men (charitably … as a generic collective noun) have, more or less equal ability to pursue their self-interests. And like Hobbes, in pursuing those interests, we rely on any advantages to assert our interest over those of our fellow human beings, putting us into a “State of War.” Whereas Hobbes sees the solution to residing in a “state of nature” in naming a Sovereign who exercises absolute power and stands outside the reach of a civil society organized by laws, Locke sees the solution in the creation of representative government that may be focused on an individual as an executor, but that is fundamentally built on the interests of individuals who all have a stake in the commonwealth. For Hobbes, people follow the sovereign out of fear. For Locke, citizens follow a government of the people in order to protect their interests in the form of property.In theFirst TreatiseLocke undermines the argument that God provided Adam with “dominion” over the world and that this dominion is the basis of kingly rule. He replaces that reading with one in which God provided Adam withuseof the world and all the plants and beasts within it, as a space of the Common Good. It is out of the Common Good that property is made. “God, who hath given the World to Men in common, hath also given them reason to make use of it to the best advantage of Life, and convenience. The Earth, and all that is therein, is given to Men for the Support and Comfort of their being […] and nobody has originally a private Dominion, exclusive of the rest of Mankind” (286). Property is what emerges from the our skilled use of resources found in the Common: “[w]hatsoever then he removes out of the State that Nature hath provided, and left it in, he hath mixed his Labor with, and joyned to it something that is his own, and thereby makes it his property” (288). Property arises from our skilled use of natural resources that are held in common and that everyone has the ability to transform through their own labor into their own property (289). But property rights only extend to what one can make adequate personal use of (290). Political power is the creation and enforcement of laws for the preservation of the common good (268) and the government that serves this purpose is the focus of theSecond Treatise.I love the principles of theSecond Treatisebut it raises all kinds of questions for me. For example, what counts as property? I think Locke had in mind things that we literally make with our hands, but can that extend to the products of intellectual labor? Can it extend to things like our personal data, things that we create passively with the movements of our bodies in space? Also, what does it mean to have access to the Common Good and what resources are to be considered part of the Common? It seems like we would need to define that in order to understand the scope of the law that is meant to protect that access. If we are talking about access to hunting grounds or arable lands in parcels that can be managed by a person or a family, the scope of protective law might be easier to define. But when the common includes information or access to infrastructure allowing us to make use of resources, or when it references our capacity or means to access things like education and credentials then the protective scope of the law widens still. I prefer to think of property as value and access as means, and it seems worthwhile to consider how the laws support the creation of value and secure our means of creating it, not to the exclusion of others’ abilities to secure the same but merely to secure our own. Locke also thought about the way that money creates problems for a system of value based on labor (301), but he doesn’t really resolve that satisfactorily. Maybe he is understanding the function of money as Aristotle did in his treatment of justice. Money is an equalizer for the unequal application of natural justice.The latter half of theSecond Treatisedescribes a system of political society that people enter into to preserve their property-based stakes in this world. Political Society as an organization of individuals has “in itself the power to preserve the property, and in order thereunto punish the offenses of all those of that society; there, and there only is the political society, where everyone of the members hath quitted this nature power, resign’d it up into the hands of the community in all cases that exclude him not from appealing for protection to the law established by it” (324). In the political society, the majority rules, and the majority is represented by the Legislative branch of elected officials. Interestingly, Locke thought of the Legislative as the “Supream Power” (366) precisely because it serves the majority by passing laws that protect individual property rights and their access to the common good. When no new laws were needed (!!) the Legislative would disband, essentially making it an ad hoc assemblage. The Legislative ought always to reflect the majority of the population and it should be disbanded and reformed through election (or revolution) when it does not. Locke also envisions an Executive branch that enforces laws and a Federative branch that makes treaties and enlarges the community. The Judiciary was not seen as a separate, independent branch that figured out the vagaries of the law — that work was delegated, distributively to magistrates. All in all, a fascinating discussion of a system that is self-correcting, power-balanced, and self-checking. It also a system that must adhere to the will of the majority, lest people have a right to dissolve government, sometimes abruptly and violently. It is system that does not tolerate despots.Upon finishing theSecond Treatise, it was clear to me why this text was so inspirational to the founders of American democracy. And although our motives are different, I have to agree with my state legislators who push to make sure that texts like this are read and studied. I think it is an important task for admirers of democracies to read this text and understand what Locke was advocating to build and preserve. The motivations for and conditions under which Locke wrote these treatises in the 1680s might have changed, but there is no doubt that issues related to the representation of individual interests in the operation of our system of government remain absolutely pertinent today.
