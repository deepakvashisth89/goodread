At first glance, this is the story of three brothers (Brady, Warren, Boyd) and how they react to their erstwhile father returning to their lives but, in reality, it’s a book which predominantly follows the grandson Fleming (Boyd’s son), as he navigates these three curious influences. His own father is absent in many ways but mostly because he is fixated on the pursuit of his wife who left him for another man. Warren, meanwhile, is a drunk womaniser who turns up on Fleming’s doorstep at the most inopportune moments, while Brady is probably the most stable of the three which is a bizarre statement given that he believes he has second sight and can put curses on people (which he does, not just for himself but also for a young man named Albright who owes another man money). But anyway...

This book has that very American southern gothic quality which, in truth, I’ve never really liked but I have to say that part one did contain some of the most gorgeous prose I’ve read in a very long time. It is full of colour and vibracy, rich with inventive descriptions which demonstrate exquisite language that is often sincerely mesmerising. Some of it was truly magnificent. I did however feel that part two, three, and four focused a little more on dialogue and pushing the plot along whereas part one was where Gay introduced his characters and their world, and where, as a result, he was allowed to let his writing flow, the narration becoming almost deliciously indulgent (in a good way). He tones that down (I felt) for the following sections and prioritises the plot points. That being said, even then you would still find similar moments of wonderful writing.



Despite the quality of prose (and despite it becoming more plot driven in the later sections), my interest did start to wane somewhat as it went along, and I would have preferred if the book had been more focused on narration and language all the way through, rather than character and story. It jumps around a little too much for my tastes and introduces a few characters that aren’t adding much. When Gay lets things settle, when he simply describes the environment, the weather, the settings, it becomes far more impressive, often beautiful.

Fleming stands out as the most sympathetic character, his desire to be a writer a small (presumably biographical) element of his aspirations. Boyd eventually does track down his unfaithful wife (with unpleasant consequences), and Brady does indeed put a hex on an innocent man. The whole thing is very Southern gothic but not in that unbearable way that I generally dislike. This was more thoughtful and less ''. I thought it was a superb novel, but I so badly wanted more of what we had in part one. It seemed that Gay slightly ran out of steam and wanted to priorities the drama and the individuals causing it. Still very good though.
