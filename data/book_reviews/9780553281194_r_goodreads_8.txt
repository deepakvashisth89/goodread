Still the high water mark of special forces novels.The Five Fingershas a level of verisimilitude unmatched by any other similar work of fiction--or arguably even non-fiction--and for more than 35 years I (and many others) have wondered if this was a real mission disguised as a novel. The question is still unanswered.A group of seven special operations soldiers are chosen to embark on a top-secret mission to assassinate eleven high-ranking North Vietnamese and Chinese officials, including General Vo Nguyen Giap, commanding officer of the North Vietnamese Army.The unit outfits, selects weapons, and trains for the mission. The mission then begins as the unit is dropped into Laos. They march for weeks through eastern Laos and into North Vietnam, leaving a very bloody trail of engagements in their wake. Their march continues into China, where . . . let's just say things don't go as planned. Their circuitous exit from China and North Vietnam is even bloodier and more terrifying than the route in, culminating in a nightmarish conclusion.In the back of the book, the author bio tantalizingly describes Gayle Rivers (a pseudonym) as the team's second-in-command--seemingly confirming the theory that it's non-fiction. Much debate has taken place over the years as to Rivers' actual combat involvement, both here and in his subsequent books. Maybe this is an account of an actual event in which Rivers has made embellishments to enhance or disguise the reality. One thing that jumps out of this novel is that Rivers knows his stuff--it would be very difficult for a layman to invent the kinds of things that are detailed here and do it credibly. Whether Rivers was one of the primaries on an actual mission, we may never truly know, but the book still stands for itself as unsurpassed in the genre.
