Thank you Debbie Zapata for this trip down memory lane. Our reading excursions always have plenty of side quests, off topic discussions, unexpected turns, broken bones, elaborate twists, a twitter-patted hiatus (specific to this one), job interferences, absurd tangents, and boisterous laughter – yes, I can hear you from eleven states away.



This book has an abundance of negative reviews, which narrowly focus on vernacular and Native American stereotypes. Some consider the content “demeaning” to indigenous people.

was written in 1980: Native Americans were called Indians. This book does not degrade Native Americans, it empowers them. Little Bear is a brave, intelligent, highly skilled individual, mastering carpentry, hunting, and survival skills. He quickly processes a problem and takes logical action, at least, logic that would apply to an 18th century Indian.

People are entitled to their opinions, but some opinions are poorly thought out. Additionally, Little Bear has no problem bossing giants around while he screams: “Make magic! Now!” Giants who could easily squash him with a hand palm to the table. This is not stupidity. Little Bear will challenge a titian, even if the outcome is death, if it proves he’s an Indian with fortitude.


My fourth grade teacher, Mrs. October, who sported a mole to the left of her bottom lip, read to the class. Students rolled their eyes, yawned, uttered distaste, looked out the window, and contracted narcolepsy. Girls would pass notes to girlfriends, who’d pass notes to "acquaintances," who’d roll notes into balls to throw at a boy sitting in the delinquent corner.

These were vain attempts to distract Mrs. O from reading, because once she projected her voice the class went silent: ears were immediately addicted.

She’d read one chapter a day, close the book and put it on her lap. The class pleaded: “A little more.”

Mrs. O smiled, went to her desk, locked the book in her drawer and said, “No.”

On the day she closed the book after the last chapter she said, “That ends Omri and Little Bear’s story.”

We looked down at our desks, dejected.

After a long pause she uttered, “Does it?”

“To be read,” she said, “next year.”
