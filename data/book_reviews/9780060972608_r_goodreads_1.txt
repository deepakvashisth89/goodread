Berman’s biography of Schweitzer is an entertaining read. Life in rural Gabon in 1960 was farther away in time and space than the numbers would suggest: pet owls and deer, spoiled bushmeat, an amoral expat arms dealer (OK, not so unfamiliar), local healers who range from deceitful misanthropes to the intuitive, inintelligible and ultimately heart-breaking Mama Sans Nom. Berman describes patients’ suffering from diseases both preventable and curable, the care provided to the sick by relatives and local communities, and his and his wife’s own evolving emotional responses. We come to understand why Schweitzer ran the hospital as he did, and perhaps to agree with Berman that under the circumstances and in its historical context, Schweitzer’s choices were, if sometimes imperfect, also fundamentally moral, kind, and effective. This isn’t fine arts literature or groundbreaking historical insight, but it is well-written.The book is much more than a fine travelogue, however. Schweitzer’s hospital reflected his belief in the redemptive power of committed work, especially work with one’s hands. The description of Schweitzer’s jungle duet is ethereal. Ultimately, Berman is most interested in Schweitzer as a philosopher and theologian. Berman, apparently a quiet atheist, is an ideal presenter because he is sceptical and probing but without an alternate agenda. We discover Schweitzer mainly through their wide-ranging and mostly-gentle dialogues, which Berman places lightly in the context of details of Schweitzer’s life that he read in advance. The frontispiece declares “Schweitzer’s ‘Reverence for Life” is epitomized in his paraphrased sentence: ‘A farmer working to earn his daily bread may cut down a million stalks of grain, but on his way home he should consciously crush not a single flower.’” Now decades after the 1960s and all that has followed, the approach seems almost ordinary. Ordinary, that is, in the way of the apocryphal adult who summed up having recently read Shakespeare for the first time as ‘mostly a bunch of platitudes.’James Brabazon, another of Schweitzer’s biographers, spent half a decade looking for Schweitzer’s feet of clay. Like Brabazon, Berman's initial scepticism led instead to profound admiration. (In light of Schweitzer’s early and deeply influential advocacy of a nuclear testing ban, Brabazon even wonders if the CIA initiated some of the efforts to discredit Schweitzer.) Berman is convincing in presenting Schweitzer as a powerful role model for almost anyone. Whether or not one wants the role model, Schweitzer is a fine acquaintance to discover. That Schweitzer should be at most vaguely familiar to many people now is a shame. Don’t miss this book!
