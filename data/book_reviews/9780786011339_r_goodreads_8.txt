I finished this a few nights ago It was actually a "re-read" for me. Originally I bought and read it a little bit after it was published (probably around 2003 or 2004- I would have been 16 or 17 then.) I lent out my copy to someone at some point and never got it back. Buuuut I found it on Thriftbooks.It did get quite a bit of flack; it was just hit and miss with some people. 📚I would also like to point out this was heavily covered in the national media at the time Kendall Francois was arrested, and it's been 28 years since then. So there's not really any "spoilers" here. I hope to encourage other people to read it.On some level I can understand why other readers felt the way they did- Fred Rosen's literary style isn't as developed as say, Michelle McNamara or John Douglas.In the book, there was also alot of descriptions of roads and how they intersect and all of this- I understand that Rosen was trying to give a lay of the land, especially the fact that the victims were prostitutes and he was trying to explain their day to day life. And I also think to a degree he was trying to match what we see on Dateline- people do alot of driving around on true crime shows. 📺But as a reader, we don't have those visuals. I'm not from New York, or Poughkeepsie. I live in a rural world totally different from that urban life so what road leads to what didn't actually compute to me. 🚗I also strongly disagree with Rosen's opinion on FBI profiling and the tasks of the Behavioral Science Unit. The profile the FBI gave to local detectives (who we know the perpetrator is Kendall Francois) was actually accurate with the exception of Francois' race- everything else was right about him generally. From John Douglas' book, violent crime is alot deeper than organized vs. disorganized and signature/MO. I feel like Rosen just scratched the surface on that. To say profiling is "police voodoo" was a straw man argument in my opinion. Just didn't feel it. 💁‍♀️With those fallacies in mind, his writing was enough to keep me engrossed in the events that were unfolding. I do appreciate the amount of research and first hand interviews Rosen was able to put together from the perspectives of the detectives and survivors. (Francois as well as his family declined to answer any questions as far as the book.) Rosen also pulled court documents and other sources. We have to consider this research was at the advent of the internet, and ChatGPT and modern ways of researching didn't exist then. 💻I'm glad I re-read it because there are things that I understand on a deeper level than I did when I first read it.For example: how in the f*ck did Kendall Francois' family live in that house like that? How was the smell not attaching to them? Alot of bad smells attach to clothes. How were people that knew the Francois family not picking up on the odors? Like coworkers that worked with say, his mom working at the freaking psychiatric unit- didn't freaking notice that? No one ever saw anything, or had any clue??? The odor was noticed in the house by the family, and Francois told his family it was a family of raccoons in the attic, and that was just the end of it. That's all the answers we have on that.❓️I don't see how Francois' father didn't know, either. He was a Vietnam veteran and honestly, from conversations with my dad, Francois' father had to know that was human and not a family of raccoons. Idk, I guess people don't always investigate a little further things they really don't want to know.The book also uncovered some of the dehumanization that occurred in the media regarding the victims- by the media and the community. I do think from the detectives' perspectives, they did want to catch Francois. They literally had no information, and some time had passed before anyone even reported some of the victims missing. Diane Franco was the literal saving grace. 🙏I also did my own research afterward. It's also interesting how little information there is about this particular case outside of Rosen's book. It's one of the few sources listed on Wikipedia. Fred Rosen died in 2020 and his website has been taken down. Kendall Francois died in prison of AIDS in 2014 (justice in itself if you ask me.) Idk I guess I'm waiting for a "tell all" book from the little sis. 👀I also looked up the Francois property, and surprisingly it's been restored. Honestly I thought it should have been demolished. (It was so bad, the forensic team had to wear HAZMAT suits to collect evidence wtf.) Now, it looks like any other house, and that's just psychologically perplexing to me. 🏠The Poughkeepsie Murders also changed how capital punishment is dealt with. Just prior to Francois' arrest, New York brought back the death penalty under Governor Pataki. The law there is the prosecution has 120 days to file a motion to seek capital punishment. In Francois' case, his attorney was trying to enter a guilty plea before the District Attorney could file, and the New York Supreme Court was like, "No, my dude. We're not starting a bunch of racing to the courthouse." (Although, with alot more legalese than I just said.) ⚖️Just mind blown that anything like that could occur to begin with. 🤯#fredrosen #truecrime #truecrimestories #books #booktoker #booktok #booksworthreading #readinglist2026 #readingchallenge
